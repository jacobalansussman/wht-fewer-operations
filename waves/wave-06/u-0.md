## 0. In four sentences

The two-chain program for subset sums does NOT give linear size, and the reason has a name. Measured least corner counts
are 5, 13, 30, 80 at 4, 8, 16, 32 points (every restart of a fast annealer lands on exactly these), that is 1.25, 1.625,
1.875, 2.5 corners per point, and the best found at 64 and 128 points is in section 1. A short packing argument (section 3,
[derived], not reviewed) shows that corners per point is unbounded: it grows at least like 2^(c k / log k) with k = log2 N,
and from about k = 192 the two-chain program is provably worse than Yates. The argument uses three hypotheses, and they are
the to-do list (section 4): one linear order on the inputs, the plain picture, and every output being a neighbour of only two
others.

## 2. What the best pictures look like, and which steps are expensive [ran]

- In every best picture at 8, 16, 32, 64 points ALL output steps join two outputs one inside the other, and nearly all change
  ONE bit (13 of 15, 29 of 31, 61 of 63): the output order is a Gray code (a walk through all subsets changing one element at
  a time), with one jump to the empty set and one to the full set at the end. The same holds for the inputs (at 64 points 62 of 63
input steps are nested, at 128 points 126 of 127 output steps).
- A one-bit step from S to S + e changes exactly the inputs T with e in T and T inside S + e: a sub-cube with 2^|S| members.
  Its cost is twice the number of runs that sub-cube makes in the input order (one less if it starts the order).
- **The expensive steps are the ones high in the lattice.** Average non-zeros per step by the size of the larger output set:

| points | size 2 | size 3 | size 4 | size 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 16 | 2.0 | 2.14 | | |
| 32 | 1.89 | 2.8 | 3.1 | |
| 64 | 2.5 | 3.19 | 4.53 | 5.18 |
| 128 (short run) | 2.31 | 3.86 | 5.63 | 7.15 (size 6: 9.93) |

- **The rhythm 2, 2, 4, 4 explained.** A Gray walk goes up and down between neighbouring levels. While it moves among sets of
  size 2 and below, every step's sub-cube (2 or 4 inputs) is one run: cost 2. When it moves among sets of size 3 and 4 the
  sub-cube (8 or 16 inputs) is two runs: cost 4. The walk alternates between a low stretch and a high stretch, hence 2, 2, 4, 4.
  At 64 points the high stretches cost 6 and 8. Section 3 says why a big sub-cube cannot stay in one piece.
- Entries of M are all +1 or -1 in every best picture (40 and 40 at 32 points).

## 3. The wall: a packing lemma [derived tonight, checked on the found pictures, not reviewed]

Words. For an output S write "below S" for the set of inputs T inside S (2^|S| of them). A *block* is a set of inputs that
are consecutive in the input order. The *cost* of an output step is the number of non-zeros of its row of M.

**Block lemma.** Take any plain picture and a step between neighbouring outputs S and S' with S not inside S' (one of the two
has this property; for the last output, S' is nothing). If the step costs at most c, then there is a block of at least
2^(|S| - 1) / c consecutive inputs ALL of which are inside S.
Proof. The difference of the two rows is +1 exactly on the inputs inside S and not inside S'. There are at least 2^(|S|-1)
of them (all T inside S that contain one fixed element of S outside S'). Each maximal run of +1 ends at a place where the
difference changes, so there are at most c runs, and the longest has at least 2^(|S|-1)/c members; all are inside S. QED.

**Packing.** Two outputs S1, S2 of the same size j have only 2^|S1 n S2| inputs in common below them. Fix t and call a family
of j-sets *spread* if any two of its members share at most j - t elements. Give every member a block of exactly L inputs below
it, with L > 2^(j-t). Two such blocks overlap in at most 2^(j-t) places, so their starting points differ by at least
L - 2^(j-t), and a spread family has at most P = (N - L)/(L - 2^(j-t)) + 1 members. Any family of j-sets contains a spread
sub-family such that every member is within t - 1 exchanges of it, so
   (number of outputs of size j owning a block of length L) <= P * V,   V = sum over i < t of C(j,i) C(k-j,i).
**Count.** Every output lies in at most two steps. So (steps of cost at most c) <= 2 * sum over j of min(C(k,j), P V) with
L = 2^(j-1)/c, and corners >= (c + 1) * (N - that number) for every c.

**What it gives** [ran, `wall.py`, whole numbers; the best c is chosen by the program]:

| k = log2 N | 16 | 32 | 48 | 64 | 96 | 128 | 192 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| corners per point at least | 1 | 1 | 1.81 | 2.64 | 6.53 | 15.1 | 109.8 |
| Yates, gates per point (k/2) | 8 | 16 | 24 | 32 | 48 | 64 | 96 |

Asymptotically [derived]: if corners <= C N then half of the steps cost at most 2C, and the middle levels (j near k/2, where
almost all outputs live) allow only about 2^(k/2) * k^(2 log2 C + O(1)) such outputs against 2^k / sqrt(k) needed. So
log2 C >= about k / (5 log2 k): **corners per point >= 2^(c k / log k), more than any power of log N.** The two-chain
program for subset sums is therefore not linear, not N log N, and from about N = 2^192 on it is provably worse than Yates.
The same holds with inputs and outputs exchanged (complement all addresses).

**Checks** [ran, `lemmacheck.py`]: on the 16-point and 32-point best pictures every step satisfies the block lemma (0
violations), and for every c the number of steps of cost at most c is at most twice the number of outputs owning a block of
the promised length (at 32 points: c = 2: 21 steps, 32 outputs; c = 4: 31 steps, 32 outputs). The bound itself says nothing
below k = 48: at the sizes one can search, the lemma shows the CAUSE (section 2's table) and not a number.

**Honest limits.** (i) Proved for plain pictures. With signs on inputs and outputs a run of constant value may mix inputs
below S and below S'; I expect the same result with S union S' in place of S but have not written it [guess]. (ii) The
constants are loose; the true count surely leaves N log N much earlier than k = 192: the measured per-point values 1.25,
1.625, 1.875, 2.5 and at most 3.73 already bend upwards. (iii) It is a statement about this model only: it is no lower
bound for subset-sum circuits.

## 4. What the wall is made of, and three ways around it ("yes, and")

The proof uses exactly three things. Each is a hatch.

1. **The input nodes are runs of ONE order.** Blocks of one length that overlap little must start far apart: that is the
   packing. With a general merge tree on the inputs (the "letter" of k-cross) a step is a signed sum of c tree nodes, and the
   set where the sum is +1 is a node MINUS smaller nodes; such pieces do not pack (a node minus two children can lie below S1
   and the same node minus two other children below S2). So the lemma does not cover: chain on the outputs, TREE on the
   inputs. Smallest experiment: at 16 and 32 points, fix a balanced tree on the inputs in a Gray order, count for each output
   step the least number of tree nodes whose signed sum is the row difference (a small dynamic programme on the tree), and
   solve the output walk; compare with 30 and 80. The lemma predicts where a gain must come from: the middle levels.
2. **Every output is a neighbour of only two others.** In the walk, an output of size about k/2 has to be reached by a cheap
   step from one of two neighbours. If outputs hang on a TREE instead (each output = an earlier wire plus or minus one block,
   any earlier wire), an output still needs one cheap edge, so the lemma survives [derived: the count "at most two steps"
   becomes "at least one cheap edge per output" and the same packing applies]. What breaks it is HELPER wires: values that are
   not outputs, with input sets that are not "everything below S". An output of the middle level reached from a helper that
   already holds most of it needs no long block inside S. The cheapest helpers to try are sums over an interval of the lattice,
   {T : A inside T inside S}: the row difference of a one-bit step is exactly such a sub-cube.
3. **Blocks must lie INSIDE S.** That is the plain picture and Z itself. For the matrix D Z or for another member of the class
   the +1 region is different; q-cross found the transform's own picture quadratic, so I do not expect a gain here.

Two smaller facts worth keeping:
- **In any order of the 2^k subsets, at most 2N / 2^j sub-cubes of dimension j are blocks** [derived: two blocks that are
  sub-cubes of the same size meet in a sub-cube, so in at most half]. A Gray walk needs about C(k, j) one-bit steps with a
  sub-cube of dimension j. So one-run steps are at most a vanishing share of all steps: about N^0.78 of N. This also answers
  brief 3's question for large k: a picture in which every step is a one-run step does not exist beyond small k [derived];
  the formula corners = 2N - d is a small-size phenomenon.
- The best 16-point picture is self-mirror (section 1). If that is a law, the search space at 32 and 64 points halves: anneal
  one order and take the other as its mirror.

## 5. Growth law

corners(N) / N = 1.25, 1.625, 1.875, 2.5, then at most 3.73 (64 points) and at most 5.59 (128 points), the last two from
single unconverged runs. Not bounded [derived, section 3:
it tends to infinity faster than any power of log N]. At the sizes searched it is already above (1/2) log2 N + constant with a
constant that grows: per point minus k/2 is 0.25, 0.125, -0.125, 0, at most 0.73. So of the brief's three outcomes none:
the law is WORSE than Yates', eventually by more than any polylogarithmic factor.
