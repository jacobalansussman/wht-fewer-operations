# u-3 zletter: with a free tree on each side, subset sums cost 4, 8, 20, 52 rectangles, and the gate count lands on Yates' number (4, 12, 32) and then one above it (81)

11 October 2026, 01:36 to about 02:35 EDT, 60-minute box. Private. One agent; nothing reviewed, nothing in Lean, nothing published.
Marks: [ran] = exact run of mine tonight (files in `u-3-zletter/`); [ran, mod 3] / [ran, mod 5] = exact, but modulo that prime;
[ran, annealer] = a search, so an UPPER bound, realised with whole numbers and replayed exactly on all N basis vectors;
[derived] = by hand tonight, checked by nobody; [read] = from tonight's reports; [guess].
Gate count everywhere: **(G)**, a gate is any a*u + b*v, scalings free. Count (O), OpenAI's, is not used.

**No circuit below Yates' count (12, 32, 80, 192), and no growth law below (1/2) N log2 N. Nothing here changes the order.**

Words. N = 2^k points; an *address* is k bits, read as a subset of {1..k}; `e` is the empty set. *Subset sums*: y_S = the sum of x_T
over the T inside S; its matrix Z has a 1 where T is inside S. D(S) = the set of all T inside S (a *down-set*); row S of Z is the
indicator of D(S). *Yates*: the usual method, (N/2) log2 N gates (4, 12, 32, 80, 192 at 4 to 64 points). *Merge tree*: start from
the N inputs and repeatedly replace two held sums by their sum (N - 1 gates); its *nodes* are the sets of inputs under each sum; a
*chain* is the most unbalanced tree. *Rectangle*: (a set of outputs) x (a set of inputs) with one number. *Letter*: Z written as a
sum of tau rectangles (node of an output tree) x (node of an input tree).
*The network picture* [read: p-8 section 1; it carries over word for word]: fix the input tree. Then tau = the number of steps of the
shortest network that joins 0 and the N rows of Z, a *step* being "add a number times the indicator of one node". The output tree is
the shape of that network and need not be chosen. A *junction point* is a point of the network that is not a row.
*Spanning model*: the same with no junction points and whole numbers: every row is 0 or an earlier row plus a few nodes.
*Gates of a letter*: N - 1 for the input tree, plus one per step, except that a first step from 0 is free (it is a node itself).

## 1. Tools, and their checks on known answers

- `dwz.c` [ran]: p-8's `dw.c` with one line changed, the target rows are the rows of Z (entry 1 where x is inside y) instead of
  the rows of H. Exact shortest network over GF(P) by the Dreyfus-Wagner dynamic programme on the P^N vectors; rows may be scaled
  (the target of row S is the line {m z_S}), steps are m times a node, the input scaling is the last N numbers of the input line.
  Check: at 4 points the two-chain picture has 5 corners (q-cross) and I get 4 for all 15 labelled trees modulo 3, 5 and 7; a
  4-rectangle letter at 4 points by hand: {y_12} x everything, {y_1, y_e, y_2} x {e,1}, -{y_e, y_2} x {1}, {y_2} x {2}.
- `dwzg.c` [ran]: the same in *gate mode* (third argument `g`): 0 and every multiple of a node are one point, so a first step from
  0 costs nothing; it prints the steps that cost a gate, and gates = N - 1 + that. Check: the hand design of line 2 gives 6, so 13
  gates, which is what its exact replay counts.
- `trees_z.py` [ran]: all merge trees on 2^k labelled inputs up to permutations of the k address bits (the only symmetries of Z
  that keep the model: 23,100 classes at 8 points, 9 at 4 points). `trees8z.txt` has one line per class: orbit size, 7 node masks.
- `zmst.c` [ran]: the spanning model for any N up to 64, whole numbers, plain (all scalings 1). The distance of two rows is the
  exact least number of nodes whose signed sum is their difference (a small dynamic programme on the tree); then a minimum spanning
  tree on {0} + rows; then an annealer over input trees (move: cut a subtree off and graft it elsewhere). With `GATES=1` in the
  environment it minimises gates instead of rectangles. Every number it prints is an upper bound for that tree.
- `zreplay_tree.py` [ran]: rebuilds the design from the tree alone, writes the gate list, and replays it with whole numbers on all
  N basis vectors against Z. Every count below marked "replayed" passed it.
- Cross-check of the two models [ran]: on the first 17,602 classes of the 8-point list (the run was stopped by its time limit) the spanning count of rectangles equals the
  exact count (junction points allowed, modulo 3) every time; in gates, on all 125 best classes and on every 40th class (699
  classes): equal every time. So at 8 points a shortest network for Z never needs a junction point. For the transform it does
  (p-8: 5 junction points at 8 points). This is why I trust the spanning model one size further than I otherwise would.

## 3. The design with tau = N, and the lemma that stops it

[derived] In the plain spanning model a one-node step joins two rows only if one row's subset is inside the other's (the
difference of two 0/1 rows is plus or minus a 0/1 indicator only then), and the node is D(S) minus D(S'), S' inside S. So a letter
with tau = N is: a parent p(S) strictly inside S for every S (or "nothing", meaning the step starts at 0), such that the N sets
D(S) minus D(p(S)) are pairwise nested or disjoint.
Two rows S1, S2 of the same size m that share m - 1 elements, U = their intersection: neither set contains the other's top, so
they must be disjoint, and U lies in both down-sets, so U must be removed from one: **p(S1) = U or p(S2) = U.**
- k = 3: the three pairs must be oriented in a cycle, p(12) = 1, p(23) = 2, p(13) = 3; then p(i) = e is forced, and
  p(123) = nothing works. That is the design of line 2: 8 rectangles, 13 gates, replayed (`z8_tau8_13gates.txt`). [ran: `dwz`
  gives 8 for this tree modulo 3 and modulo 5.]
- **Level lemma.** Call a row *good* if it is one node away from another row or from 0. For each U of size m - 1, all good rows of size m
  above U but one have parent U, and a row has one parent; counting, (m - 1) x (good rows of size m) <= C(k, m-1).
  So good rows of size m number at most C(k, m-1)/(m-1): for k = 4, 4 of the 6 pairs and 3 of the 4 triples; for k = 5,
  5 of 10, 5 of 10, 3 of 5; at the middle level about a fraction 2/k.

      points                      8    16    32    64    128    1024
      rows that can be good       8    13    20    30     49     243
      so tau is at least          8    19    44    98    207   1,805       (2N - good rows)
      found                       8    20    52  (147)
      (N/2) log2 N - N + 4        8    20    52   132    324   4,100

  (Section 5 item 6(a) sharpens the first line to at most k good rows of each size; the numbers up to 64 points barely move.)
  The lemma is a linear floor, about 2N. It does not say that two nodes are enough for the others, and the search says they are
  not: at 32 points the best design has 14 rows at one node, 16 at two, 2 at three.
- Where the cost sits [ran, `bylevel.py`, `bylevel.out`]. In both rectangle-optimal designs EVERY step goes from a smaller row
  to a larger one (14 of 14 and 30 of 30; none between rows that are not nested, none from 0 except e and the full set): the
  network is a spanning tree of the subset lattice, the model of the lemma. Nodes per step by the size of the subset:

      16 points   size 2: 1,1,1,1,2,2 (the lemma allows four 1s)      size 3: 1,1,2,2 (allows three)
      32 points   size 2: five 1s, five 2s (allows five)   size 3: ten 2s (allows five 1s, the design uses none)
                  size 4: 1,1,2,3,3 (allows three 1s)      mean per size 0..5: 1, 1, 1.5, 2, 2, 1

  The lemma is tight for the pairs and not above them; the cost per row rises with the size of the row, which is what
  N log N looks like here.
- The shape of the good designs [ran, read off `z16_tau20_33gates.txt`, `z32_tau52_81gates.txt`]: the used nodes are almost all of
  the form "the sets inside S that contain i" ({2,12}, {3,23,34,234}, {4,14,24,124}, {1,13,134}), i.e. D(S) minus D(S - i), or
  such a set with one corner cut off. The tree does follow the nesting of the subsets. What cannot be continued by hand is the
  choice of ONE element i for each S so that the sets stay nested or disjoint: that is exactly what the lemma limits.

## 5. Going around (what the failure is made of, and three ways past it that I tried)

The failure: a row of size m near k/2 is far, in nodes, from every other row. The letter gives each output ONE earlier output
and nodes of ONE family of nested-or-disjoint sets.
1. **Junction points** (outputs that are not rows, to be shared by several rows). Exact at 8 points: they never help, on any
   tree compared (section 1). At 16 points I have no exact tool (3^16 vectors); the spanning model is what I ran.
2. **Numbers.** Modulo 5 (numbers 1 to 4 on steps and rows) on 16 of the 125 best trees: 12 gates on each. No gain.
3. **Signs on the inputs** (the centre of p-8): all 128 sign patterns on 14 of the best trees, modulo 3, gate mode: 12 gates for
   the plain pattern only (14 of 1,792 designs), 13 to 19 for the others. q-cross saw the same for two chains ("the best pictures
   are plain").
4. [derived, not run] **A lower bound that explains which trees lose**: for an output node A and an input node B, the rectangles
   strictly inside A x B number at least rank(Z restricted to A x B) - 2, because rectangles that contain all of A give equal rows
   and those that contain all of B give equal columns. For two balanced trees aligned with the bits, the 3^(k-2) blocks of 4 x 4
   that are copies of the 4-point matrix (rank 4) give tau >= 2 * 3^(k-2): about N^1.58. So a good letter cannot have both
   trees balanced and aligned with the bits (with a free output tree the aligned input tree is fine: 12 gates at 8 points).
   Families of blocks that share no rectangle can be summed. I did not turn this into a bound for all trees: on a chain it
   gives only N.

5. [derived, not run] **Several input trees instead of one.** Let the steps use nodes of r different input trees (r(N - 1)
   merges). The level lemma holds for each tree separately, so at most r C(k, m-1)/(m-1) rows of size m are one node away, and
   "every row one node away" needs r >= (m-1)(k-m+1)/m for every m: about k/2 trees for the middle rows alone, and
   (k/2)(N - 1) merges is Yates' count. So the lemma, read backwards, says where N log N comes from in this family of models:
   **one family of nested-or-disjoint sets serves a share of about 2/k of the middle rows at one node each**, and k/2 families
   cost what Yates costs. A letter is the other end of that trade: one family, and the middle rows pay 2, 3, ... nodes.
   Whether the trade has an interior point below both ends is the two-node version of the lemma (mutation 4).

6. [derived in the last ten minutes, a sketch, unchecked] **Two sharper counts, and they change the reading of the tie.**
   (a) In the plain spanning model write A(S) = S minus p(S), the elements a one-node step adds. Two rows of the same size are
   compatible only if A(S) misses S' or A(S') misses S; so the sets A(S) of the good rows of one size are pairwise disjoint,
   and **at most k rows of each size are one node away: at most about k^2/2 in all, not 2N/k.** (It agrees with the designs:
   five pairs at 32 points, four at 16.) The floor is 2N minus O(log^2 N).
   (b) Steps that only ADD nodes (no node subtracted; all but one step of the 32-point design are of this kind). If D(S) minus
   D(S') is a disjoint union of c nodes, one of them, B, has at least 2^(m-1)/c inputs, lies inside D(S), and the union V of
   its members has at least m - 1 - log2 c elements and lies in S. The smallest such nodes are disjoint, so there are at most
   c N / 2^(m-1) of them; above one of them the big nodes are nested and their V's take at most 2 + log2 c values of size up
   to m; one V serves at most C(k, 1 + log2 c) rows. So the rows of size m with an add-only step of at most c nodes number at
   most (c N / 2^(m-1)) (2 + log2 c) C(k, 1 + log2 c), against C(k, m) rows. At m = k/2 that is about 2^(k/2) times a polynomial
   in k against 2^k / sqrt k: **for large k almost every middle row needs more than any polynomial in k of added nodes.**
   It bites late (for c = 2, at k = 40 and not at k = 20), so no run of tonight could see it. If it is right, a letter that only
   adds is far WORSE than Yates in the limit, the tie at 8 and 16 points is a small-size fact, and the only letters with a
   future are those whose steps subtract big nodes (runs on a chain are the simplest case, and section 6 limits those too).

## 6. A by-product for the two-chain briefs: one-run steps die out

[derived; `onerun_count.py`] On a chain input tree (running sums along one order of the inputs) a *one-run step* writes the
difference of two rows as one interval of the order. In the plain picture the two rows are then S' inside S, and the interval is
D(S) minus D(S'), of length L >= 2^(m-1), m the size of S. Take two such steps with different larger ends S1, S2 of the same
size m. Their intervals meet inside D(S1 and S2), and a short count gives: the overlap is at most half of each interval.
(Let U be the common part of S1 and S2, u its size, at most m - 1; t the size of S1'; w the size of the common part of U and
S1', at least u + t - m. The overlap lies in D(U) minus D(S1'), so it is at most 2^u - 2^w <= 2^(u-m) (2^m - 2^t) = 2^(u-m) L1
<= L1/2; the same for L2.) Two intervals that overlap in at most half of each cannot contain one another, so the later one
starts at least 2^(m-2) after the earlier. So **at most 2^(k-m+2) + 1 rows of size m are the larger end of a one-run step**,
against C(k, m) rows of that size; and in a path a row is an end of at most two steps.

    points                     32    64   256   1,024   2,048   4,096   16,384   65,536
    steps                      31    63   255   1,023   2,047   4,095   16,383   65,535
    one-run steps at most      54   116   418   1,260   2,124   3,612   11,016   30,128

So "every output is its neighbour plus or minus one run" (brief 3; true at 4 and 8 points, 13 of 16 and 21 of 32 in q-cross's
search) **cannot hold from 4,096 points on**, and the share of one-run steps tends to zero. (A first version of my count forgot
that a row can be the larger end of two steps and said 512 points; the table above is the corrected one.) This kills the 2N - 2 line for corners
as an asymptotic law. It does not prove more than linear: a step with two runs is still cheap. The same count works for steps
with at most c runs only after one more idea (a step with c runs contains an interval of length L/c; the overlap bound then needs
rows whose intersection is small, a code); I sketched it and did not close it. If it closes, two chains need more than any fixed
number of runs per step for almost all rows of middle size.

## 7. What it became

**The question.** Three models now have the same sentence: optimise over a large family and land exactly on a Yates-type count.
The transform's records (m-cross: N - 1 + (N/2) log2 N), the two-chain corners (q-cross E: (N/2) log2 N + 1 at 4 and 8), and
here: one free tree per side, gates = (N/2) log2 N at 4, 8, 16, with 750 different optimal trees at 8 points and no tree below.
The letter is not Yates' circuit (Yates is not a letter: its (N/2) log2 N sums do not form one tree), so the tie is not a tautology.
**Is there a potential that every gate of a letter raises by at most one and that Z holds (N/2) log2 N of?** At 8 points the
statement is finite and true modulo 3. The 750 optimal labelled trees are listed (`out8zg_all.txt` against `trees8z.txt`).

**Mutations.**
1. Find the invariant on the 8-point data: for each of the 23,100 classes the exact count is known (12 to 16); which function of
   the tree predicts it? Candidates: the number of rows that are nodes (free outputs), the sum over nodes B of (distinct patterns
   of the rows on B, up to a constant) - 1, which is a lower bound for the steps strictly inside B [derived, section 5.4's cousin].
2. Decide 80 or 81 at 32 points (longer runs with the gate objective, or the exact programme with junction points restricted to
   0/1 vectors on ONE tree: 2^32 states is too many, so it needs the tree's block structure). A letter at 79 would be news.
3. Free the one thing the letter keeps rigid: let an output be made from TWO earlier outputs (inclusion and exclusion:
   y_S = y_(S-i) + y_(S-j) - y_(S-i-j) + the sum over the sets inside S that contain i and j). The output side becomes a
   graph and the level lemma no longer applies. This is the nearest model that is not a letter and not Yates.
4. The count of section 5 item 6(b) for steps that SUBTRACT nodes: a step with c nodes still contains a "region" (a node
   minus at most c - 1 nodes inside it) with at least 2^(m-1)/c inputs inside D(S). How many such regions can one family of
   nested-or-disjoint sets have, with the union of their members of size at most m? If few, letters are worse than Yates for
   large N by proof and the whole rectangle road is closed for subset sums; if many, the family that has many is the
   construction to look at. This is the one question of this report that is about the order.
5. Close the c-run version of section 6: it would say the two-chain corner count is not O(N) by proof.

## 8. Criticism, last

- "No 11 at 8 points" is exhaustive over input trees but modulo 3, plain inputs, and in the model "all 7 merges of the input
  tree are built". A design that leaves the top merges unbuilt was not counted separately; I expect no gain (the all-ones output
  needs N - 1 additions under it in any circuit) but did not prove it. Signs on the inputs were tried on 14 trees only, other
  input scalings not at all.
- 16, 32, 64 points are an annealer on the spanning model: upper bounds. "Never 31 in 28 runs" is evidence, not a minimum.
  The equality of the spanning model and the exact one was checked at 8 points only, where no row is far from the others.
- The formula (N/2) log2 N - N + 4 has three constants and four data points, two of which (4 and 8) are simply N.
- The level lemma is for the plain spanning model (no junction points, no scalings). The overlap count of section 6 and the
  block-rank bound of section 5 are by hand, tonight, unchecked; the c-run extension is a sketch; and section 5 item 6 was
  written in the last ten minutes of the box with no numerical test at all (it cannot be tested below about 2^40 points,
  which is its own warning: an argument that only bites there can hide an error for a long time).
- I did not read the lower-bound literature for subset sums with subtraction (range sums in the group model); a known bound for
  tree-shaped programs may exist.
- A bug of mine cost five minutes: a `//` comment inserted in the middle of a line of `dwzg.c` commented out N = 2^K (the tool
