# u-6 twotrees: two merge trees do not buy the order, and why; then the same idea moved to subset sums, where it meets a new wall

11 October 2026, 01:40 to about 02:30 EDT, 60-minute box. Private research: nothing published, no git, no Lean, nothing reviewed.
Marks: [ran] = exact run of mine tonight (files in `u-6-twotrees/`, whole numbers and bit masks only); [derived] = by hand tonight,
checked by nobody; [read] = from tonight's reports; [guess].
Gate count everywhere: **(G)**, a gate is any a*u + b*v, scalings free.
**No circuit below any known count. No growth law below N log N.** What is new: one measurement, one placement, one small theorem.

Words. N = 2^k points; an *address* is k bits, read as a subset of {1..k}. *The transform* H: entry (y, x) is (-1)^(common ones).
*Subset sums* Z: y_S = sum of x_T over the T inside S. *Yates*: the usual method for Z, (N/2) log2 N gates.
*Merge tree*: repeatedly join two held sums on disjoint input sets; its *nodes* are the sets under each sum. *Cube tree in bit order
(b1, b2, ...)*: the nodes are the address sets that agree on b1; on b1, b2; and so on. *Tree wire*: entries +1, -1 on one node, 0 elsewhere.
*Full wire*: all N entries +1 or -1 (a sign vector). *Hub* (or *centre*): a full wire that is not an output. *Shift*: w = u - 2v where v is a held
tree wire: it flips the signs of u on v's node. *Agreement rule* [read: h-6]: the result is a sign vector only if v equals u, up to one
sign, on v's node. *Free count*: the same game with the agreement rule dropped (it is then not a circuit, only a count).
*Chain*: an order of the N inputs; its *running sums* are F_b = the sum of the first b + 1 inputs in that order (N - 1 gates).
*Terms* of a vector along a chain: the number of places where it changes value along the order, a 0 being added after the last input;
it is the number of running sums needed to write the vector. *Extra vertex* (Steiner point): a full wire that the walk passes through
and that is not an output.

## 2. Why agreement can never be cheap: the idea is inside wall M  [derived; the theorem is Morgenstern's, from memory, as in j-cross]

Take ANY program whose gates are joins u + v or u - v and shifts u + 2v or u - 2v, on any wires at all. Look at the largest
absolute value of a determinant of a square block of the matrix of held wires. A join at most doubles it, a shift at most triples it.
It starts at 1 and must reach det H = N^(N/2). Hence

  **joins + log2(3) * shifts >= (N/2) log2 N.**

Nothing in this uses trees, bit orders, address maps, hubs, the number of patterns per node, or even that wires are sign vectors.
So: with J joins spent on holding trees, shifts >= ((N/2) log2 N - J) / 1.585. Two trees: shifts >= 0.315 N (k - 4) + 2. At 16 points
this says "at least 2" and is empty, which is why the experiment cannot see it; at 2^10 points it is 1.9 N against the free 1.5 N;
at 2^20, 5 N. **The ratio (shifts with agreement) / (free count) is at least 0.21 (k - 4) for two trees: unbounded.**
What the free count was counting: "flip u on a node" is u - 2 (u restricted to the node), and the restriction is a wire that has to
exist. The agreement rule is not a technical nuisance of the model; it is the determinant bound seen from inside the model.
j-cross already has this wall ("M, one magnitude": 0.3155 N log2 N). m-cross's hybrid 2 walked back into it. The hypothesis to
break is M's: **magnitudes whose ratios grow with N**. [derived] The budget says how much: with c N gates the average gate must
carry log2(|a| + |b|) = k / (2c) bits, so typical coefficients like N^(1/(2c)) between wires of comparable size.

## 3. Yes, and (1): partners that never need agreement, and where that leads  [derived]

A partner needs no agreement if it is the all-plus pattern on its node (the plain sum over the node): adding a multiple of it is
always a gate. Then (i) the walk no longer stays on sign vectors, (ii) by section 2 the multiples must be large, (iii) one tree's
nodes are far too few: in the all-plus dictionary of one tree the transform needs quadratically many terms [read: q-cross, the
corner count of H; here: a character whose top bit is i needs 2^(i-1) nodes]. Use the nodes of all trees at once: the cubes
up(J) = {T containing J} are nodes of every tree whose order starts with the bits of J, and

  (-1)^(common ones of S and T) = sum over J inside both S and T of (-2)^(size of J),   i.e.   H = Z D Z^T.

Read as a tree game: the Z^T half holds ONE all-plus wire on each of N nodes taken from many trees (any two of these nodes overlap
without nesting: this is not one merge tree modulo 2); D gives wire J the magnitude 2^(size of J), typically 2^(k/2) = sqrt N,
exactly the budget of section 2 for c = 1; the Z half is the walk through the outputs, y_S = sum over J inside S of (scaled) wire J.
So the honest form of "two trees without agreement" exists, breaks both "one tree" and "one magnitude" at once, and costs two
subset-sum transforms. Breaking "one tree" alone is section 2 (worthless); breaking them together is this identity.
The twotrees question therefore moves to Z: how cheaply can N overlapping cubes be reached from one another?

## 4. Yes, and (2): two chains for subset sums  [ran: `zchains.c` (to 64 points), `zchains7.c` (128-bit rows, to 128 points)]

Program shape. Hold the running sums of t chains (N - 1 gates for the first, N - 2 for each further one). Walk through the outputs
along a spanning TREE rooted at 0: child = parent + or - running sums of one chain; an edge costs its number of terms; each term is one
gate. q-cross's two-chain program is the case t = 1 with a path. Search: anneal the chain orders (swap two places or reverse a
segment), objective = weight of the minimum spanning tree; the best program is rebuilt and compared with Z entry by entry at the end
("replay exact" in every output file).

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 4 (1.00) | 4 (1.00) | | 6 / 8 | 4 |
| 8 | 10 (1.25) | 8 (1.00) | | 16 / 20 | 12 |

(These are the best of one or two annealing runs each: upper bounds for the model, not minima. For comparison q-cross's path

What I see. (i) Per output, every column grows by 1.25 to 1.55 per doubling, and the factor does not fall with size: this looks
like a power of N (about N^0.4 to N^0.5 per output), not a constant and not a logarithm. (ii) Two chains cost about two thirds of
one chain per output, three chains a little less; from 64 points on two chains win in total gates. (iii) Edge costs spread out as
N grows (at 128 points, one chain: edges of 1 to 11 terms, the commonest 4 and 6): there is no "almost every step is one run".
(iv) Nothing is below Yates.

## 5. A wall for the plain corner programme, several chains, any tree  [derived tonight; three counting lemmas; nobody has checked them]

Written before I saw u-0, u-1, u-2, which prove the one-chain, path case with sharper counts. Kept because it is a different
proof and covers t chains and trees of unbounded degree; where the four overlap they agree.

Setting. t chains. Vertices: 0 and the N rows z_S = indicator of down(S) = {T inside S} (plain picture: no signs). A tree on these
vertices; an edge is written in one chain; its cost is the number of terms of the difference. Level of S = its size m.
Call S *c-good* if, in some chain, down(S) contains an interval (consecutive inputs) of length at least 2^(m-1) / (c+1).

Lemma 1 (an edge makes its upper end good). If the edge between S and S' costs at most c and size(S) >= size(S'), S different from
S', then S is c-good. Proof: the difference is +1 on A = down(S) minus down(S'), which has at least 2^(m-1) elements, and a vector
with at most c changes has at most c + 1 runs; so A, which lies in down(S), contains an interval of length |A| / (c+1). The same holds
for an edge from 0. Consequence: if S' is NOT good, every cheap edge at S' goes to a strict superset S of S' (if S' were not inside
S, the part where the difference is -1, down(S') minus down(S), would have 2^(m'-1) elements and make S' good), S is good, and
down(S) minus down(S') is a union of at most c + 1 intervals.

Lemma 2 (good sets are rare). At level m there are at most t (8 (c+1) N / 2^m + 1) d k^(2d-2) good sets, d = 2 + ceil(log2 (c+1)).
Proof: two sets of level m whose intersection has at most m - d elements have down-sets meeting in at most 2^(m-d) points, less than
half the interval length; two intervals of equal length L that share at most L/2 points start at least L/2 apart; so a family of
good sets with pairwise small intersections has at most 2N / L + 1 members per chain; and every good set is within d - 1 exchanges
of a member of a maximal such family (at most d k^(2d-2) sets each).

Lemma 3 (a good set has few cheap subsets). For S of level m, the S' inside S of level m - j with down(S) minus down(S') a union of
at most c + 1 intervals number at most (2 (c+2) 2^j + 1) d' k^(2d'-2). Proof: the same packing inside the cube down(S), ordered as
the chain orders it: down(S') is then a union of at most c + 2 runs of that induced order, so it contains a run of length
2^(m-j) / (c+2), and runs of sub-cubes that meet in less than half start far apart.

Count. Suppose the tree weighs at most c0 N and put c = 8 c0. Fewer than N/4 vertices have only edges dearer than c. Every other
output is good, or is a cheap subset of a good set of a higher level. By Lemmas 2 and 3 the outputs of level m' of these two kinds
number at most t^2 k P(k) N / 2^(m'), P a polynomial in k of degree O(log c). Below level m0 = O(log t + log c log k) there are at most
k^(m0) outputs in all; from m0 on, the bound sums to N/16. Total: fewer than N/4 + N/8 + o(N) < N. Contradiction once
2^k is much larger than k^(m0).

**Statement.** In the plain picture, with any fixed number of chains (the count allows t up to any fixed power of k), every program
of the shape "output = another output plus running sums of one chain" has more than c N terms for every constant c when k is large.
In particular the corner count of the plain subset-sum picture, per point, is unbounded [q-cross's hope was a bounded number;
its data 5, 13, 30 = 2N - 3, 2N - 2 are the small-size regime, where k^2 is not small against N].
The same holds when running sums are replaced by the node sums of any input merge tree: nodes are intervals of the tree's leaf
order, and c node sums make at most 2c changes.
Two smaller facts from the same packing, easier to test [derived]: a running sum can be the whole difference of at most ONE pair
of outputs (the pair is determined by the set), and the sets down(S) minus down(S') that can be prefixes of one order have sizes
2^a - 2^b or 2^a, so a chain gives at most (k+1)(k+2)/2 one-term edges: **terms >= 2N - t (k+1)(k+2)/2 always**, which is q-cross's
"2N - d" (u-1 has the sharper 2k + 1 for one chain and a path). [ran] The trees found obey it: one-term edges 6, 5, 6 at 32, 64, 128
points with one chain (allowed 21, 28, 36), 18, 17, 15 with two chains.
And at level m at most 4N / 2^m of the cubes {T inside S, T contains i} can be intervals of one order: in the middle levels almost
no output is "its lower neighbour plus one run" (this bears on brief 3, onerun).
Honest size of the theorem: with these constants the contradiction needs k of the order of 250 (u-2's version bites at k = 51).
It is a statement about the order, which is what is asked; the measured rise of section 4 is the evidence at sizes one can see,
and its factor per doubling of N, 2.6 to 3.0 on the total, sits inside u-2's window "between 2.5 and 3 per bit".

## 6. What the wall leaves open, and a first poke at it

The proof uses three things, each a hypothesis to break:
- **Both ends of every step are outputs.** With extra vertices (full wires that are not outputs) the lemmas say nothing. Yates'
  method is of that kind, and so is every letter on an output merge tree that is not a chain (its partial sums over an internal
  node are not outputs). This is the same thing as the hub in the transform's circuits.
- **One chain per step.** A step written with running sums of two chains at once is not a union of few intervals in either order.
- **Plain.** With signs on inputs and outputs the +1 part and the -1 part of a difference are no longer separated.
[ran, `zchains7.c` with a seventh argument; `zs_*.out`] First poke at the first: take the best chains of section 4 and add, greedily
and one at a time, any of the 3^k - 2^k lattice intervals [L, S] = {T : L inside T inside S} (the wires Yates' method holds) as an
extra vertex of the tree. At 8, 16, 32, 64 points, one and two chains: **no single extra vertex shortens the tree** (0 added in all
eight runs). Limit: the chains were optimised without extra vertices, and vertices were tried one at a time; a pair of them, or
chains annealed together with them, was not tried.

**The better question this turned into.** For subset sums, with partners restricted to running sums of a few chains: what is the
smallest set of non-output full wires that brings the terms per output back to a constant, and how does its size grow? If it must
grow like (N/2) log2 N, the chain programme is Yates in disguise; if a linear number suffices, that set is the object to look at.
Smallest case to play: 32 points, two chains, extra vertices free; find any set of extra vertices that brings 46 terms below 2N - 2.

## 7. Criticism, last

- Section 1 is a heuristic count in h-6's model: spanning trees and a greedy improvement, centres restricted to quadratic
  forms without linear part, one sign pattern per node per tree; the wider scan (linear parts) was cut by the time limit. None of the
  65-gate counts was written out and replayed as a circuit. They are constants in any case.
- Section 2 is Morgenstern's bound quoted from memory, as in j-cross; the application is two lines. It is not new; what is new is
  only that the twotrees idea sits inside it, whatever the trees.
- Section 3 is a reading of a known identity (g-4), not a result.
- Section 4's numbers are annealed upper bounds for one model; at 128 points each column is a single run and probably under-annealed.
  The fitted "power of N" is five data points.
- Section 5 is the part to attack. I proved it in my head inside the hour; the constants were not optimised; Lemma 2's covering step
  and Lemma 3's passage to the induced order are where I would look first for a slip. It covers the plain picture only, and steps
  between outputs only. It does not touch general circuits, and it does not say subset sums need more than linear size.
  Three other agents proving the same wall in the same hour is agreement between unreviewed arguments of one kind, not a review.
- Section 6's negative is one greedy pass.
- Nothing here changes the order, or any constant.
