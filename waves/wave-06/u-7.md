# u-7 quotient: the first useful Q gate. What a quotient can and cannot buy

11 October 2026, about 01:42 to 02:15 EDT, 60-minute box. Private. One agent; nothing reviewed, nothing in Lean, nothing published.
Marks: [ran] = exact run of mine tonight (whole numbers only; files in `u-7-quotient/`); [derived] = by hand tonight, checked by
nobody; [read] = from tonight's reports; [guess].
Gate count everywhere: **(G)**, a gate is any a*u + b*v, scalings free. Count (O) is not used.

**No circuit below a known count, no growth law below N log N. Nothing here changes the order or any constant.**

Words. N = 2^k points, addresses are k bits. *Output* h_y: the row y of the transform, entries (-1)^(x.y). *Full wire*: a held
wire with all N entries +1 or -1 (an output, or a *hub* if it is not an output). *Form*: a wire with entries 0, +1, -1; its
*support* D is the set of its non-zeros. A form is *compatible* with an output h if it equals +h or -h on its support.
*Affine span* of D: the smallest set of addresses containing D that is closed under x + y + z; it has 2^j points, j its dimension.
*Class*, *Q gate* (difference of two wires of one class, divided by a power of 2), *shift*, *partner*, *tight*, *over the limit*:
as in WAVE6-PICKS. *Flip*: a shift f -> f - 2p where the form p equals f on its support D; it changes the sign of f on D.

## 0. In ten lines

1. [derived; ran at 8 points on all 3,280 forms] **The partner bound.** A form on a set D that is compatible with m outputs has
   m <= N / 2^(dimension of the affine span of D) <= N / |D|. Equality m |D| = N holds exactly for the tight wires (characters on
   affine subspaces: 128 at 8 points). So a quotient wire with entries 0, +1, -1, used as the partner of the last step into an
   output, obeys the same product law as a tight wire. **The number of outputs one quotient can finish does not grow with N; it
   falls as the quotient gets large.** Section 2.
2. [ran] With any coefficients and any held non-output wire as the second parent: a new quotient of two full wires is one gate
   from **at most 2 outputs at 8, 16 and 32 points** (the 19, and the staircase at three sizes), and the only ones that reach 2
   are the half pieces made from two outputs (m-cross's circular case). Section 3.
3. [derived; ran to 64 points] Why m-cross's circularity holds at every size: h_a - h_b = +-(h_c - h_d) forces {a,b} = {c,d}.
   No coincidence among 28, 120, 496, 2,016 pairs. Section 1.
4. [derived] **Where two wires equal modulo 4 come from: three signs satisfy a + b + c = -abc modulo 4.** So for full wires
   f1 + f2 + f3 = -(f1 f2 f3) modulo 4, entries 1 and 3: every parent of the brief (u = h - 4 h*a) is a sum of three sign vectors
   whose entry-by-entry product is -h, and a is the set where the three agree. Two gates each. A parent in ONE gate exists only
   where the output itself is one gate away (it is the same gate with the wrong sign of the coefficient). Section 4.
5. [derived] A quotient modulo 4 of two such parents is a quotient modulo 2 taken twice: it is a full wire restricted to the
   intersection of two difference sets, and it lives in the span of four full wires whose product is +1 everywhere. For outputs
   those are the planes of four outputs (m-cross's quarter pieces). Section 4.
6. [ran] **Inside the records nothing can be reused.** Take every pair of held full wires and both quotients (f - f')/2, (f + f')/2:
   156, 90, 380, 1,560 quotients in the 19 and the staircase at 8, 16, 32 points, and all of them are different. No difference
   of two full wires occurs twice. A Q gate would have nothing to carry from one place to another. Section 3.
7. [ran + derived] Gaussian integers: H S H = (1+i)^k S^-1 H S^-1 and (H S)^3 = (1+i)^k N hold exactly at 8 and 16 points
   (S = the diagonal i^(number of ones)). A quotient by 1+i of two product wires is a bent hub in one complex gate. But a complex
   product wire with factors (1, +-i) is nothing more than two real forms, one on the even-weight addresses and one on the odd,
   and the "real part" is their sum or difference: half order is a join on the parity split. No new law. Section 5.
8. [ran] Subset sums: **none of the 2,748 optimal 8-point subset-sum circuits (12 gates) contains a gate that can even be read
   as a Q gate**, in any order of its wires. Section 5b.
9. [ran] The contrast that names the wall: the published unit's 120 wires live in 8 dimensions and 3,360 pairs of them have a
   sum or difference that is again one of the 120, each of the 120 hit 28 times. The transform's N outputs live in N dimensions
   and no difference is hit twice. **A quotient has something to carry only where there are more wires than dimensions.**
   A piece of the transform of that kind, exact: the 120 x 120 block of the 256-point transform on the addresses where a
   quadratic form is 1 equals 8 I + J - 2 (G o G), G the table of inner products of the 120 lines of E8: a diagonal plus rank 35
   (1 of 6 at 16 points, 7 of 28 at 64), and its spinor half is a 64 x 28 block of the 64-point transform. Section 6.
10. The idea survives in one place, which no bound here touches: **quotients used on hubs, not on outputs** (a translation
   carried between intermediate full wires). That needs four held full wires at the corners of a parallelogram; the records have
   none. The question it becomes is in section 6.

## 1. Why quotients of outputs are circular (all sizes)

[derived] For a != b, h_a - h_b = 2 h_a on the half-space {x : (a+b).x = 1} and 0 elsewhere. If h_a - h_b = +-(h_c - h_d) the two
half-spaces are equal, so a + b = c + d = z, and h_a = +-h_c on that half-space. The ratio h_a h_c is the character of a + c; it
is constant on the half-space of z only if a + c is 0 or z. So {a, b} = {c, d}. A half piece made from two outputs can be the
difference of no other pair of outputs.
[ran, `qa.out` part 1] 8, 16, 32, 64 points: 0 coincidences among 28, 120, 496, 2,016 pairs.

## 2. The partner bound

**Lemma** [derived]. Let p be a form with support D, and let A be the set of y with p = +-h_y on D. Then A lies in one coset of
the space orthogonal to the directions of D, so |A| <= N / 2^j <= N / |D|, where 2^j is the size of the affine span of D.
Proof. For y, y' in A the product h_y h_y' is the character of y + y' and is constant on D, so (y + y').(x - x0) = 0 for all x
in D and one fixed x0 in D. The differences x - x0 span a space of dimension j; its orthogonal has N / 2^j elements.
[ran, `qa.out` part 2] 8 points, all 3,280 forms up to sign: no violation. Table (size of D, number of compatible outputs: how
many forms): (1,8): 8; (2,4): 56; (3,2): 224; (4,2): 56; (4,1): 448; (4,0): 56; (5,1): 448; (5,0): 448; (6,1): 224; (6,0): 672;
(7,1): 64; (7,0): 448; (8,1): 8; (8,0): 120. Equality m |D| = N: 8 + 56 + 56 + 8 = 128 forms, the characters on the 8 points,
28 lines, 14 planes and the whole space.

What it says about the brief's first experiment. [derived by the curator, confirmed] every form is the quotient (u - v)/4 of two
parents congruent to one output h modulo 4: the quotient wires are simply ALL 3^N forms. As the partner of a last step
(output = full wire -+ 2 * form) a form must be compatible with that output. So a quotient on |D| points finishes at most
N / |D| outputs, exactly like a tight wire, and a quotient on more than N/2 points finishes at most one. The brief asked whether
"a quotient of two full-width parents has no such limit". For the step into outputs it has the same limit. Births into outputs
per quotient: at most N / |D|, not growing with N.

Where the bound stops (this is the hatch): it says nothing about a form used to flip a HUB. A form on D can flip every held full
wire that agrees with it on D, and for hubs there is no limit on how many do.

## 4. Parents: wires equal to an output modulo 4

**Identity** [derived; four cases by hand]. For signs a, b, c: a + b + c = -abc modulo 4. More generally a sum of 2m+1 signs is
(-1)^m times their product modulo 4.
Consequences, all [derived]:
- f1 + f2 + f3 = -(f1 f2 f3) modulo 4 for sign vectors, with entries 3 where the three agree and 1 elsewhere. The brief's parent
  u = h - 4 h*a is exactly such a sum with f1 f2 f3 = -h and a = the set where the three agree; and every such u is a sum of
  three sign vectors in 3^(N - |a|) ways. Two gates from three held full wires.
- For outputs: h_a + h_b + h_c = -h_(a+b+c) modulo 4. Every sum of three outputs is congruent to minus the fourth output of
  their plane. The quotient (h_a + h_b + h_c + h_(a+b+c))/4 is h_a on a quarter of the points: m-cross's quarter piece.
- Four sign vectors with product -1 everywhere: (f1 + f2 + f3 + f4)/2 is again a sign vector. This is the 4-point brick applied
  entry by entry (the 4-point transform divided by 2 maps such quadruples to such quadruples).
- **One-gate parents.** In a stock of forms, a wire a*s + b*t with all entries of size 1 or 3 is, up to scale, one of: s + 2t
  with s full (entries 3 where t = s, sign changed where t = -s); 3s - 2 s|D; s + 3t with s, t on complementary sets; or
  s - 4 s|D and 3s - 4 s|D, which are congruent to s itself. In the first three cases the wire is congruent modulo 4 to
  s - 2t or s - t, a wire one gate from the same two parents. So **a parent of an output h comes in one gate only when h itself
  is one gate from the stock; the parent is that gate with the wrong sign.** Two parents for the same h never cost less than
  two gates each unless h is already one gate away. (By hand, for stocks with entries 0, +1, -1; not run.)
- **Two parents sharing two of their three wires are equal**: f1 + f2 + f3 = f1 + f2 + f5 modulo 4 forces f3 = f5. Sharing one
  wire: (u - v)/4 = (f2 + f3 - f4 - f5)/4 with f2 f3 = f4 f5, three gates from four full wires, and it equals f2 on
  {f2 = f3} and {f2 != f4}: **the quotient modulo 4 is two quotients modulo 2**, a full wire cut down to the intersection of two
  difference sets. It needs four held full wires with product +1 everywhere.
- [ran, `qb.out` B4] Such quadruples in the records: in the 19, 286 triples of full wires, 72 with a held product (18
  quadruples: the 14 planes of outputs and 4 that involve hubs); no triple of hubs has an output as its product (0 of 10).
  Staircase 16: 189 quadruples, 140 of them planes of outputs; 4 triples of hubs whose product is a hub, none an output.
- A Q gate cannot leave the plane spanned by its two parents. So the wires reachable by Q gates alone from m wires are the
  vectors v with 2^j v in the lattice they span: Q gates fill in the lattice, they add no dimension.
- **The ladder** [derived; two halves ran, `qd.out` D1, 8 and 16 points]. The wires congruent to a whole-number combination of
  outputs modulo 2^j form a ladder whose j-th rung is the Reed-Muller code of degree j (functions of the address bits of degree
  at most j). Ran: the indicator of every coordinate subcube of codimension j is 2^-j times a sum of 2^j outputs; and the
  normal form of the transform under whole-number row and column operations is 2^j with multiplicity C(k, j) (1,3,3,1 and
  1,4,6,4,1). Read off: a sign vector h * (-1)^g is congruent to an output modulo 4 only if g has degree 1, that is only if it
  IS an output, so **every hub sits on rung 1 and no hub is congruent to any output modulo 4**; a parent h - 4 h*a sits on rung
  2, and on rung 3 exactly when the indicator of a has degree at most 2 [that last clause derived only].

So the answer to "where do two different cheap wires that are equal modulo 4 come from": from four full wires whose product is
+1 at every point. Among outputs that is the group law and it is circular. Among hubs it is a condition nobody has asked a
search to satisfy, and the records satisfy it 4 times at 8 points.

## 5. Gaussian integers  [ran, `qc.out`; derived]

lam = 1 + i, 2 = -i lam^2. S = diagonal i^(weight of x). Rows of H S are the complex product wires with factors (1, +-i).
- [ran, 8 and 16 points, exact] H S H = lam^k S^-1 H S^-1 and (H S)^3 = lam^k N. (The third-turn identity of g-7 in this dress:
  every entry of the double transform H S H is divisible by lam^k, half the valuation of N.)
- [ran] For every pair of rows c_y, c_z of H S, exactly 2 of the 4 units u make (c_y - u c_z)/lam a wire with unit entries
  (56 of 112 at 8 points, 240 of 480 at 16). Real parts of (1 +- i) times those: 32 sign vectors at 8 points (distances
  2,2,2,2,4,4,4,4 to the outputs), 128 at 16 points, of which 64 are at distance 6 from all 16 outputs (bent) and 64 have
  distances 4,4,4,4,8,...,8. The 8 (16) hubs of h-5 are among them. The centre of the 19 is not.
- [derived] A step of half order, w - lam * (w on D), multiplies the entries on D by -i: a quarter turn. Two of them are a flip.
- [derived] A complex product wire c_y has its real part on the even-weight addresses and its imaginary part on the odd-weight
  ones. Over the reals it is two forms on the two halves of the parity split, and Re((1 +- i) c_y) is their sum or difference:
  one join. The Gaussian quotient is that join. The partner bound of section 2 applies to phase forms word for word.
  **Half order gives no law the reals do not have.**

## 5b. Subset sums: is there a Q gate in an optimal circuit?  [ran, `qe.py`, `qe.out`]

Input: h-4's list of all 2,772 sets of five helper wires that give a 12-gate subset-sum circuit at 8 points modulo 3
(`h-4-zetafloor/z8_p3_full.local.out`), digit 2 read as -1. 2,748 replay exactly over the whole numbers (h-4's count). For each
I listed every way each of the 12 made wires is a*u + b*v of two of the 20 wires (exact test, h-4's `in_span`), the kind of
that gate by supports, and by a dynamic programme over the 4,096 subsets the least number of Q gates over all orders.
Result: **least number of Q gates 0 in all 2,748; and in none of them does any wire have even one pair of parents with a common
support.** So at 8 points a Q gate is absent from every optimal subset-sum circuit of that list, as it is from every transform
circuit of the night. (m-cross's shadow lemma then applies to all of them: each is, modulo 2, a circuit over the two-element
field.) Limit: the list is "found modulo 3 with helper entries 0, 1, 2"; circuits with other entries are not in it.

## 6. What the idea turned into

Three ways round the failure, and where each ended:
1. "Parents that are not outputs" -> they are sums of three full wires; the quotient needs four full wires with product +1
   (section 4). Circular for outputs, absent among hubs in the records.
2. "Entries of two sizes" -> the one-gate parents are the wrong-sign twins of one-gate outputs (section 4). No profit.
3. "Half order over the Gaussian integers" -> a join on the parity split (section 5).
What is left, and is not covered by any bound of tonight:

**A quotient pays only when it carries a translation between hubs.** Picture every circuit with entries 0, +1, -1 as a set of
forms, with gates = (number of forms held) - N. A form is born from below (join of two forms on disjoint sets), sideways (a flip
of a form by a held sub-form), or **from above** (Q: a form restricted to where it agrees or differs with a second form on the
same set; or a form minus a compatible sub-form). The full wires and the flips between them make a graph. A Q gate closes a
cycle in that graph: its quotient is the signed sum of the forms along the other path between its two parents, obtained in one
gate instead of (length of the path - 1) joins. It pays when that sum is used again as one flip somewhere else, that is when
four held full wires f, f', g, g' satisfy f - f' = g - g' (a parallelogram). By section 2, if g' is an output the form lives on
at most N/2 points for 2 uses, N/4 for 4. If g and g' are hubs there is no limit.

**The contrast** [ran, `qf.out`]. Among the 120 lines of E8 that the published unit is built on [read: h-7, through m-cross;
I did not read the certificate] the quotient is everywhere. Take one root per line, 120 vectors in 8 dimensions: of the 7,140
pairs, 3,360 have a sum or difference that is again one of the 120, and each of the 120 is hit by exactly 28 pairs. In every
transform circuit of the night the count is "each difference once" (section 3). A root system is closed under differences
wherever the inner product is odd; N independent outputs in N dimensions have no reason to be. So "where do two
different cheap wires that are equal modulo 4 come from" has a second answer: **from a set of wires much larger than the
dimension of its span**. For the transform that can only be a piece of it that is a diagonal plus a matrix of low rank.
One such piece, checked exactly at 256 points [ran]: take Q(x) = x1x2 + x3x4 + x5x6 + x7x8 on the address bits and the 120
addresses with Q = 1 (120 = (256 - 16)/2, each with 56 neighbours: h-7's numbers). The 120 x 120 block of the transform on
those rows and columns (columns relabelled by swapping the paired bits) is M with M^2 + 8M - 128 = 0 exactly and trace 120:
**M = 8 I - 24 P, P a projection of rank 35.** [ran, `qg.out`, 16 and 64 points; `qf.out`, 256] At N points the block on the
n = (N - sqrt N)/2 addresses with Q = 1 is (sqrt N / 2) I - (3 sqrt N / 2) P with rank(P) = n (sqrt N - 2)/(3 sqrt N):
**1 of 6 at 16 points, 7 of 28 at 64, 35 of 120 at 256**, tending to one third [the formula for larger sizes is the trace
count]. The best ratio is at the smallest size. (This is the local face of h-5's hub that differs from every output on
(N - sqrt N)/2 points; the classical name is the graph of a quadric.)
A seed [ran, `qh.out`, exact]: 35 = 36 - 1 is the space of symmetric 8 x 8 matrices without the trace. Index the 120 by one
root r_j of E8 per line, G = their table of inner products (2 on the diagonal, 0 and +-1 elsewhere). Then the block is
**M = 8 I + J - 2 (G o G)**, G o G being G squared entry by entry (checked on all 14,400 entries, with M^2 + 8M - 128 = 0 again).
So the low-rank part is x -> T = sum of x_j r_j r_j^T (a symmetric 8 x 8 matrix), then y_i = r_i^T T r_i. For the 56 roots with
two non-zero coordinates that is T_aa + T_bb +- 2 T_ab. For the 64 roots with eight half-integer coordinates it is a sum of
+-T_ab over the 28 pairs a < b with signs e_a e_b, and [ran] those 28 sign patterns are pairwise orthogonal, generate a group of
exactly 64 sign vectors, and multiply into each other exactly when the two pairs share a point: they are 28 of the 64
characters of a 64-element group, sitting on its quadric (28 = (64 - 8)/2). **The spinor half of the 256-point block is a
64 x 28 block of the 64-point transform with its columns on the 64-point quadric** (rows relabelled by an address map), used
once forwards and once transposed. So the 256-point piece calls a piece of the 64-point transform of the same kind.
Not done: a gate count for this route; the same step from 64 points down to 16, where the block is 2 I minus one sign vector
times itself; and any comparison with the certificate of the published unit (h-7 reports rank 8 for its scatter matrix, which
has entries +-1/2 where this block has +-1: G itself where this is G o G).

**The better question.** Is there a set of O(N) sign vectors containing the N outputs, with many parallelograms among its
non-outputs, in which every form has a birth? Put the other way: more useful wires than the dimension they span, at every
scale. Smallest exact case: 8 points, forms only (3,280 of them), births from below, sideways and above: is the minimum number of forms 27 (the 19), or less? Then: does the minimum over circuits with births from
above grow like (N/2) log2 N or slower. m-cross's floor is stated for "no Q gate"; this is the first model in which it could
be tested with Q gates and a finite pool.

A caution I derived on the way [derived]: any design that holds all characters of one half-space together with N/2 outputs of
different classes holds both half-size transforms up to N gates (the outputs minus their held halves are the characters of the
other half). So a recursion "all forms on one half, then a walk through the outputs" cannot beat the textbook; a saving must
avoid ever holding a complete level.

What a linear law would look like here [derived]: a reduction of the N-point transform to a bounded number of pieces of the
N^(1-e)-point transform with O(N) gates of glue gives O(N) outright (T(N) = c T(N^(1-e)) + a N). The quadric blocks are pieces,
not the whole matrix, and their rank fraction tends to 1/3, not to 0; nothing above closes that gap.

## 7. Criticism, last

- The partner bound covers forms (entries 0, +1, -1) used with coefficient 2 on a full wire. For other coefficients and other
  second parents I have only the runs of section 3, on the stocks of two circuit families up to 32 points.
- "All quotients distinct" is a fact about four circuits. It is close to forced when every partner is a tree wire used once;
  I did not prove it, and I did not test h-1's 52s, the 54 or h-8's 21.
- The Reed-Muller ladder in section 4: the inclusion and the divisor count ran at 8 and 16 points; the rung-3 clause is by hand.
- The 120 x 120 block is one exact computation at 256 points; the formula for other sizes is a trace count that assumes the
  same two eigenvalues. Its link to the published unit is by the numbers 120, 56 only; I did not read the certificate.
- The subset-sum census covers h-4's list (entries 0, 1, -1, found modulo 3), not all circuits.
- The one-gate-parent statement is a case list by hand for stocks with entries 0, +1, -1.
- I did not run the search of section 6. Nothing here is a circuit.
- The brief's item (c), the smallest closed loop, is answered only as a bound: two parents (4 gates, or 3 from four full wires),
  one quotient, k <= 2 outputs in every stock tried; it never pays there.
