11 October 2026, about 03:14 to 03:40 EDT, 30-minute box. Private. One skeptic; nothing in Lean, nothing published.
Marks: [ran] = exact run of mine tonight, my own code (`v-1/`); [derived] = by hand tonight; [read]; [guess].
Gate count: **(G)** throughout (a gate is any a*u + b*v, scalings free).

**Verdict: the wall holds. No circuit, no saving, no growth law below N log N comes out of it. It is a correct lower bound for one
restricted program shape, and it closes that shape for good: at least about N^1.32 / log N gates, against Yates' (N/2) log2 N.**

Words. N = 2^k points; an address is a subset of {1..k}. *Subset sums*: y_S = sum of x_T over the T inside S. *Yates*: the usual
method, (N/2) log2 N gates. *Picture*: the 0/1 table "T inside S" with the outputs in one chosen order (rows) and the inputs in
another (columns), a row and a column of zeros added at the end. *Step*: two neighbouring rows (the last row's neighbour is the
zero row). *Difference of a step*: row minus next row; its entries are +1, 0, -1. *Corner*: a place where a difference changes
value along the inputs. *Two-chain program*: running sums of the inputs in the column order, then each output from its
neighbour; it costs N - 2 + corners gates. *Run*: a maximal stretch of consecutive inputs on which a difference is one non-zero
value. *Plain*: no signs or scalings on rows or columns.

## 1. The claim, stated exactly

One model: plain picture, any order of the inputs, the outputs on a path (any order). One quantity: c(N), the least number of
corners. Four lower bounds on it, and one yes-or-no fact:

| source | statement | growth it gives for corners |
|---|---|---|
| u-0, section 3 | a step of cost c forces c or fewer runs, so a block of 2^(size-1)/c consecutive inputs inside one output; such blocks pack badly | per point at least 2^(c k / log k), more than any power of log N |
| u-1, section 2 | #(steps whose larger output has size s and whose +1 part is at most r runs) <= 2 (N-L+1) C(k-lam, s-lam) / (l-L+1), l = 2^(s-1)/r, L <= l, lam = log2 L rounded up | N^(1.10 - o(1)) |
| u-2, section 3 | runs >= (5/2)^k / (400 (1 + ln N)) | N^1.32 / log N |
| u-6, section 5 | the same kind of count for outputs on ANY tree and up to a power-of-k number of chains, as long as both ends of every edge are outputs | more than c N for every constant c |
| u-2, sections 1, 2 | "every step is exactly one run" is possible at 4 and 8 points, impossible at 16 on a path and at 32 on a tree | (a yes-or-no fact) |

## 3. The proofs, read line by line [derived]

All four hold. I found no wrong step. What I found instead:

1. **u-2 is correct and is the strongest.** Step 1 (the j-th longest run through an input lies inside j/2 different outputs at
   once, so it has at most 2N/j inputs), step 2 (Cauchy-Schwarz), step 3 (every path makes each input change membership often).
   The last line, 5^k/100, is proved for all k: with J binomial (k, 1/5), the mean of (J/k)^2 is at least 1/25, and the "- 1/2"
   costs at most a factor 4.
2. **A slip of presentation in u-2.** The sizes "passes N at k = 35, 3N at 41, Yates at 51" are true for the EXACT sum
   (I get 35, 41, 51 and the factor 2.474 per bit at k = 100). The displayed formula with the constant 400 gives 43, 48, 59.
   Either way: provably worse than Yates from N = 2^51.
3. **u-1's step "each output is the top of at most two steps"** is true on a path for the plain reason that an output has two
   neighbours. It is false on a tree. Lemma 3 and the entropy arithmetic are right: I get the threshold 0.1028, exponent 1.1028.
   Two improvements, free: N/2 steps have a large top, not N/4; and letting the size threshold move gives exponent **1.1127**
   (at size 0.555 k). My explicit version of the bound: 1.22 N at k = 19, 10.7 N at k = 64, 110 N at k = 96, 1,229 N at k = 128
   (u-1: 1.001, 3.65, 28.5, 308). Still far below u-2.
4. **u-2 on trees.** Step 3 loses nothing on a tree: hang every output on its edge towards "nothing"; that is one edge per
   output. Only step 1 feels the tree: an output with D neighbours can be the top of D steps, so the bound is multiplied by 2/D,
   as u-2 says. A hub with (5/4)^k neighbours escapes u-2. u-6 covers hubs, with a weaker law that its count implies:
   per output at least 2^(c k / (log k)^2) [derived from its section 5].
5. **u-6's three lemmas** read correct, including the two places its author doubted (the covering step; the passage to the
   induced order inside a down-set). Its hypothesis is the real boundary of the whole wall: both ends of every edge are outputs.
6. **A gap between two notions.** "One run" (the difference is a single stretch of +1 or of -1) is slightly narrower than
   "at most two corners in the step": the pattern "+1 on a beginning, then -1, then 0" has two corners and two runs. The
   exhaustive "no" answers the first question only. It does not matter for the growth law.

## 4. The exponents 1.10 and 1.32 reconciled

Same model, same quantity, no conflict. They are three lower bounds of rising strength and one upper bound:

  N * 2^(c k / log k)  (u-0)   <=   N^1.10 (u-1; 1.11 optimised)   <=   (5/2)^k / (400 (1 + ln N)) = N^1.32 / log N  (u-2)
  <=   c(N)   <=   7 * 3^(k-2) + 2 = 0.78 N^1.585  (Gray on both sides).

Why they differ: u-0 counts outputs through pairwise intersections (expensive); u-1 counts the outputs above one window
directly; u-2 uses all the runs through one input at once and both signs. The truth lies between 2.5 and 3 per address bit.

## 5. Is it known?

- The exact statement (corners of the subset-sum table under two orders) I did not find. [read, three searches, not exhaustive]
- The nearest known theory. Subset sums are *dominance sums* (sum the points below a corner) for the 2^k points of {0,1}^k.
  Chazelle, "Lower bounds for off-line range searching", Discrete and Computational Geometry 17 (1997) 53-65: for n points and n
  boxes in a FIXED dimension d, at least n log log n operations with subtraction, at least n (log n / log log n)^(d-1) without.
  Here the dimension is log2 n, outside those theorems.
- Without subtraction the question is settled: Kennes (1992) shows the additions-only subset-sum transform on a lattice needs
  one addition per edge of its diagram, (N/2) log2 N for subsets, so Yates is optimal there [read through Bjorklund, Husfeldt,
  Kaski, Koivisto and others, arXiv:1603.03889].
- With subtraction nothing above linear is known for subset sums. So the known theory predicts nothing for "linear or not": it
  says N log N if subtraction is forbidden and leaves the rest open.
- The mechanism (few sets of a family can be intervals of one order) is the consecutive-ones problem (Booth and Lueker 1976
  for the test; minimising the number of blocks is NP-hard). Tonight's lemma is that problem for sub-cubes. [from memory]

## 6. Does it scale?

Yes, and against the user. Lower bound: factor 2.47 per bit at k = 100, tending to 2.5. Upper bound: exactly 3 per bit (Gray).
Searched minima 5, 13, 30, 80, then at most 239 and 715: factors 2.6, 2.3, 2.7, at most 3.0, 3.0. Yates' own factor tends to 2.
Corners per point are unbounded; the hope of q-cross section 4 ("if corners per point stay bounded, subset sums cost O(N)") is
refuted in its own model.

## 7. In the user's terms

- Circuit size for n points from this route: **at least n - 2 + n^1.32 / (400 (1 + ln n)), at most n - 2 + 0.78 n^1.585.**
  Saving exponent: none. It is worse than N log N from 2^51 points on, and worse than Yates in every search below that.
- What it rules out: subset sums, hence the transform (two subset-sum transforms and one layer of scalings), in linear size by
  "running sums of the inputs in one order, each output from another output".
- What it does not rule out, in order of how solid the exclusion is:
  1. *helper wires*: values that are not outputs (Yates has them; so does every output merge tree that is not a chain). This is
     the hypothesis every one of the four proofs needs. Nothing proved.
  2. *signs or scalings on BOTH sides*. u-1 has a sketch, u-2 covers negated inputs only. Not checked by anyone.
  3. *several orders combined inside one step*, and tables of running sums in two or more dimensions (u-2 tried regular tables:
     they triple per bit too).
- It is a bound in a model. It says nothing about general circuits for subset sums.
