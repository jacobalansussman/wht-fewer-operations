passes in H = D_0 K_1 D_1 ... K_r D_r with free diagonal matrices D_i. A *hyperplane* is a subspace of half the addresses.

## 2. x-4 lemma D

**Claim.** If H = D_0 K_1 D_1 ... K_r D_r with flag kernels K_i (any flags) and diagonals D_i, then r >= 2^(ceil(k/r) - 2).

**The proof, re-derived [derived], three steps.** (i) Cut each kernel at level t: the terms with j <= t are zero unless
x + y lies in M^i_t (blocks of 2^t addresses); the terms with j > t are constant on the cosets of M^i_(t+1), so their sum has
rank at most N/2^(t+1). (ii) Product of r such factors = (product of the block parts) + (a matrix of rank at most
r N/2^(t+1)): telescoping, each of the r differences contains one low-rank factor. The product of the block parts is zero
unless x + y lies in W = M^1_t + ... + M^r_t, which has at most 2^(rt) addresses. (iii) If rt < k, W lies in a hyperplane P;
for x in P and y outside P the block parts give nothing, so that block of H has rank at most r N/2^(t+1). It has rank at
least N/4. Take the largest t with rt < k, t = ceil(k/r) - 1. Nothing wrong found.

**My runs** [ran, `z-2/lemmaD.py`, `lemmaD.out`].
- The rank fact: for every hyperplane, the block "rows in it, columns outside it" of H has rank N/4 (N/2 - 1 hyperplanes) or
  N/2 (N/2 hyperplanes), at 8, 16, 32, 64, 128 points (x-4 ran 8 to 32). Modulo 2^61 - 1, which can only under-count the
  rational rank, so "at least N/4" is safe.
- The count itself: 21 random products (random flags, weights, diagonals; r = 1 to 4; 16, 32, 64 points), every hyperplane
  containing W, every t with rt < k: no block exceeds r N/2^(t+1). The bound is met with equality (rank 8 against 8 at 32
  points with r = 2, t = 2; 16 against 16 at 64 points), so the count has no slack of its own. Exact fractions up to 32
  points; at 64 points modulo the prime (a check, not a proof, in that direction).

**It holds for more than claimed** [derived]. ANY block of N/2 rows and N/2 columns of a sign matrix with orthogonal rows has
rank at least N/4 (the squares of its singular values add up to N^2/4 and none exceeds N). So lemma D holds for every
relabelling of inputs and outputs, linear or not, and for every Hadamard matrix. Random blocks: least ranks 2, 5, 13, 28, 63
at 8 to 128 points; a block built to be bad (rows a hyperplane, columns in equal pairs) has exactly N/4 [ran].

**Pushed further** [ran]. Least r allowed by lemma D alone:

| k | 4 | 6 | 8 | 16 | 64 | 1024 | 4096 | 65536 | 2^20 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| r at least | 2 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 13 | 128 | 410 | 4682 | 61681 |
| r log2(k) / k | 1.00 | 0.86 | 1.13 | 1.00 | 1.22 | 1.25 | 1.20 | 1.14 | 1.18 |

Growth law: r >= c k / log2 k, with c between 0.86 and 1.33 at every k I tabulated (14 values up to 2^20). (x-4's table entry 3 at k = 6 is lemma C, not lemma D.)

**What the count cannot do** [derived]. With r = k/2 (the known program, one pair of bits per pass) the count at t = 1 reads
N/4 <= (k/2) N/4: slack of a factor k/2. So lemma D does not decide between k / log2 k and k/2, as its author says.
It is a count of the kind "block-diagonal plus low rank" (matrix rigidity). [recalled] The transform is known NOT to be rigid
(Alman and Williams 2017, arXiv 1611.05558; Dvir and Liu 2019, arXiv 1902.07334), so a count of this kind cannot be the way
to a bound for circuits in general; it bites here only because the blocks are forced to be cosets of subspaces.

**In the user's terms.** A program that is a sequence of separately computed flag passes has at least N gates for every pass that is
not a mere relabelling (every output of such a pass is a new wire) and at most 3N - 3, so it costs between about N k / log2 k and (9/8) N k. Saving exponent 0.
Not linear. If r = k / log2 k were reached, the gain would be a factor log log N: not a power of log N, so below the
standing bar either way. The model is narrow: over the real numbers it cannot even write one butterfly in one pass.

## 3. x-4 lemma C, completed over the real numbers

**Claim.** H = D_0 K_1 D_1 K_2 D_2 is impossible from 32 points on, real numbers. m_i = the first step of flag i;
e = the sign H(m_1, m_2); chi(y) = H(m_1, y), psi(x) = H(x, m_2). Peeling gives: for all x, y with x + y outside
{0, m_1, m_2, m_1 + m_2}, 1 - p(x) chi(y) - q(y) psi(x) + e p(x) q(y) chi(y) psi(x) = 0, with p(x) p(x + m_1) = 1 and
q(y) q(y + m_2) = 1.

**Case e = +1** [derived, then ran]. The bracket is (1 - p chi)(1 - q psi). If some p(x0) is not +1 or -1, then q(y) =
psi(x0) off four points, and any x with the other value of psi needs p(x) = chi(y) for N - 8 values of y, which contain both
values of chi once N/2 > 8: impossible. So p and q are signs. Put a = p psi, b = q chi; the condition is "a(x) = b(y) = c
implies psi(x) chi(y) = c". If the set a = +1 contains both values of psi, the set b = +1 has at most 8 addresses, and then
a = -1 is empty once N/2 - 8 > 4; so a = +1 everywhere and b = -1 everywhere. Otherwise p and q are constants, which fails.
Result: exactly two solutions, p = eta psi and q = -eta chi, for every N >= 32, and with them the bracket is zero everywhere.
[ran, `z-2/lemmaC2.py`, `lemmaC2.out`, exhaustive over sign-valued p, q]: 32 points, all 465 pairs m_1, m_2 with
m_1.m_2 = 0 (x-4's 450 plus the 15 with m_1 = m_2): exactly these two solutions each. 64 points, 85 pairs (every 23rd):
the same. 16 points: 18 of 105 pairs have a third solution (x-4's "three at 16 points"); 8 points: up to 36.

**Case e = -1** [derived, not run]. The bracket forces p chi different from -1, then q(y) = psi(x) g(x)^chi(y) with
g = (1 - p)/(1 + p), so g = A psi and q = A^chi, and the bracket is zero everywhere. Same as x-4's line; I agree with it.

**Case m_1 = m_2 = m, the one x-4 only sketched** [derived; the first identity ran, `lemmaC3.py`, `lemmaC3.out`, 60 random cases at 8, 16, 32 points, exact]. The
peeling leaves a_1 a_2 (d(x) + d(x + m)) = 0, so D_1 = diag(d) is odd under the shift by m. Then the cross term of the two
kernels vanishes and, with u = 1/d_0, v = 1/d_2, G_i = the part of kernel i that is constant on pairs {z, z + m}:

    H(x, y) u(x) v(y) = d(x) G_2(x + y) + d(y) G_1(x + y)     for x + y outside {0, m}.

Writing it at (x + m, y) and at (x, y + m) and comparing gives u(x + m) = lambda psi(x) u(x) and
v(y + m) = -lambda chi(y) v(y) with lambda^2 = H(m, m). Over the real numbers lambda = +1 or -1, and then
2 d(y) G_1(x + y) = H u v (1 + lambda chi(y) psi(x)), 2 d(x) G_2(x + y) = H u v (1 - lambda chi(y) psi(x)). So chi = psi,
one of G_1, G_2 is zero on a hyperplane (minus {0, m}) and nonzero off it, and, being a flag function, it is a CONSTANT off
that hyperplane while the other one vanishes there. Then the block "rows in the hyperplane, columns outside" of H is a
rank-one matrix up to scalings. Its rank is at least N/4. Contradiction from 8 points on.
Over the Gaussian numbers lambda = i or -i is possible when H(m, m) = -1 and this argument stops: the sub-case x-4 names
as open stays open there.

**Verdict.** Lemma C holds over the real numbers for every N >= 32, all three cases now with an argument; cases 2 and 3 rest
on hand work of tonight (x-4's and mine). It changes two entries of a table (k = 5, 6), not the growth law.

So the hatch the record uses is named: **a hub whose two halves disagree on the lower bits** (a sign vector that is not a

**In the user's terms.** The floor proves that a larger coefficient on a two-piece hub never changes the order (rate 3/4 at

## 5. The transposition rule, and the two short lemmas

**Rule** [derived; ran, `z-2/transpose.py`, `transpose.out`]. Take a program with g gates for a matrix with m rows (outputs)
and n columns (inputs), no zero row or column, outputs on m different wires. Reverse every arrow, keeping the number on it.
A wire used d times becomes a sum of d terms, d - 1 gates (d if it is also an output). Total: 2g - (n + g - m) =
**g + m - n**. Square matrix: the same count. Free scalings are exactly what makes it clean: the numbers ride on the arrows.
316 random programs (2 to 7 inputs, up to 22 gates, coefficients up to 3, some outputs reused inside): the reversed program
computes the transpose exactly and has g + m - n gates, every time. The 19-gate circuit reversed: 19 gates, exact.
Count (O) [derived, not run]: additions change by m - n, scalar multiplications are unchanged.
[recalled] Known: Bordewijk 1956; Fiduccia 1973; Hopcroft and Musinski 1973; Kaminski, Kirkpatrick and Bshouty, J. Algorithms
1988; the textbook of Buergisser, Clausen and Shokrollahi (1997), chapter 13; Bostan, Lecerf and Schost 2003.

**Lemma 1: T(N) <= max over q of c(q) <= 2 T(N)** (c(q) = least gates for the XOR-convolution with q). Right side:
transform, scale, transform back. Left side: H is one such convolution between free scalings. Holds; it does not even use
transposition. **"k even" is not needed over the Gaussian numbers** [ran, k = 1 to 8, all entries]:
H(x, y) = i^|x| (-i)^|x + y| i^|y|, with |x| the number of ones. Over the rationals with k odd, use k - 1 and one textbook
level. [recalled] Known in other clothes: Bluestein 1970 for the cyclic transform; the relation (phase gate x Hadamard)^3 =
a constant of quantum circuits. An equivalence, so it predicts nothing about linear size.

**Lemma 2: T(N)/2 <= c*(N) <= sqrt(N) T(sqrt N) + N - sqrt(N)** (c* = least cost of a V with V H' V^T diagonal). Left: H' =
N V^T D^-1 V and the rule above (V is invertible, so the rule applies). Right: V = (sum and difference on exchanged
pairs)(half transform). [ran] V H' V^T is diagonal with values N, 2N, -2N at 4, 16, 64 and 256 points (w-cross ran to 64);
H' = N V^T D^-1 V exactly at 4, 16, 64; pair gates 2, 12, 56, 240 = N - sqrt N. Holds.

## 6. Adding-only neighbour sums: exact

**Claim recalled by w-cross:** circuits that only add need of the order of k N gates for neighbour sums on the cube.
**Exact statement** [derived]. With gates u + v only, the least number is **N (k - 1 - floor(k/2)/2)**: (3/4) k N - N for
even k, (3/4)(k - 1) N for odd k. Proof. No cancellation, so every wire that reaches an output is a set of inputs inside
that output's k neighbours, and each gate joins two disjoint sets. Unfold one output into a tree: k leaves, k - 1 different
gates. Three addresses have at most one common neighbour, so a gate on 3 or more inputs serves one output only; two
addresses have at most two, so a gate on 2 inputs serves at most two outputs; a tree holds at most floor(k/2) gates on 2
inputs. Charge each gate to the outputs it serves: at least k - 1 - floor(k/2)/2 per output.
[ran, `z-2/nbr.py`, `nbr.out`] The pairing program meets it exactly for k = 2 to 10 (2, 12, 32, 96, 224, 576, 1280, 3072,
6656 gates); common neighbours of two addresses: at most 2, of three: at most 1 (checked to 64 points).
At 8 points the bound, 12, equals the least count with ANY coefficients modulo 2, 3, 5 [read: d-0].
[recalled] Known as a type: Nechiporuk 1969, Mehlhorn 1979, Pippenger 1980, Wegener 1980 for matrices without large blocks
of ones; survey by Jukna and Sergeev 2013. And the known warning: Find, Goos, Jarvisalo, Kaski, Koivisto, Korhonen 2013
(arXiv 1304.0513) give matrices where circuits that subtract are far smaller than circuits that only add. So this bound is
no evidence about neighbour sums with subtraction, which is w-cross's open question; it only says a cheaper program must cancel.
