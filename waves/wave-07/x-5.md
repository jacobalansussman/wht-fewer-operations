**No circuit below a known count and no growth law below N log N. Nothing here changes the order.**

Words. N = 2^k points; a point is a set S of coordinates. *Subset sums* Z: y_S = sum of x_T over T inside S. *Yates*: (N/2) k gates.
U: "add one element", (U x)_S = sum of x_(S-i) over i in S; Z = exp(U). A *string*: vectors v, Uv, U^2 v/2, ... until zero; its
length is d + 1. A *Jordan basis*: a basis made of strings; there are C(k,r) - C(k,r-1) strings with d = k - 2r. B: the change
from the standard basis to a Jordan basis; then Z = B^-1 (Pascal blocks) B. *Coupling*: joining two groups of coordinates whose
strings are known into the strings of the union (a block matrix, one block per pair of strings and per set size, the *weight
block*). *Tower*: coupling one coordinate at a time. E[d]: the average of d over the N points (each point sits in one string).

## 2. B, built and counted (part ii) [ran, `bcost.py`, `bcost.out`]

Two Jordan bases, integer vectors, columns of B^-1:
- *web*: matched pairs (bracket matching) carry e_a - e_b, the d unmatched coordinates carry the sum of all j-subsets (d-0, i-cross).
- *tower*: from a string L_0..L_d on k-1 coordinates, the new coordinate gives a long string L'_j = L_j (x) e0 + L_(j-1) (x) e1
  and a short one F_(j-1) = j L_j (x) e0 - (d-j+1) L_(j-1) (x) e1. Up to scalings it is the only basis adapted to the tower.
Both pass: Z times column (string, j) = sum over m >= j of C(m, j) column (string, m), and rank N, for k = 2..8.

beat Yates only if b(m) < m/4 for some m. The tower has b(m)/m = 0.25, 0.33, 0.41, 0.45, 0.49, 0.52, 0.54 and rising to 1.

## 3. The identity, and why the bracketing cannot matter [derived; ran in `ident.py`, `ident.out`]

Couple a string of length a + 1 with one of length b + 1. The block of set size w has m_w = min(w, a, b, a + b - w) + 1 vectors
on each side; price it dense, m_w (m_w - 1) gates (exact for m = 2: a full 2 x 2 block is 2 gates).
**Claim.** With f(d) = (d+1) d (d-1)/3: the price of the coupling is the sum of f over the strings produced minus
(b+1) f(a) minus (a+1) f(b). So the total price of any tree of couplings is the sum of f over the final strings.
Why [derived]: for b = 1 the price is 2a and f(a+1) + f(a-1) - 2 f(a) = 2a. The general pair formula I did not prove by hand;
[ran, `pairid.py`] it holds for all 6,561 pairs with a, b from 0 to 80, and the totals below agree. Then: sum of f = (N/3) E[d(d-1)], and E[d(d+2)] = 3k (the trace of
the total spin squared, 3/4 per coordinate), so the total is **N (k - E[d])**.

| k | tower | balanced tree | lopsided tree (1/3 : 2/3) | sum of f | N (k - E[d]) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 26 | 26 | 26 | 26 | 26 |
| 8 | 1114 | 1114 | 1114 | 1114 | 1114 |
| 16 | 689402 | 689402 | 689402 | 689402 | 689402 |
| 24 | 286926756 | 286926756 | 286926756 | 286926756 | 286926756 |

(also k = 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 12: all equal.) i-cross wrote "a cheap B cannot be a tree of couplings" from the first level alone
(k/4 per point). This is the full count: no bracketing is better than any other, and the count is k minus a square root.
Limit: the price m(m-1) is an upper price for blocks of size 3 and more. A coupling still moves nearly every point, and with
the recursion t(k) = 2 t(k/2) + c(k) for two halves, c(k) >= about 1 gives order k whatever the blocks cost [derived, rough].

**The tower is a butterfly network.** Pass i pairs the vector (string, j, new coordinate 0) with (string, j-1, new coordinate 1)
and applies [[1, 1], [j, -(d-j+1)]]. That is one 2 x 2 kernel per pair of points per coordinate, as in Yates and the textbook,
on another pairing and with a kernel that depends on the block. So "is the large coefficient in the same place where Yates
spends its k/2": yes, literally. Yates spends half a gate per point per coordinate with coefficient 1; the tower spends
about one gate per point per coordinate with a coefficient of size about sqrt(k).

The budget per gate grows like (1/4) log2 k, the total like (k/4) log2 k per point. So B has growing coefficients and far more
volume than the transform needs, and still k passes. Growing coefficients alone are not the missing thing; s-cross fact E asked
for 0.277 sqrt(k)/C per gate and the tower supplies more than that already at k = 8.

## 4. Going around, three ways [derived, none run beyond what is said]

**4.1 B is half of the transposed subset sums, then a projection.** In an orthogonal Jordan basis the coordinate of x along
(head h on sets of size r, depth j) is the head h applied to "the sum of x_T over the T of size r + j that contain R", R of size
r. Those sums for all R, r, j are exactly the pieces of Z-transpose, kept apart by size. So B = (projections onto heads) after
(the pieces of Z^T): the basis change contains the transform it was meant to avoid. Yes, and: only about sqrt(k) values of r
carry almost all points, so "one pass of O(N) per value of r" would give N sqrt(k). The pass for r = 0 and r = 1 is linear
(all level sums, then the sums over the sets of each size containing one given element, by splitting the coordinates in two
halves and turning the circuit around: about 2N gates). For r of order k/2 the same splitting is Z on each half again. It stops there.

**4.2 Add many coordinates at once to a long string.** For d >= m, a string of length d + 1 times m raw coordinates splits
into 2^m strings, one per up/down path. Each weight block is 2^m x 2^m and is a product of m of the 2 x 2 kernels above with
slowly varying ratios: a tensor power of a 2 x 2 kernel, slightly non-uniform. So B is about sqrt(k) such transforms of size
2^sqrt(k) per point. If kernels of that kind had linear circuits, B would cost N sqrt(k); with what is known it is k per point
again. This is "B is the transform again" with a number on it, and the reverse of the brief's hope: B is not below the
transform, it is made of transforms.

**4.3 Do not change back.** With an orthogonal Jordan basis, B commutes with the diagonal D of H = Z D Z^T up to a diagonal, so
H = B^-1 (Krawtchouk blocks) B^-T: two uses of B, not four (i-cross has this as H = (-2)^pairs times Krawtchouk blocks).
Yes, and: nothing in that argument needs strings. It needs only a basis in which the form x^T H y is diagonal. Section 5.

## 5. By-product 1: the transform as a quadratic form, and the family of all its diagonalisations

[derived] Let the rows of V be any basis with V H V^T = D diagonal. Then H^-1 = V^T D^-1 V, and H^-1 = H/N, so
**H = N V^T D^-1 V and T(N) <= 2 cost(V)** (a circuit read backwards computes the transpose at the same cost for a square matrix
[recalled: the transposition rule]). Members: V = Moebius, the inverse of Z (cost (N/2) k, diagonal (-2)^|S|: the textbook)
[ran, `ident.out` part 5, k = 1..6]; V = an orthonormal eigenbasis of H (g-0's mirror); V = Pascal^-1 B. The family has
N(N+1)/2 free parameters; a search of tonight's reports for this form found no member priced that is neither a product of
2 x 2 kernels nor an eigenbasis (I did not read every report).
[derived] *Covering count.* H/N has no zero entry, so every pair of points must lie together in the support of some row:
the sum over rows of s(s-1)/2 is at least N(N-1)/2 (s = size of the row's support). Moebius: 49 against 28 at 8 points. The
count is weak (one full row satisfies it) but it kills "all rows of bounded support".
[ran, `peel.py`, exact clique search over vectors with entries 0, +1, -1; `peel_a.out`, `peel_b.out`] Largest family of pairwise
H-orthogonal, non-isotropic vectors:

| points | support at most 2 | at most 3 | at most 4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 3 | | 4 (a full basis) |
| 8 | 4 | 4 | **8 (a full basis)**, supports 2, 2, 4, 4, 4, 4, 4, 4 |
| 16 | 5 | | not run |
| 32 | at least 6 (time limit) | | |

Growth law of the first column: k + 1 (e_0 and e_0 - e_i: the first rows of Moebius). Sparse lines cannot be peeled off in bulk.
The full basis at 8 points with supports at most 4 (`v8_sparse4.txt`, diagonal 4, 4, -8, -8, 8, 8, -8, -8, checked) is not a
product of 2 x 2 kernels. Its exact price [ran, mincirc]: no circuit with 12 gates or fewer modulo 5 (exhaustive for 0 to 4
helpers; 5 helpers ran out of time) and none with 12 modulo 7 (exhaustive, 4 helpers allowed). So this member costs at least 13
modulo 5 and modulo 7, more than Moebius's 12: no gain. It is one member; the clique search returns one largest family, not all.
A trap I walked into and out of [derived]: a V that calls a half-size transform (x^T H x = p^T (H' q) for suitable p, q) gives
T(N) <= 2 T(N/2) + O(N), the textbook, because V is used twice. A linear V must be linear outright.

## 6. By-product 2: one step up the cube

[derived; ran as U Z = L Z - Z L for k = 1..8, `ident.out` part 1] Since [L, U] = U, Z L Z^-1 = L - U. So
**U = L - Z L Z^-1: cost(U) <= 2 cost(Z) + N** (Z^-1 is Z between two diagonals of signs). In the same way the cube's
neighbour sum A (each vertex gets the sum of its k neighbours) is H L' H / N with L' the diagonal of k - 2|S| [ran, k = 1..8]:
**cost(A) <= 2 T(N)**. So: T(N) = O(N) forces Z = O(N) [read: g-4] and that forces U and A into O(N) gates. The converse
fails as far as I can see: Z = exp(U) needs k steps.
What is known about U: its matrix has (N/2) k ones and no 2 x 2 block of ones (two sets share at most one set just below both),
so without cancellation it needs (N/2) k - N + 1 gates [recalled, not checked]. With cancellation nothing is known. U splits
by set size into the steps W_r (sets of size r to size r + 1; each output is a sum of r + 1 inputs; naive r gates per output,
trivial floor 1). [derived, from the transposition rule] c(r) = c(k-r-1) + C(k,r) - C(k,r+1) for c(r) = cost(W_r), and so
**W_1 costs exactly C(k,2) and W_(k-2) exactly k(k-2): the naive count, with any coefficients.** At k = 4: 6 and 8.
The first open case: W_2 at k = 5 (the 10 triangle sums of the 10 edges of the complete graph on 5 points): between 10 and 20.
The shortest relation among the outputs that I know has 8 terms (the eight faces of an octahedron with alternating signs; there is
none on 4 or 5 points, where the triangle sums are independent) [derived for 4 and 5 points; guess that 8 is least].
This is the nearest problem I could find that is necessary for a linear transform and still open at a size a search can exhaust.

## 7. What it became

The brief's question has an answer: gates(B)/N grows like k for every tree of couplings, by an identity, and the large
coefficients sit exactly on Yates's butterflies. What is left of "the price of B" is two questions that do not mention B:
(a) the cheapest basis orthogonal for x^T H y, a family in which the textbook, the mirror and the Jordan route are single points;
(b) the cost of one level step W_r with cancellation, which a linear transform would have to make O(1) per output.

## 8. Criticism, last

- The identity's general pair formula is checked by machine for a, b up to 80 and through the totals of three tree shapes, not
  proved; the tower case (b = 1) is a two-line proof. The dense price m(m-1) is an upper price for m >= 3, so "every tree costs the same" is a statement about
  that price, not about minimum circuits; the lower end of the class (order k) is a rough count.
- "The only basis adapted to the tower" and "only about two basis vectors can be standard" are for bases that respect set sizes.
  A Jordan basis that mixes sizes is allowed and was not explored. That is a real hole.
- Section 4.2 is an argument about large d, not a computation.
- T(N) <= 2 cost(V) uses the transposition rule from memory, in count (G). The 8-point sparse basis was priced modulo 5 and 7
  only, and "none modulo p" is not "none".
- Section 6 assumes nothing about direct sums for its exact values (they are for single levels), but "U costs 17 at 16 points"
  would need levels to add, which is not proved; I do not claim it. The cancellation-free count is recalled.
- The peel table is for entries 0, +1, -1 only. Other coefficients were not tried.
- I spent 400 seconds on one mincirc run that timed out. Constants at one size were not the task; the run was a test of whether
  a non-product member of the family undercuts Moebius, and it did not.
- Nothing here changes the order, or any constant.
