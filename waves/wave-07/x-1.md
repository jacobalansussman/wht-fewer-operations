nothing published. Marks: [ran] = exact run of mine tonight, whole numbers, every circuit replayed on all N x N entries (files in
`x-1-digits/`); [ran, exhaustive mod p] = `d-0-zeta/mincirc`, exhaustive over GF(p), so "none" means none modulo p only;
[derived] = by hand tonight, checked by nobody; [read] = from tonight's reports; [guess].
Gate count everywhere: **(G)**, a gate is any a*u + b*v, scalings free. Count (O), OpenAI's, is not used (section 5 says where
that matters).

**No circuit below a known count for the transform or for subset sums, and no growth law below N log N. Nothing here changes the
order.** What is new: the exact cost law of a digit by the known route, with replayed circuits; a reformulation of "the first d
digits" as a problem where no known wall applies; one mechanism that makes a helper wire serve N outputs (on points in a row),
and the exact reason the cube refuses it.

Words. N = 2^k points; an *address* is k bits; |x| is the number of ones of x (its *weight*); a *light* address has small weight.
*The transform* H: entry (y, x) is (-1)^m, m = the number of common ones of y and x. *Digit expansion*: H = sum over j of
(-2)^j B_j, B_j(y, x) = the binomial number C(m, j). *Level j*: the sets U of j address bits. z_U = the sum of the inputs x that
contain U (a *superset sum*); H = Z D Z^T says: output y = sum over U inside y of (-2)^|U| z_U. *Window [lo, hi]*: the part of
that sum with lo <= |U| <= hi. *Reading side* ("in"): inputs to the z_U. *Writing side* ("out"): the z_U to the outputs.
*Yates*: the usual one-bit-at-a-time method for either side. *Trimmed*: Yates with every intermediate value dropped that no
level of the window can use. *Helper*: a wire that is not an output.

## 1. Facts [derived unless marked]

- (i) and (ii): `slices.out` part A [ran].
- **Digits are canonical.** Z has determinant 1, so H = Z D Z^T is the Smith normal form of H: the elementary divisors are 2^j,
  C(k, j) times. The list of divisors is forced; the level-j slices are the natural choice of the two sides (the sides of a
  Smith form are not unique, so other slicings with the same divisors exist; I looked at none).
- **Digits are powers of (s - 1), s the cross-ratio.** Every 2 x 2 kernel [[a, b], [c, d]] without zero entry is, up to free
  scalings of rows and columns, [[1, 1], [1, s]] with s = ad/(bc). Its k-th Kronecker power T_s has entry s^m, and
  T_s = sum over j of (s - 1)^j B_j with the SAME B_j. H is s = -1, the all-ones matrix is s = 1, subset sums (as "disjoint
  from") is s = 0. So the digit expansion is the Taylor expansion of the whole class at its rank-one member, and the circuits
  of section 2 are identities of the whole class: with (s - 1)^|U| on the diagonal they give T_s modulo (s - 1)^(d+1)
  [ran, `classcheck.py`: s = 4, -2, 3, 0, 7, every d, 8 to 64 points, the same gate counts].
  **s-cross's guess that the modulo-4 object is the first thing special to the prime 2 is wrong**: it is the first-order term
  at s = 1 for every kernel. What is special at s = -1 is only that the entries stay of size 1.
- **Three kernels on a line**: H_2 = 2 x [[1, 1], [1, 0]] - [[1, 1], [1, 1]]. That is where every coefficient-2 gate of the
  records comes from (one unit of volume each, s-cross hybrid 3).
- Row y of B_j vanishes for j > |y| (C(m, j) = 0 when m <= |y| < j). Same for columns. Line 5 above.
- **Subset sums are an exponential.** Let L be the matrix "add one element": L(y, x) = 1 when y is x plus one more bit.
  Then Z = exp(L), and the part of Z that adds exactly j elements is L^j / j!. With L^T (remove one element) and the diagonal
  these are the three generators of the rotation group of a spin, and H = exp(L) D exp(L^T) is the standard way to write one
  rotation as lower-triangular x diagonal x upper-triangular. Consequence: the first d digits see the input only through its
  parts of total spin at least k/2 - d, a space of dimension at most (k + 1) C(k, d), far below N for d well under k/2
  (the map to the z_U of the first d levels commutes with permuting the bits, and its target only contains those parts). This is d-7's symmetry basis
  seen from the digits; it explains why the low digits have tiny rank and says nothing about their cost.

## 3. From the top and from the bottom [ran, `slices.out` part C; derived]

Top window [j, k] (replayed: equal to H minus the bottom window [0, j-1]): it reads only the inputs of weight >= j and touches
only the outputs of weight >= j. Gates = 2k x (number of (k-1)-bit strings of weight >= j): at 256 points 0, 16, 128, 464, 1024
for j = 8, 7, 6, 5, 4; at 128 points 0, 14, 98, 308.
- Below N in all as soon as fewer than one string in k has weight >= j: j >= k/2 + sqrt((k/2) ln k) is enough [derived,
  Hoeffding's bound].
- Where the two ends meet: nowhere useful. Bottom [0, j-1] plus top [j, k] plus one addition per heavy output is more than the
  whole at every j (at 256 points, j = 5: 1861 + 464 + 93 against 2048), because Yates' wires serve both.
- So the sum over j of c_j is N log2 N with the profile "2N, 2N, ..., 2N, then a fall of width sqrt(k) around k/2, then 0".
  It does not fall from both ends. The low digits are dense (B_j has N^2 x P(at least j common ones) non-zero entries) and
  of tiny rank C(k, j); the high digits are sparse. Dense-and-low-rank is the expensive kind here, which is the surprise.

## 4. What the bottom digits are, and the floors [derived; small cases ran]

- **Reformulation.** Reading side of the first d digits: N inputs, V = sum over j <= d of C(k, j) outputs z_U (the moments of
  degree <= d of the input, seen as a function on the cube). By a triangular change of V coordinates it is the transform at the
  outputs of weight <= d. Writing side = the same matrix transposed: from V coefficients, the values of a polynomial of degree
  <= d at all N points of the cube.
- **Floors.** Writing side: N - 1 (every value except the one at 0 is a new wire). Reading side: N - 1 + N - V by the
  transposition principle (a circuit and its transpose differ by inputs minus outputs). For d = 1 both are met exactly:
  N - 1 and 2N - k - 2, so 3N - k - 3 is optimal for the route through the k + 1 sums.
  [ran, exhaustive mod p, `e2_k3.out`, `in_k3.out`] 8 points, d = 2: writing side 11 (none with 10 modulo 2, 3, 5, 7; found
  with 11), reading side 12 (none with 11 modulo 2, 3, 5; found with 12 modulo 2, 3): the method's numbers, and 12 = 11 + 8 - 7
  as transposition says. [ran, `e2_k4_p2.out`, `e2_k4_p3_clean.out`] 16 points, d = 2, writing side: none with 19 gates
  modulo 2 or modulo 3; the method has 26; 20 to 25 not decided (the search with 5 helpers modulo 2 was stopped unfinished after 400 s here and after
  1,100 s on the Linux machine; it prints only at the end, so it says nothing).
- **The volume count is silent here.** For V rows of H Hadamard's bound gives minors of at most N^(V/2), so the count asks for
  at most (V/2) log2 N gates. With d = k/2 - c sqrt(k ln k) one has V < N / k^(2 c^2), so the demand is o(N) for c > 0.71.
  Nearly half of the digits, in other words every digit the route pays 2N for except a band of width sqrt(k ln k), lies where
  no known wall says anything, and the method costs (2d + 1) N there against a floor of 3N.
- What this does NOT give: the lightest rows are a vanishing share of all rows (a typical row has weight k/2 +- sqrt(k), and is
  typical on every block of bits as well). Covering all rows by balls of radius d around centres needs k^(2c^2) centres, one
  free sign twist of the inputs each, so O(N) for the lightest rows would give N x polylog, not N. It is a necessary door, the
  nearest problem that might be solvable, not a route by itself.

## 5. Play: one helper for many outputs [ran, `conic.py`, `conic.out`; derived]

Smallest case of "a polynomial of degree 2 at many points": p(t) = a + b t + c t^2 at t = 0..N-1. Finite differences: 2(N - 1).
- **N + 1 gates** [ran, exact, N = 4 to 4096]: two helpers h_s = b + s c for s = N - 1 and s = N; then
  p(s - n) = p(n) + (s - 2n) h_s. The walk 0, N-1, 1, N-2, ... reaches every point with one gate each. The same helper is used
  N/2 times with N/2 different coefficients, up to N - 1. In count (O) the multiplications are charged and nothing is gained.
- Why it works: write ev_t for "the value at t" as a vector over (a, b, c), that is (1, t, t^2). Then ev_t - ev_t' is a multiple
  of ONE fixed vector, (0, 1, t + t'), for all pairs with the same sum t + t'. In pictures: the outputs lie on a conic, and a
  point off a conic lies on many of its chords. [derived, not run] On a grid with n points per side the same trick gives about
  N (1 + 2/n) for degree 2; with 2 points per side, the cube, it gives the 2N of finite differences and nothing more.
- [ran, `rowdeg.py`, exact fractions, replayed] Higher degree on points in a row: p(s - n) - p(n) = (s - 2n) x (a polynomial
  in the SQUARE of the distance to the centre, of degree (d - 1)/2 rounded down). At 256 points: degree 3 and 4 in 514 and 518
  gates (finite differences 765 and 1020), degree 5 and 6 in 777 and 783, degree 8 in 1052 (2040). So about (d/2 + 1) N
  against d N: the trick halves the number of passes, no more.
- On the cube the same reflection exists (x and its complement are symmetric about the centre), and it gives nothing: the
  square of the distance to the centre is the same for every point, so the "polynomial in the square" keeps its full degree
  [derived: the even part of a degree-2 polynomial on the half-cube is again a general degree-2 polynomial in k - 1 variables;
  total 2.5 N against 2N].
- **The cube.** [derived] A helper h serves two gates "output = a x output + b x h" only if four (or three) outputs are
  linearly dependent. Evaluations of polynomials of degree <= d at fewer than 2^(d+1) cube points are always independent (the
  smallest dependency is the alternating sum over a subcube of dimension d + 1). So for d >= 2 every such helper serves ONE
  gate. [ran, k <= 5] all 6, 28, 120, 496 pairs of cube points have different helper directions for degree 2. For d = 1 the
  helpers are the k directions, N/2 pairs each: that is why the first digit is cheap. For H itself the outputs are independent
  and the same holds.
- **Sketch of a floor** [derived, 15 minutes old, constants not worked out]. Outputs made from two helpers: draw the helpers
  as vertices and such an output as an edge. Any e < 2^(d+1) edges span at least e dimensions, so every small subgraph has at
  most as many edges as vertices; a graph on s vertices with that property up to size 2^(d+1) has at most
  s (1 + O(log s / 2^d)) edges. With the previous point: N - 1 <= s + s (1 + O(k / 2^d)). So once 2^d is well above k the
  writing side needs about N/2 helpers: **at least about 1.5 N gates, with any coefficients.** Only a constant; it says the
  second pass over the points cannot be avoided, not that d passes are needed.
- What it means for the idea. The digit ladder is expensive on the cube for a geometric reason: two points per line. Free
  scalings (the one thing count (G) and the volume count leave open) can only bite where three outputs are dependent "for
  free". On the cube the first such dependency has 2^(d+1) terms and a gate has two inputs.

## 6. Keep asking

- *Does any wire hold two digits that are both used later?* Yes, nearly all do [derived from the circuit]. On the reading
  side the wire after i steps with a prefix of weight t and a remaining address x' feeds every level from t to t + |x'|; an
  input x feeds |x| + 1 levels. The wires are not the cost.
- *What stops a wire from holding three?* Nothing. What cannot be shared is the last gate of each z_U and, on the writing side,
  the helper of each output (section 5). The cost of digit j is one gate for every (step, prefix of weight exactly j,
  remaining address): P(Bin >= j + 1) per point, and the mirror image on the way out.
- *Can the number of layers be k/d?* Processing d bits at a time needs a 2^d-point kernel of linear size: the question itself.
- *Lifting that doubles the digits?* [guess] An entry-wise step such as t -> (3t - t^3)/2 doubles the correct digits of each
  entry, but the entry-wise cube of the window [0, d] IS a window of three times the depth: nothing is saved. An operator
  step would cost two or three applications per doubling: N log N again at best. Not run.

## 8. What to try next

1. **The lightest rows.** Is there a circuit of O(N) gates for the transform at the outputs of weight <= d, uniformly in d up
   to k/2 - sqrt(k ln k)? Volume allows it; the floor is 2N; the method is (d + 1) N. Smallest undecided case: d = 2, reading
   side 3N or 2N + o(N)? At 8 points it is exactly the method's 12. Next: 16 and 32 points with a search that allows large
   coefficients (mincirc only reaches 16 points modulo 2 and 3, where free scalings are nearly absent). By section 5 a saving
   must come from outputs made of two helpers.
1b. **The same question as a puzzle a person can hold in the head** [derived]. Take an n x n x n array. (a) Reading: all its
   line sums in the three axis directions, 3n^2 outputs. Each family alone costs n^3; is the cost of the three together
   3n^3 or 2n^3 + o(n^3)? (b) Writing (the transpose): from numbers u_ij, v_ik, w_jk make all n^3 sums u_ij + v_ik + w_jk;
   2n^3 gates is obvious; is n^3 + o(n^3) possible? Floors: 2n^3 - 3n^2 and n^3. With the address bits cut in three thirds and
   n = N^(1/3), a "yes" to (a) gives the third digit's reading side in 2N + o(N) instead of 3N (every pair of bits lies inside two
   of the three thirds taken together; the rest is transforms on N^(2/3) points). By section 5 a "yes" needs outputs made of two helpers
   whose graph has cycles running through whole 2 x 2 x 2 sub-arrays, the only short dependencies there are. I could not build
   one and could not exclude it. The obvious design fails by a count [derived, quick]: take as tree the outputs with an index
   0 and let every other output close the 8-cycle of its sub-array with corner 000; on one side of the common edge 000 the
   labels must be nested (000, i00, ij0), so a cycle has at most 5 tree edges where 7 are needed. [ran, exhaustive mod p,
   `tri_n2.out`] Smallest case of (b), n = 2 (12 inputs, 8 outputs, 16 gates obvious): none with 13 gates modulo 2, none with
   12 modulo 3; 14 and 15 not decided (the search with 6 helpers did not finish in 170 s). An annealer at n = 3, 4 (54 and 128
   gates to beat for (b)) with large coefficients allowed would say more.
2. **Where are three points on a line?** In the weight coordinate. On a block of b bits the functions that depend only on
   the weight live on b + 1 points in a row, and there digit j of the transform has RANK ONE:
   K(w, v) = sum over j of (-2)^j C(w, j) C(b - j, v - j) [derived]. This is d-7's symmetry basis; its middle (the Kronecker
   power of small Krawtchouk blocks) is a transform on a grid with lines of b + 1 points, where section 5's trick has room once
   the blocks are large and the digit low. d-7's wall, the cost k per entry of the change of basis, is untouched: the cube has
   two points per line, the symmetric basis has long lines, and passing from one to the other is what costs.
3. **A real floor from section 5.** Write out the graph count; then ask what replaces "two helpers" at depth: a gate tree of
   depth t over helpers reaches 2^t terms, and the first dependency has 2^(d+1). If each level of that tree can be charged,
   the result is a floor that grows with d for the writing side, with any coefficients. I do not know that it can.
4. **The other expansion points.** T_s = sum over j of s^j A_j at s = 0, with A_j(y, x) = 1 where exactly j ones are common.
   No small number in it, but the A_j are 0/1 and H = sum of (-1)^j A_j: price one A_j.

## 9. Criticism, last

- The circuits of section 2 are Yates' method cut off. Their only news is the law c_j and that the cut-off circuit is cheaper
  than u-9's (constants). Everything adds up to N log2 N exactly, the night's usual tie.
- Section 4's "silent" is a statement about ONE wall (volume, bounded coefficients). Nobody has a floor above 3N for any
  explicit linear map with any coefficients, so "no wall" is the normal state of affairs, not a sign that O(N) is there.
- Section 5's floor sketch uses a graph lemma I did not prove (few edges beyond the vertices when two cycles cannot be close);
  the constant 1.5 could be wrong. The one-helper lemma is solid for gates of the kind output + helper only.
- The N + 1 circuit lives in count (G) alone and on points in a row; it is not a statement about the transform.
- Exhaustive searches are modulo small primes at 8 points and modulo 2 and 3 at 16 points up to 19 gates only. "None modulo p"
  is not "none". Literature: one web search on the cost of a low-degree polynomial at all points of the cube found nothing on
  target; "moments of degree 2 on the cube: 3N or less" may well be known. I did not read the wave-6 skeptics (not landed).
