**No circuit below a known count and no growth law below N log N. Nothing here changes the order.**

Words. N = 2^k points, k even. Q(x) = x1 x2 + x3 x4 + ...; D = the diagonal of the signs (-1)^Q; H = the transform with the two
bits of each pair exchanged in the output address; R = D H / sqrt(N), so R^3 = 1. *Mirror* (short, in this report, for "a reflection
that can be half of a pair"): a matrix S with S^2 = 1 and S R S = R^-1. *Pair*: (S, S R); their product is R. *Triple*: S, S R, S R^2,
the three mirrors that belong together. T(N) = the least number of gates for H (unknown; 7 at 4 points, at most 52 and 304 at 16
and 64 points tonight). *Free mirror*: a mirror with one non-zero per row (cost 0). *Stabilizer mirror*: a mirror that sends
every matrix X^a Z^b (shift the address by a, change sign by the character b) to another such matrix up to sign; D, H and
everything built from them, relabellings and quadratic signs are of this kind. c(j) = T(2^j) / 2^j, the cost per point.

## 1. The starting facts, and three more [ran: `n16.py`, `n16.out`, `fused.py`, `fused.out`; fractions]

  mirror again (checked on all 192 + 348 + 2,208 mirrors of section 3 and on every explicit one below).
- [derived] General form: S = D g with g commuting with R and D g D = g^-1; then S R = g^-1 H / sqrt N. **So a pair is the
  factorisation H = g (g^-1 H) with a special g, and nothing more.** This sentence is the whole of section 2.
- Size of the family [ran]: dimension of {X : X R = R^-1 X} is 6 = 2^2 + 2 x 1^2 at 4 points and 86 = 6^2 + 2 x 5^2 at 16 points.
- **One mirror, turned** [ran]: S R = R S R^-1 and S R^2 = R^2 S R^-2. The second and third mirror are the first one carried
  by the rotation. All three are similar matrices; nothing forces their costs to be related (0, T, T for the trivial triple).
- **Sum rule** [ran]: S + S R + S R^2 = 3 S P0 with P0 = (1 + R + R^2)/3, rank (N+2)/3. On the fixed space the three mirrors are
  equal; off it they add up to zero, like the three mirror lines of a triangle.
- **A circle of mirrors through every triple** [ran exact at 4, 16, 64: `circle.py`]: a S + b S R + c S R^2 is again a mirror when
  a + b + c = 1 and a^2 + b^2 + c^2 - ab - bc - ca = 1, for instance (2 D + 2 H/sqrt N - D H D/sqrt N)/3. These are the
  only mirrors outside the stabilizer world that I can write down in closed form, and each is a mixture of three known ones.
- **Product and sum at once** [derived from the sum rule; the row weights ran]: in the rows-then-columns triple below, the
  full third mirror, which is the whole transform between free maps, equals -(rows pass) - (columns pass) + 3 S1 P0. So the
  transform is the two half passes MULTIPLIED and also the two half passes ADDED plus a matrix of rank (N+2)/3: in the sum
  the supports do not multiply (2 sqrt N - 1 non-zeros per row), and all the density sits in the projection P0.
- **A mirror may be chosen freely on the fixed space of R**, the same choice for both members of the pair [derived, checked].
  Choosing +1 there gives the smallest mirrors: S1 = 1 - 2 P1, S2 = 1 - 2 P2 with rank P1 = rank P2 = (N-1)/3 (1, 5, 21), and
  R = 1 - 2 P1 - 2 P2 + 4 P1 P2, rank(R - 1) = 2(N-1)/3 (2, 10, 42) [ran]. With S1 built from D: P1 lives on the points with

  rank-one matrix on six points]; P2 = R P1 R^-1 is full.
- The explicit pair with both mirrors of row weight sqrt N at 16 points [ran]: S1 = D (x) (D H D / 2), S2 = (H / 2) (x) D on the

## 2. The lemma that answers the growth-law question [derived]

> For every pair, cost(S1) + cost(S2) >= cost(R) = T(N), and the pair (D, H / sqrt N) has cost 0 + T(N).
> So min over pairs of cost(S1) + cost(S2) = T(N) exactly, for every N.

Proof: run the circuit of S2, then that of S1 on its outputs; signs, scalings and the exchange of bits are free in count (G).
Consequences. (a) The table the brief wants is the table of T(N): 7, at most 52, at most 304 at 4, 16, 64 points, against
N log2 N = 8, 64, 384; shape of the best pair: one side free, the other side everything. (b) A pair with two cheap mirrors
whose sum is below the best KNOWN circuit would still be a discovery, so pairs are a search space. Sections 3 and 4 look into
it at the sizes one can run. (c) The honest number to tabulate is the best pair WITHOUT a free mirror:

| points | N log2 N | best known circuit = best pair | best pair with no free mirror | what it is |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 8 | 7 (exact) | 10 over GF(5) and GF(7), 11 over GF(13) [ran, exhaustive in those fields] | 3 + 7 or 4 + 6 |
| 16 | 64 | 52 [read] | at most 56 [derived from the 7-gate brick; not searched] | two layers of four bricks |
| 64 | 384 | 304 [read] | at most 320 = 16 x 7 + 4 x 52 [derived; not searched] | 16 bricks and four 16-point circuits |

## 4. Stabilizer mirrors: all of them at 16 points, a sample at 64 [ran: `cliff.py`, `cliff.out`]

Method. A stabilizer matrix is described by a 2k x 2k matrix of bits (what it does to the labels (a, b) of X^a Z^b). I computed
the bit matrices of R and D from the actual integer matrices at 4 and 16 points (R has order 3 there and fixes no label).
The bit matrices commuting with R were generated and counted: 18 at 4 points, 77,760 at 16 points, the orders of the unitary
groups of dimension 2 and 4 over the field with four elements, so the list is complete. Mirrors = the products (bit matrix of
D) x (one of them) that are involutions. For each, j = rank of the shift parts of the images of the sign labels; the matrix then
has 2^j non-zeros per row and is N / 2^j copies of the 2^j-point transform between free maps [recalled: the standard normal
form of such matrices; checked here only on the explicit pairs of section 1: weights 1, 4, 16, 64 as predicted].

| points | mirrors | triples (j of S, of S R, of S R^2), sorted: count |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 12 | (0,2,2): 12 |
| 16 | 1,728 | (0,4,4): 228; (2,2,4): 960; (2,4,4): 540 |
| 64 | 28,708 sampled | (0,6,6): 462; (2,4,6): 16,415; (2,6,6): 5,676; (4,4,4): 3,287; (6,6,6): 2,868 |

(The 64-point sample is 40,000 random conjugates of D by products of generators; it is not the full list.)
Regularities.
- **j1 + j2 >= k for every pair, with no exception** (0 violations in each census): the support rule (iii).
- **Every j is even** [ran: exhaustive at 4 and 16 points, the sample at 64]. Likely reason [guess, not proved]: two transforms
  with a quadratic sign between them collapse to a transform on r bits, r = the rank of an alternating form, which is even
  [recalled: the transform of a quadratic sign vector lives on a subspace of that dimension]; I did not show that every
  mirror is of that shape. So a stabilizer mirror transforms whole PAIRS of bits: there is no mirror of weight 2 at 4 points
  and none of weight 8 in the 64-point sample, and "sqrt N on both sides" exists only when k/2 is even.
- Three mirrors of weight sqrt N at once, (2,2,2) at 16 points, do not exist: the third is always full.
- Triples such as (2,4,4), (4,4,4), (6,6,6) are pure waste: two or three full or nearly full transforms whose product is one.
  (6,6,6) is the identity H D H = sqrt N D H D read as "transform, signs, transform = one transform".

**The law** [derived from the census and the normal form]: a stabilizer pair costs at most N (c(j1) + c(j2)) with j1 + j2 >= k,
and the trivial pair costs N c(k). The pair loses exactly the defect c(j1) + c(j2) - c(j1 + j2).
- c additive (textbook, c = j): tie. This is every tie of the night once more.
- c = (3/4) j + 1/4 (tonight's family): every pair loses N/4 gates, at every size.
- c = C (log N)^z with z < 1 (the published program): a balanced pair costs 2^(1-z) times the trivial one; with z = 1 - 8.7625e-4
  that is 6.08e-4 more. **The published saving is, to first order, the amount by which two mirrors lose.**
- c bounded (a linear circuit): a pair costs twice the circuit. Two mirrors are the additive structure; linear size is the
  failure of additivity. The idea points away from where the user wants to go, and that is the most useful thing it says.

## 5. "Does any rule bind a mirror that only has to turn R into its inverse?" [derived]

A single mirror: no. D is one, its mirror is spanned by single addresses. The rule binds the pair:

> If S1 S2 = R and S2 v = v or S2 v = -v, then S1 v = +-R v, so (non-zeros of v) x (non-zeros of S1 v) >= N.

(The last step is the uncertainty rule of the transform [recalled: Donoho and Stark's inequality on the cube]; R = D H / sqrt N
has the same supports as H.) Corollaries. If S1 has at most w non-zeros per column, every eigenvector of S2 has at least
sqrt(N / w) non-zeros: sqrt N for a free S1 (the known rule for the transform's own eigenvectors), N^(1/4) for the
rows-then-columns pair, where it is reached (weight 2 at 16 points). A mirror with a sparse basis forces its partner to spread
that basis over N / (its weight) addresses. My machine check of this used the columns of the projectors, which are dense; it
binds nothing and I count the statement as derived only.

## 6. Two ways around, tried

**Around 1: use the pair at once, not one mirror after the other.** What fails in section 2 is the series connection. With the
smallest mirrors, R = 1 - 2 P1 - 2 P2 + 4 P1 P2 [ran], so R - 1 passes through the 2(N-1)/3 numbers "coordinates on the two
anti-mirrors", and those could come out of ONE circuit with shared wires, then go back through one circuit. At 4 points this is
7 gates [derived, not replayed: P1 is the single address with Q = 1, the two coordinates are x3 and x0 - x1 - x2, 2 + 1 + 1 + 3
gates]: the brick again. At 16 points with the mirrors built from D: P1 costs 11 gates, but P2 = R P1 R^-1 is five combinations
of the six rows of the transform at the points with Q = 1, in and out; that is g-0's route for the 6-dimensional mirror (24
gates in, the route at least 56) with one dimension less. It is also g-7's count from the other side: one third of a transform
in, one third out. Stated exactly [derived]: on the part of the space that R moves, the two mirrors are two "complex
conjugations" that differ by the cube root of one, so a pair is the choice of ONE subspace A of dimension (N-1)/3 (the
anti-mirror) with A and R A together spanning that part; g-7's matrix B is a basis of such an A plus a basis of the fixed space,
and g-7's cost(B) + cost(B^-1) is this fused price. g-7 found 64 and 56 at 16 points over about 112,000 bases [read]. Where it stops: both anti-mirrors must be cheap to read AND cheap to write, and the second is the first one
turned by R. This is brief 5 of this wave (thirdturn-basis); I leave the pricing to it.

**Around 2: let both mirrors be full.** What fails in section 4 is that supports multiply. Mirrors of the form
(diagonal)^-1 H (diagonal) / sqrt N are all involutions and all cost T(N); the product of two is "transform, diagonal,
transform". With a quadratic sign diagonal it collapses to a transform on r bits [recalled, see section 4]: 0 bits for a
character, k bits for D. So inside the stabilizer world every word in H, quadratic signs and relabellings is ONE partial
transform [recalled: the normal form], priced by its support. Read backwards it says [derived]: D H D is the matrix (-1)^Q(x + y), so
**T(N) is the cost of adding the inputs over all N translates of one quadric**, and two translates differ exactly on an affine
hyperplane. Where it stops: the difference of two outputs is twice the same problem on a hyperplane, which is the butterfly.
What is left open by it: diagonals that are not quadratic signs (cubic signs; powers of 2 as in H = Z D Z^T).

## 7. The wall, and what its hypotheses are

**Supports multiply, so costs add.** A pair of mirrors is a cut of the work into two stages. If each stage is priced by its
support (a mirror with 2^j non-zeros per row costs N c(j)), the stages' supports must multiply to N and the costs add.
Every stabilizer mirror is priced by its support (section 4), and at 4 points every mirror is (section 3, with a gap of 3).
The hypotheses, as a to-do list:
1. *The mirror is priced by its support.* False for a mirror that is dense and cheap: a free map conjugated by a cheap map with
   long rows (running sums, a tree of sums). No such mirror of R is known. At 4 points the cheapest dense mirrors cost 6 or 7.
2. *Row weights are uniform.* (iii) and the census are about equal rows. A mirror whose rows have very different weights
   (as the rows of the subset-sum matrix do) is not covered. The simplest kind [derived]: mirrors with t "hubs" (t full
   columns in S1, t full rows in S2) and everything else sparse with w1, w2 non-zeros per row. Then R = (sparse x sparse) +
   (a matrix of rank at most 2t), and the known bound on how far the transform is from low rank [recalled: Kashin and
   Razborov, at least N^2 / (4 r) changed entries for rank r] gives w1 w2 >= N / (8t): with plain hubs, each priced as one full row or
   column and the sparse parts priced by their support, the pair costs about N^(4/3) gates or more, worse than the textbook. Hubs help only if N/3 or so of them are cheap TOGETHER, and that is
   the route of g-0 and of brief 5 (a cheap basis of a mirror and of its dual), not a new one.
3. *Two stages.* By the lemma no pair can beat T(N); the pair is only a way to look. The fused form of section 6 is not covered
   by the lemma's proof, only by its conclusion.

## 9. Criticism, last

- The main result is negative and nearly trivial: the lemma of section 2 could have been written before any run. Its value is
  that it turns the brief's growth law into T(N) itself and so closes the idea in its literal form.
- All 4-point prices are over GF(5), GF(7), GF(13). The gap "at least 10" is a statement about those fields. Over the rationals
  with a square root of 2 and of -1 adjoined I have no census; the GF(17) run timed out and left no output.
- "N / 2^j copies of the 2^j-point transform up to free maps" is recalled, not derived tonight, and the price N c(j) for it is
  an upper bound (it assumes copies cannot be shared, which nobody has proved). The census itself is exact: bit matrices only.
  I did not build the actual matrices of the 1,728 mirrors; that each bit matrix lifts to a true mirror, possibly with
  entries in the Gaussian rationals, is derived (R fixes no label) and unchecked.
- The 64-point census is a sample of conjugates of D, not all mirrors. "Every j is even" is exhaustive at 4 and 16 points only;
  the reason offered is a guess.
- Line 6's sentence about the published law treats c(N) = C (log N)^z as exact at every size and the balanced pair's halves as
  optimal circuits; it is an asymptotic reading, not a computed number.
- The 16- and 64-point entries "at most 56, at most 320" for pairs without a free mirror were not searched: non-stabilizer
  pairs at 16 points (a family with 43 parameters) were not explored at all. That is the largest hole.
- The uncertainty rule of section 5 rests on a recalled inequality, and my machine check of it is empty.
- Around 1 and Around 2 are derivations; neither was priced by a search.
