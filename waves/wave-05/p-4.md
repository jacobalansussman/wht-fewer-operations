Words. N = 2^k points. *Kernel*: a 2 x 2 matrix [[a,b],[c,d]]; its *power* is the Kronecker product of k copies; the transform is the
power of [[1,1],[1,-1]]. *The class*: powers of kernels with no zero entry and non-zero determinant. *Merge tree*, *node*, *tree
rectangle*, *tau* as in WAVE5-PICKS note 1: a matrix that is (row scaling)(column scaling) times [1 + numbers on tau - 1 tree
rectangles] costs N - 2 + tau gates. *Centre*: that rank-one part. rho = ad/(bc), one number per kernel; the transform is rho = -1.

## 0. In ten lines

1. [ran] **The class has one parameter and the 13 has a formula.** Every kernel of the class is [[1,1],[1,rho]] up to scalings, so
   its power has entries (scalings) x rho^(x.y), x.y = the number of common 1-bits. With symbols a, b, c, d the twelve constants of
   the 19-gate circuit are: six times rho - 1 = det/(bc), six times 1/rho - 1 = -det/(ad). Nothing else. Section 1.
2. [ran] **The 13 is not the 4-point identity applied twice. It is the 4-point identity applied ONCE, to copies 1 and 3, with copy 2
   standing by.** The whole identity is one line of whole-number algebra, section 2. There is no statement about three copies.
3. [derived + ran] What makes 13 and not 16 (the brick tensored with a kernel, 22 gates) is the choice of centre: each of the four
   quadrants is wrong in exactly one of two directions, in a cycle. I call it the *cyclic* centre. The 4-point brick has such a form too.
4. [derived + ran] **A conservation law.** With j outer bit pairs there are 4^j blocks; whatever the centre, the total number of
   mismatched bits over the blocks is exactly j 4^j, and a block with d mismatched bits costs about 2^d times its inside. So the
   cost is at least 8^j against the textbook's 9^j, with equality exactly when every block has j. The 13 is the equality case at j = 1.
5. [ran] **Growth law of the rule: bad.** Nested, its ideal count is T(k) = 8 T(k-2) - 3: 5, 13, 37, 101, 293, 805, that is
   N^1.5; it passes the textbook's 770 at 128 points. And from 16 points on its rectangles are not tree rectangles (37 is a true
   identity for every kernel, with 8 crossing pairs of row sets). On trees the rule gives **49 at 16 points for every kernel**
   (63 gates, textbook 64); greedy tilings of the nested rule then give 177 at 32 points (textbook 130). Section 3.
6. [ran] **Divide by zero.** At 4 and 8 points a constant vanishes only when det = 0: no kernel of the class loses a gate. From 16
   points on, constants rho^2 - 1 appear, and they vanish at exactly one member: rho = -1, the transform. In one tiling 8 of 56
   rectangles drop for the transform and for nobody else. With overlaps allowed the class gets back to 49 too. Section 4.
7. [ran, exhaustive] **The pattern of the 5 and the 13, read literally, is "at most one rectangle per node of the row tree",** which
   would be tau <= 2N - 1, linear. It has no third term: at 16 points NO Hadamard matrix of any kind has that form (balanced trees,
   tiling; 154,781,762 search nodes, and again with a symmetry cut). The search reproduces 5 and 13 at 4 and 8 points. Section 5.
8. [ran] At 8 points, 13 is the minimum even with two rectangles per node (exhaustive, balanced trees, tilings).
9. No circuit below the best known counts; no growth law below N log N. The 49 is one gate under the textbook, not a record (52).
10. Wall, named: section 6. The better question it leaves: section 6, last paragraph.

## 1. (a) The twelve constants as symbols

[ran, `a_symbolic.py`] Every entry of a kernel power is a monomial in a, b, c, d, so hyb4.py's solve can be done on exponent
vectors, exactly, with no computer algebra. For hyb4's placement (kernel columns x = 0..7, rows y = 0..7):

    centre, by column:        a^2c, abc, abc, abd, a^2d, abd, b^2c, b^2d
    row scaling, by row:      a/c, 1, 1, c/a, 1, c/a, d/b, cd/(ab)
    K / (row scaling x centre) = 1 on 32 cells, = rho on 16 cells, = 1/rho on 16 cells,      rho = ad/(bc)
    the twelve rectangles: six carry rho - 1 = det/(bc), six carry 1/rho - 1 = -det/(ad).

So the scalings are monomials and the constants have degree 1 in rho. class19.txt agrees: for [[1,2],[2,1]], rho = 1/4, and the file's
3 and -3/4 are 1/rho - 1 and rho - 1 (its 6 is 3 times a scaling). k-cross's "determinant times a monomial" is this, for all kernels.
[derived] Reason: divide row 0 by a, row 1 by c, column 1 by b/a; the kernel becomes [[1,1],[1,rho]]. So the class is a
one-parameter family and every question about it is a question about the whole-number matrix x.y.

## 2. (b) The formula, and the answer to "what is the statement about three copies?"

[ran, `b_rule.py`: checked on all 64 cells, and the twelve rectangles it produces are k-cross's twelve as sets]
Rename the bits in the order in which the two trees split: columns X1 = x2, X2 = x1, X3 = x3; rows Y1 = y3, Y2 = y1, Y3 = y2. Then

    x.y = X1Y3 + X2Y2 + X3Y1                      (the first split of one tree is paired with the LAST split of the other)
    centre exponent  q(X) = X3(1 - X1) + X1X2 ,   row exponent  q'(Y) = Y1Y3
    m = x.y - q - q'   is, in the quadrant (X1, Y1):
        (0,0):  X2Y2 - X3            (0,1):  X2Y2 - Y3
        (1,0):  Y3 - X2(1 - Y2)      (1,1):  X3 - X2(1 - Y2)

Each quadrant is a difference of two things that are 0 or 1, so m is -1, 0 or +1, and in each quadrant the non-zero cells are three
tree rectangles: 1 + 4 x 3 = 13. That is the whole identity. The kernel power is (scalings) x rho^m, and rho^m = 1 + (rho - 1) on
the +1 rectangles + (1/rho - 1) on the -1 rectangles.

Reading. Bits 1 and 3 are crossed (X1 with Y3, X3 with Y1): that is the 4-point brick on copies 1 and 3. Bit 2 is paired with
itself: copy 2 is an untouched 2-point kernel sitting inside each quadrant (the term X2Y2, or -X2(1-Y2), which is the same kernel
with the other centre). **So the 13 is "brick on copies 1 and 3, tensor one kernel on copy 2", with a centre chosen so that the
cross terms fit.** Cayley-Hamilton is used once. There is no identity of three copies behind it.
[derived, not run] Symmetry types: the centre q = X3(1 - X1) is not invariant under swapping copies 1 and 3, so the 13 rectangles do
not respect the splitting of the third power into symmetry types. The usual brick (rank one + det x signed swap) does respect
the swap of its two copies; the form used inside the 13 is a different, lopsided form of the same 5:

    4 points, usual form :  X1Y2 + X2Y1 = X1X2 + Y1Y2 - (X1 - Y1)(X2 - Y2)           four single cells
    4 points, cyclic form:  q = X2(1 - X1), q' = Y1Y2;  m = -X2, -Y2, +Y2, +X2 in the four quadrants      four rectangles of two cells

[ran] Both give tau = 5, 7 gates. The usual form is "polarisation": a product is a square plus a square minus a square of the
difference. The cyclic form is the one that extends.

**Why 13 and not 16.** [derived] In the quadrant (X1 = a, Y1 = b) the exponent is a Y3 + b X3 + X2Y2. A centre is one function of X
times one function of Y, so over the column half X1 = a it can contain b X3 for one value of b only, and over the row half Y1 = b it
can contain a Y3 for one value of a only. A quadrant where the centre has the wrong b is *mismatched in X*; wrong a, *mismatched in
Y*. The textbook centre leaves the four quadrants with 0, 1, 1, 2 mismatches; the centre of the usual brick, 0, 0, 2, 2; the
cyclic centre, 1, 1, 1, 1. A quadrant with d mismatches costs 2^d T' - 1 rectangles, T' = tau of what sits inside (one less
when its two mismatches have opposite signs and cancel on a diagonal, which is the usual brick's case). So

    textbook centre  9 T' - 3        usual brick's centre  10 T' - 5        cyclic centre  8 T' - 3
    inside = nothing (T' = 1):   6,  5,  5              inside = one kernel (T' = 2):   15,  15,  13

[derived: these are ideal counts with overlapping rectangles; only the cyclic column was run (5, 13, and 37 in section 3).]
At 4 points the two bricks tie at 5; with anything inside, only the cyclic one keeps the saving. That is the step from 5 to 13.
(These are counts of one-letter forms. The 22-gate "brick tensor kernel" and the 24-gate textbook are circuits of another shape.)

## 3. (c) The rule in k and its growth law

**The rule.** Cross the outermost pair (X1 with Yk, Xk with Y1), use the cyclic centre on it, put the rule for k - 2 inside.
[derived] In a quadrant mismatched in X the matrix is [M | rho^(+-1) M], M = the inside relative to its own centre. With T' = tau
of the inside this needs 2(T' - 1) + 1 rectangles: the inside, one big rectangle for the wrong half, the inside again. So

    T(k) = 1 + 4 (2 T(k-2) - 1) = 8 T(k-2) - 3 :      k even 1, 5, 37, 293, 2341        k odd 2, 13, 101, 805
    textbook tau = N log2 N - N + 2 :                  2, 6, 18, 50, 130, 322, 770, 1794

It reproduces 5 and 13. As a growth law it is N^1.5 (a factor 8 for every factor 4 in N); it is under the textbook up to 64
points (293 against 322) and over it at 128 (805 against 770). The saving is not growing faster than N; it turns negative.

**The conservation law.** [derived; ran for j = 1 (all 16 centres) and j = 2 (all 65,536), `b_rule.py`] Take j outer bits on each
side, so 4^j blocks (a, b). A centre of this kind chooses, for each column block a, the one row block b*(a) it is right for, and
for each row block b the column block a*(b). The mismatch of block (a, b) is the number of bits in which a differs from a*(b) plus
the number in which b differs from b*(a). Summed over all blocks this is j 4^j, **whatever the choice**: each choice is compared
once with every string. By convexity the sum of 2^mismatch is at least 4^j 2^j = 8^j, with equality when every block has exactly j.
The run: total always 4 and 32; least sum 8 and 64; textbook choice 9 and 81. So within one centre the trick of the 13
is "9 becomes 8 per outer pair" and nothing better exists: half of the outer bits are always wrong.
(The law is about the leading term 2^d T'; the small corrections of section 2, minus 1 or minus 2 per block, are outside it.)

**16 points, ideal.** [ran, `f_ideal37.py`] The 1 + 36 rectangles of the rule are a true identity on all 256 cells for
[[1,2],[3,4]], [[2,7],[5,3]], [[1,1],[1,5]] and the transform. But the row sets contain 8 crossing pairs (sets that overlap
without one containing the other), and so do the column sets: a quadrant mismatched in X wants the node "X4 = 1" (X4 split
first), its neighbour in the same column half, mismatched in Y, wants the nodes "x' fixed, X4 free" (X4 split last). One tree
cannot hold both. At 8 points the inside has only single rows and columns and the clash does not arise; that is why 13 exists.
[derived, rough] Supplying the extra sums outside the tree costs about 3 gates per half, 12 in all: 14 + 37 + 12 = 63 again.

**16 points, on trees.** [ran, `f_tree49.py`] Cut each big rectangle into four lines: 1 + 48 rectangles, row sets and column sets
both nodes of one tree (no crossing pair), identity exact on all 256 cells for the same four kernels. So **tau = 49, 63 gates, for
every kernel of the class at 16 points**: one under the textbook, the figure k-cross and g-1 found for the transform alone by solver.
(Only the rectangle identity was replayed; I did not write the 63 gates out.)

**Beyond, greedy tilings** [ran, `d_tiling.py`; largest rectangle first, an upper bound, not a minimum; rule nested, natural bit order,
all 576 orders at 16 points, 41 at 32, one at 64]:

    points                         4     8     16         32      64
    class (m constant)             5    13     61 (57)    221     969          (57: inner brick as four cells, b_rule.py)
    transform (m odd)              5    13     49         177     705
    textbook                       6    18     50         130     322
    transform, rectangles/point    1    1.5    3          5.5     11

Rectangles per point double with every bit from 16 points on: on trees the nested rule is of order N^2. It dies, as k-cross's
"1, 1.5, 3" suggested; this gives the next two terms for this family and the reason (the clash above, then tiling by lines).

## 4. (d) Dividing by zero on purpose

- [ran] **8 points.** The constants are rho - 1 and 1/rho - 1. Zero only at rho = 1, which is det = 0. No invertible kernel loses
  a gate in this placement. [guess] The same in the other 287 placements: they are this identity moved by symmetries; not checked.
- **Blow-up.** 1/rho - 1 is infinite when ad = 0 (a zero on the diagonal: the subset-sum kernel), rho - 1 when bc = 0 (triangular).
  [read, h-6-classhub/limit19.out] Those limits of the 19-gate program were taken in wave 4: 12, 13, 13, 14 gates against a textbook
  of 12 for a kernel with a zero. Nothing gained there; I did not redo it.
- [ran] **16 points: the first constant that vanishes for an invertible kernel, and it is the transform's.** In tilings of the
  rule m takes the values -2 and 2, with constants rho^2 - 1 and rho^-2 - 1. These vanish for rho = -1 (and rho = 1): among
  invertible kernels, exactly the transform. In `b_rule.py`'s tiling of 56 rectangles, 8 have |m| = 2: the transform needs 48,
  every other kernel 56. That is k-cross's "the transform reads the exponent modulo 2, the class as a whole number" turned into a
  count for the first time. It does not survive overlaps: with overlapping rectangles the class reaches 48 as well (section 3).
  [ran, solver, `c_min16.py` on the Linux machine, 420 s each, time limit reached, lower bound only 40, uncertified]: best found
  for this design, transform 49, rho = 2: 54 (worse than my explicit 49, so the solver was far from done).
- [derived] In general a constant rho^v - 1 vanishes when rho is a root of unity of order dividing v. The transform has order 2, the
  smallest possible; a kernel with rho a cube root of unity reads the exponent modulo 3. If any saving belongs to the transform
  alone it must come from rectangles that mix cells with m = +1 and m = -1 (or 3, ...), which the transform may merge and the class
  may not. In the greedy tilings the transform's count is lower by 20 to 27 percent at 16, 32, 64 points; whether a minimum
  shows a gap is open (letter16, Brief 2, has the tool).
- **Degree of the constants.** [ran] |m| is at most 1 at 4 and 8 points, 2 at 16 and 32, 3 at 64: about k/2. For a real rho other
  than +-1 the constants reach size |rho|^(k/2), a power of N. For the transform |rho| = 1: no large number ever appears. The "large
  coefficients the bounded-coefficient wall cannot see" are not in this rule for the transform.

## 5. The pattern taken literally: one rectangle per node

Look at the 5 and the 13 without the algebra. [ran] In both, every node of the row tree carries **at most one** rectangle, its
column set is one node of the column tree, and the rectangles do not overlap (4 rectangles on 6 nodes; 12 on 14). If that had a
k-th term, tau <= 2N - 1 and the transform would cost at most 3N - 3 gates. So I searched for the third term directly.

Search [ran, exhaustive, `onenode.c`, `pernode.c`]: balanced row tree, balanced column tree; every row node except the root gets
no rectangle or one rectangle (itself) x (one column node); along each path from a row to the root the column sets are disjoint;
the resulting sign matrix must be a Hadamard matrix, that is, any two rows differ in exactly N/2 places. Nothing about the
transform is assumed (at 16 points there are five kinds of Hadamard matrix; the test covers all).

    4 points :  272 solutions (34 up to swapping the children of row nodes), fewest rectangles 4  -> tau 5, 7 gates   (known answer)
    8 points :  8,192 (64), fewest 12 -> tau 13, 19 gates                                                          (known answer)
    16 points:  NONE. Exhaustive twice: 154,781,762 search nodes without any symmetry cut, 2,672,080 with the children sorted.
                Eight rows (one half of the tree) can be completed; a ninth row never.

So the pattern has no third term, for the transform or for any Hadamard matrix. Mutations, each one run:
- [ran, exhaustive] 8 points, up to TWO rectangles per row node, at most 11 rectangles: none (294,107,398 nodes). So 13 is the
  minimum at 8 points for balanced trees and tilings with two per node; k-cross had it by solver for one design.
- [ran, NOT exhaustive] 16 points, one per node, overlaps allowed (minus-sets combine by exclusive or): stopped at 240 s,
  1.3 x 10^9 nodes, none found, still no ninth row.
- [ran, exhaustive, Linux machine] 16 points, two rectangles allowed on the four nodes of 4 rows, one elsewhere: none
  (108,623,144 nodes, 88 s). Still no ninth row.
- [ran, NOT exhaustive] 16 points, two rectangles allowed on every leaf, one elsewhere: stopped at 360 s, 2.7 x 10^8 nodes, none
  found, no ninth row in the part explored.
- [ran, NOT exhaustive] 16 points, two rectangles allowed on every node, at most 36 in all (36 would be 51 gates, one under the
  record): stopped at 300 s, 3.6 x 10^8 nodes, none found. Here a ninth row IS reached: the "two halves never fit" obstruction
  belongs to one per node; with two per node the question is open and the search simply did not finish.
- [ran, NOT exhaustive, Linux machine] 16 points, two rectangles allowed on the eight nodes of 2 rows, one elsewhere: stopped
  at 420 s, 4.8 x 10^8 nodes, none found, no ninth row in the part explored.

What "exhaustive" covers: balanced trees on both sides, one column node per rectangle, no overlaps. What it does not: other tree
shapes, overlaps (first mutation, unfinished), more rectangles per node (unfinished).

## 6. What the failure is made of, and the question it leaves

**The wall.** One centre is right for only half of the outer bits (the conservation law), and a one-letter form pays for every
wrong bit by a factor 2. The 5 and the 13 are the best possible distribution of that debt, not a way out of it. Past 8 points the
debt also breaks the trees (a quadrant wrong in X and its neighbour wrong in Y want the last bit split first and last). And the
literal pattern "one rectangle per node" stops because two halves of 8 rows, each of which exists, never fit together.
This is k-cross's L3 and 1 D from the inside: the saving so far is one free contraction (one hub) per block, a constant.

**Going around, tried.** (1) Keep the rule, drop the trees: 37 is an identity but costs 63 once the extra sums are paid. (2) Keep
the trees, cut the big rectangles: 49. (3) Drop the rule, keep only its shape (one per node): none at 16. (4) Use the one thing the
transform has and the class has not (constants vanishing at rho = -1): 8 rectangles drop in one tiling, none after overlaps.

**The better question.** "A bounded number of tree rectangles per node" IS linear size for circuits of this shape, and it is a
finite question at each N that says nothing about the transform until the last line: *is there any Hadamard matrix of order 16 or
32 that is a sum of at most c rectangles per node, for c = 2 or 3, on any pair of trees?* The search here closes c = 1 on balanced
trees. By k-cross 1 D the rectangles other than the root must cover at least N(N-1)/4 cells, so with cN rectangles their average
area is at least about N/(4c) while no one-signed rectangle exceeds N: every rectangle has to be within a constant of the largest
possible. At 16 points, with one per node, the leaves carry exactly 64 of the needed cells [derived: two sibling rows differ in 8
places, and only their own two rectangles can make the difference], so the 14 inner nodes must carry the rest with column sets of
at most 2, 4, 8 columns. A proof by hand of "none at 16" along these lines would say which of the two (area, or fitting two halves)
is the real obstruction, and whether unbalanced trees escape it.

**Next, in order.** (a) Run `pernode` to the end for "two per node" at 16 points on the Linux machine (it is a single-thread C
program; unknown length). (b) Unbalanced trees: the search fixes balanced trees on both sides; a caterpillar on one side is the
Potts shape, the only linear one of the night. (c) The gap question of section 4 with an exact minimum instead of greedy tilings.

## 7. Criticism, last

- The formula, the 13, the 37 and the 49 are exact identities of whole-number exponents, checked cell by cell, so they hold for
  every kernel with no zero entry. But "49 means 63 gates" uses k-cross's count N - 2 + tau; I did not write that circuit out.
- The growth table on trees is greedy tilings in a few bit orders: upper bounds for ONE family of centres (the nested rule). A
  better centre at 16 points is letter16's job; nothing here says 49 is the least for the class.
- The conservation law is about centres that are right or wrong bit by bit. A centre that is not of that product kind is outside
  it. The exhaustive search of section 5 does not have this limit (it tries every sign matrix of the shape), but it has its own:
  balanced trees, one column node per rectangle, tilings.
- "None at 16" was found by two versions of one program of mine. The check is that both reproduce 5 and 13 and agree with each other
  (272 = 8 x 34, 8,192 = 128 x 64). Nobody else has run it.
- The solver runs of section 4 hit their time limit on a loaded machine; they are candidates and their lower bound (40) is useless.
- Symmetry types: argued in one line, not computed. The 288 placements: not examined.
- Five short control runs of `pernode` (the longest 21 s) were started directly instead of through light.py, by mistake.
- I read no literature. [recalled, unchecked, may be out of date] Writing a kernel power as low rank plus sparse and tensoring is
  the method of the published work on small circuits for Kronecker powers; the ideal count 5^(k/2) of the brick tensored with
  itself is of that kind. Whether the 13-rectangle form or the conservation law is in that literature I do not know.
- No change of order. No record.
