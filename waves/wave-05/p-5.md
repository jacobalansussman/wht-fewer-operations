Gate counts: **(G)** = any a*u + b*v is one gate, scalings free. **(B c)** = my bounded count for this report: a gate is
a*u + b*v with a, b whole numbers of size at most c, extra values allowed, and every output must be the row EXACTLY
(no free scaling). (B 1) is "additions and subtractions only". "In place" = no extra value: a move is row_i <- row_i +- row_j.

Words. N = 2^k points. K = [[1,1],[1,2]]; its k-th Kronecker power K_k has entries 2^(number of common ones of the two
addresses); determinant 1. Z = subset sums, K = Z Z^T. H = the transform (entries +1, -1), U = H / sqrt(N) the orthonormal
transform (determinant +-1 as well). *Volume floor* (Morgenstern): with coefficients of size at most 1 a gate can at most
double any minor (sub-determinant) of the matrix computed so far, so gates >= log2 of the largest minor of the target.

## 2. Experiment (b): the singular-value floor for K_k [derived + ran, `floor.py`, `floor.out`]

K's singular values are phi^2 and phi^-2 (phi the golden ratio), so K_k has phi^(2(k-2j)), C(k,j) times. The sum of the
squares of all r x r minors is at least the product of the r largest squared singular values, and there are C(N,r)^2
minors, so gates >= max over r of [sum of the r largest log2(singular value) - log2 C(N,r)].

| k | 4 | 8 | 16 | 32 | 64 | 128 | 256 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| floor / N, K_k | 0.29 | 0.59 | 1.21 | 2.12 | 3.42 | 5.26 | 7.86 |
| floor / N, Z_k (g-4's) | 0 | 0 | 0.12 | 0.57 | 1.21 | 2.13 | 3.43 |
| bounded cost of the diagonal L_k / N (section 3) | 2.08 | 3.04 | 4.36 | 6.22 | 8.83 | 12.5 | 17.7 |
| textbook / N | 4 | 8 | 16 | 32 | 64 | 128 | 256 |

Asymptotically 0.554 sqrt(k) - 1 (for coefficients of size c divide by log2(2c)). It is positive from k = 1 and twice
g-4's floor for Z, as it should be (K's singular values are the squares of Z's). Growth law: **sqrt(k)**, as guessed.

## 3. Where the floor lives, and why K is not the freest member [derived; the 2 x 2 identities [ran], `words.py`]

K is symmetric with eigenvalues phi^2, phi^-2, so K = R diag(phi^2, phi^-2) R^T with R a rotation, and
K_k = R_k L_k R_k^T. L_k is a diagonal with entries phi^(2(k-2j)): scaling a wire by 2^e costs |e| bounded gates, total
1.39 N E|k - 2J| = 1.11 N sqrt(k). That is within a factor 2 of the floor of section 2: **the floor is the price of L_k**,
as g-4 found that Morgenstern's N log N for H is the price of one diagonal. The rest is R_k, an orthogonal matrix.
A power of a rotation is two calls of U and three diagonals of unit-size complex numbers (any 2 x 2 rotation is
phase, H, phase, H, phase), each diagonal N bounded gates. So over the complex numbers

    0.554 N sqrt(k) - N  <=  cost_bounded(K_k)  <=  4 cost_bounded(U_k) + 1.11 N sqrt(k) + 6 N.

Directly, with real numbers only [ran as 2 x 2 identities, exact in Q(sqrt 2) and Q(sqrt 17)]:
- K = D H diag(a,b) H D with D = diag(1, sqrt 2), a, b = 1/2 +- sqrt(2)/4: **two calls of the transform**.
- p H = diag(1,x) K diag(a,1) K diag(1,x), a = (-9 + sqrt 17)/4, x = (3 - sqrt 17)/4, p = (-5 + sqrt 17)/4: **two calls of K**.
- H = L K and K = L^-1 H with L = [[1,0],[3,-2]] (a triangular member, textbook k N / 2): one call, but the fee is of the
  order of the thing itself. Both directions keep coefficients bounded; neither is cheap. (Experiment (c): no direction
  through a triangular word helps; the two-call diagonal words do.)
A diagonal power diag(1,x)^(k), normalised to determinant 1, has entries x^(j - k/2): bounded cost of order N sqrt(k).
Hence, in the bounded world, for members normalised to determinant 1:

    cost(K_k) <= 2 cost(U_k) + O(N sqrt k),      cost(U_k) <= 2 cost(K_k) + O(N sqrt k).

So (1) the unnormalised members differ by N log N of pure scaling (that is all Morgenstern measures); (2) the
determinant-1 members are one problem up to a factor 2 and N sqrt(log N); (3) the hub is U, where even the sqrt(k) floor
is absent. **"A bounded family for K at N sqrt(log N)" is equivalent to "the orthonormal transform in O(N sqrt(log N))
bounded gates"**, and the upper and lower bound for K would then meet. The brief's idea survives, moved to its true home.

## 4. The quantity that a circuit for K must make [derived + ran, `words.py`, `inplace.py`]

Quasi-entropy: p_il = M_il (M^-1)_li; every row of p sums to 1; Phi(M) = - sum p_il log2 |p_il|. For a unitary M it is
the entropy of the squared entries, Ailon's potential [recalled]. It is additive: Phi(A (x) B) = n_B Phi(A) + n_A Phi(B).
For a 2 x 2 kernel [[a,b],[c,d]] the row is (ad, -bc)/det:

| kernel | row of p | Phi per row and bit | sum of |p| per row |
|---|---|---|---|
| H | (1/2, 1/2) | 1 | 1 |
| K, and [[2,1],[1,1]] | (2, -1) | -2 | 3 |
| Z (any triangular kernel) | (1, 0) | **0** | 1 |
| [[1,1],[1,3]] | (3/2, -1/2) | -1.38 | 2 |
| [[1,2],[3,4]] | (-2, 3) | -2.75 | 5 |

So |Phi| = k N for H, 2 k N for K, 0 for subset sums. It is zero exactly when the kernel has a zero entry [derived:
p log|p| + (1-p) log|1-p| vanishes only at p = 0, 1]: the boundary members, whose textbook is half, are the ones with no
entropy to make. In the textbook in-place program for K_2 [ran]: Phi after the 8 moves = 0, -4, -4, -8, -8, -12, -12, -16.
The down shear of each butterfly makes nothing; the up shear makes 4. This is d-0's remark "every known gain lives in the
meeting of the down pass and the up pass", as a number.

Is it a floor? A move changes Phi only in two rows, by an amount bounded by the size of the cross terms M_jl (M^-1)_li,
that is by the conditioning of the state [derived; this is the shape of Ailon's theorem, [recalled]: N log N / R gates
when every intermediate matrix has condition number at most R, no extra memory]. Without that hypothesis it fails [ran]:

| moves m on 3 wires (row_i +- row_j) | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| largest abs(Phi) | 4.0 | 6.49 | 10.76 | 14.76 | 27.05 |
| per move | 2.0 | 2.16 | 2.69 | 2.95 | 4.51 |
| largest sum of abs(p) | 7 | 15 | 39 | 99 | 259 |

(exhaustive over all states at each depth; 42,108 new states at m = 6; extremal state at m = 6: rows (0,3,5), (1,1,0),
(1,3,3)). The reason [derived]: a row of p like (P, -P/2, -P/2 + 1) has entropy of order P, not log P, and P can grow
like a power of 2.6 per move. On 2 wires this cannot happen (a row is (P, 1-P), entropy about log P, 1.39 per move).
So: **the meter is N log N for K, a well-conditioned gate raises it by a constant, an ill-conditioned state can pump it,
and pumping needs at least three wires that are nearly dependent.** K_k itself has sum |p| = 3^k per row, so the target
is ill conditioned and the theorem as recalled says nothing about K; for U it says: either extra memory or a state with
condition number of order (N log N)/gates. For linear size that is only log N. It is a mild demand, not a wall.

## 5. What the idea became

Not "K has no floor, so look for a cheap family there", but:
**In the bounded-coefficient world the whole class, normalised to determinant 1, is the orthonormal transform up to
N sqrt(log N); the only meter that reads N log N on it is the quasi-entropy; and the only known ways to make entropy
faster than 2 per gate are (i) an extra value and (ii) an ill-conditioned state of three or more wires.**
Tonight's facts fit: every saving found needs an extra value (k-cross L6; mine: K_2 in place is 8, with a fifth value 7),
and no saving yet uses (ii) (k-cross hybrid 4: constants up to 1024 buy nothing). (ii) is the unexplored door, and
section 4's table says where it opens: not on pairs of wires, on triples, after about five moves.

1. **Measure the published program with this meter.** A recursive program has finitely many distinct gate coefficients,
   so apart from its final scalings it IS a bounded-coefficient program, and by Morgenstern it must then compute something
   of volume about that of U, not of H: read in these terms the 8.7625e-4 program is a statement about the orthonormal
   transform. Measure on its smallest instance: the coefficient set, where the final scalings sit, the condition number
   of its stages, the number of extra values, and Phi per gate. If Phi per gate exceeds 2 anywhere, that gate is the
   lever and its neighbourhood is what to enlarge. [guess that this is cheap; I did not open the program.]
2. **Entropy pumps at 4 and 8 points.** Search (exhaustive at 4 wires with one extra value, annealed at 8) for the
   largest Phi-gain per gate that ENDS well conditioned: pump up, spend, come back. Growth law: best gain per gate as a
   function of the number of wires. If it stays near 2 when the end state must be orthogonal, that is a new floor for U
   with no conditioning hypothesis at small size; if it grows, the pump is a brick for a faster program.
3. **Rotations of small angle.** The entropy of the power of a rotation by angle t is k N h(sin^2 t), which tends to 0
   with t, while its textbook stays k N and its count (G) at 4 points stays 7 [read: i-cousins, generic kernel]. So the
   meter and the count part ways as t -> 0. Find the 4- and 8-point bounded minimum as a function of t (t with rational
   sine and cosine: 3-4-5, 5-12-13, 20-21-29): does the count ever follow the entropy down? If yes, a product of few
   small rotations reaching 45 degrees is a program; if no, the meter is not the right one and the reason is the next idea.
4. **K_3 exactly, in (B 1).** The 19 uses 2 and -1/2. With +-1 only is the 8-point minimum 24? A restricted exhaustion
   (helpers that are sums of K_2's helpers) would say whether "a doubling is needed for every gate saved" is a law:
   at 4 points it is one doubling for one gate.
5. **The literature check I did not do.** Ailon's three papers (unitary gates and entropy; the R-well-conditioned model;
   algebraic condition number) and whether "bounded coefficients, extra memory allowed, orthonormal transform" has any
   bound at all. If none, section 3 says the bounded complexity of every determinant-1 member of the class is open
   between N sqrt(log N) and N log N, and for U between N and N log N.
