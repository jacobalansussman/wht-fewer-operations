# e-6-shifts: transform N shifted copies at once and read the diagonal

**First line, as asked: no circuit below the known counts and no growth law below N log N came out of this.** Every collapse landed on 7, 8 or more gates at 4 points (count G; never 6) and on 56, 60 or 64 at 16 points. What did come out: a working collapser, a rule that predicts its answers, the reason the idea cannot beat a direct program in a gate count, one anomaly of the first wave explained (why two "flags" work at 8 and 16 points and never at 32 or 64), and one new identity (the multiplication table).

10 October 2026, 22:09 to 22:50 EDT. Private; one agent; nothing reviewed, nothing in Lean, nothing published.
Marks: [ran] exact computation, file named; [derived] worked by hand here, not run; [read]; [guess].
Count used everywhere unless said: **(G)**, a gate is any a*u + b*v, scalings free. Count (O) (OpenAI's, additions and scalar multiplications each charged) was not computed by the program; by hand every 7-gate result below is the known "7 in G, 8 in O" circuit, so nothing is below 8 in (O) either.

Words. *N-point transform*: the Walsh-Hadamard transform H on N = 2^k numbers; H(w, s) = +1 or -1 by the parity of the bits shared by w and s. *Shift array*: the N x N array X with X(a, b) = x(a xor b), i.e. every row is the data x moved by one address; writing it costs nothing because a copy of a number is free. *Big program*: a program for the N^2-point transform (the transform of the array in both directions). *Subgroup K*: a set of addresses closed under xor. *Collapse*: feed the big program the array made from only N unknowns, treat wires that carry the same combination of the unknowns up to a factor as one wire, drop what the wanted outputs do not need, count the rest.

## 1. In seven lines

1. The collapser exists and was run on 118 different 16-point programs under 70 ways of writing 4 numbers into 16 places. No collapse below 7 (the census of the first wave says 6 is impossible; this is also a check of the collapser). [ran]
2. **At 4 points the machine is complete as a generator**: the collapses of four big identities reach all 228 of the 228 optimal 7-gate circuits of the census, and nothing outside it. [ran: `allK.out`, last lines]
3. A 90-gate complex program that nobody designed as a 4-point program (two "flag" steps) collapses, using only its own gates, to an optimal 7-gate real circuit for some subgroups, and to 8 gates with the complex helper (1, i, i, -1) for others. The 87-gate "Clebsch" program collapses to 7 for exactly 20 of the 35 subgroups. [ran]
4. **The rule.** For a program that is a chain of passes, only the passes that open a new direction of x cost anything: with the 7-gate kernel, 28 for a pass that opens two directions, 16 for one, 0 for none. Predicted 56, 60 or 64 at 16 points out of 256; matched 14 of 14 subgroups tried. [derived, then ran: `up256.out`]
5. **The wall, and it is short**: a collapse is itself an N-point program, so the best collapse over all big identities and all free ways of writing x into the array equals the best direct N-point program, never less. Going up before coming down can find programs; it cannot beat them. [derived]
6. Side result that closes a door of the first wave: writing the transform as a product of "flag" steps needs at least k/2 of them for k address bits, so two flags stop at 16 points. This explains the first wave's "0 hits in 60,000 at 32 points". [derived]
7. New identity: the big transform of the **multiplication table** of x (field multiplication in place of xor) is again a multiplication table, of the Kloosterman-sum image of x. [derived, then ran at 4, 8, 16: `idents.out`]

## 2. What was built and run

Folder `waves/wave-02/e-6-shifts/`. All arithmetic exact (Python fractions, Gaussian rationals a + b*i); nothing modulo a prime.

- `collapse.py`: the collapser, and builders for 16-point programs, each checked to compute the 16-point transform exactly before use:
  - textbook (64 gates), in two orders of the four bits;
  - "two-pass": the 7-gate 4-point program on rows, then on columns (56 gates), also with a different 7-gate variant on every row and column;
  - "rook": 4x - 2(row sum) - 2(column sum) + total on a 4 x 4 array (63 gates), two orders of adding;
  - "Clebsch": total - 2(sum over the six-point set y + {0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 15}) (87 gates), four ways of pairing the six terms;
  - "two-flag": the 54 pairs of flags of `i-hatch/flags.py`, in both orders, 108 programs of 90 gates each, complex. A flag is a nested chain of subgroups; a flag step is "a weighted sum of block averages over the chain", about 3N gates as I built it.
- Two ways of writing 4 unknowns into 16 places, for each of the 35 two-dimensional subgroups K of the 16 addresses:
  - *fan-out* ("pull"): x is copied onto the four cosets of K; the answer appears on the four addresses perpendicular to K. For K = the diagonal {(a, a)} this is exactly the shift array.
  - *sparse*: x sits on the four points of K, zeros elsewhere; then every one of the 16 outputs is one of the four answers up to sign. For K = the diagonal this is "x on the diagonal of the 4 x 4 array", the other embedding asked for.
- Two ways of counting what survives:
  - *strict*: a wire may only be made the way some gate of the big program made it;
  - *pool*: the big program only suggests helper wires; any two available wires may be combined. (Searched exhaustively up to 7 gates in pool mode and 8 in strict mode.)
- `e6b.py` re-runs the first wave's census (`i-play/n4.py`, 228 gate sets) and classifies every 7-gate collapse against it.

## 3. Why: three small laws [derived]

**Law 1 (no gain).** Whatever the big program P, the free embedding E (copies, zeros, scalings) and the outputs read, the collapsed program is a program on N inputs computing the N-point transform. So cost(collapse) >= best direct N-point cost, and equality is reached (take P = a best N-point program on the rows, anything on the columns). The same holds for any "encode for free, run something big, read off" scheme. In a count of gates the detour never pays; it is only a way to search.

**Law 2 (product programs collapse to one factor).** If P = (program A on rows) then (program B on columns), and X is the shift array, then after the row pass row w of the array is the single number (Hx)(w) times a sign pattern (the curator's remark), so every gate of the column pass computes a multiple of one of its inputs: free. The collapse is A, once. For a chain of passes on digits and a general subgroup, a pass along directions that x already "knows" modulo K is free and the others pay; that is the 28 / 16 / 0 rule above.

**Law 3 (the fold rule).** Call a big program *covariant* when shifting the array along K permutes its wires (with signs). On data that does not change along K, wires in one orbit carry the same value. A gate that joins two wires of the same orbit is then a scaling or a zero and costs nothing; a gate joining two orbits costs one per orbit of gates. So collapse cost = (gates between orbits) / N. The textbook has exactly half its gates of each kind, which is why the 2k-digit textbook collapses to the k-digit textbook and to nothing less. For a collapse to beat N log N the big program would need its between-orbit gates to number fewer than N^2 log N, half of what the textbook has; by Law 1 this is the same as having a better N-point program.

**Link to the floor note.** The sparse embedding on the diagonal of two digit columns is exactly the test data of the floor theorem (`wht38/floor/NOTE.md`, section 3: "feed every live array data on the diagonal of the chosen pair"), and the shift array is its mirror image (transpose). So the floor theorem's potential argument and this collapse look at the same object; the collapse keeps the circuit where the floor note keeps only a dimension. I did not get a stronger floor out of this: after one block and a relabelling the data leaves the class where wires merge, and a "cheap" sum of two arrays can then have as many distinct wires as entries.

## 4. Identities and regularities met

A. [ran at 4, 8, 16] H * X * H = N * diag(Hx) for the shift array, and its mirror H * diag(x) * H = shift array of Hx. In words: the big transform sends "constant along the diagonal" to "supported on the diagonal" (this is Poisson summation: summing over a subgroup on one side is sampling on the perpendicular subgroup on the other).

B. [ran at 4, 8, 16] **X + diag(Hx) commutes with H and X - diag(Hx) anticommutes with it.** So the "pinch" of the brief (the part of an array that commutes with the reflection H / sqrt(N)) is, for a shift array, simply (X + diag(Hx)) / 2. The pinch of the one array that is free to write down already contains the answer; computing a pinch is not easier than the transform.

C. [derived, then ran at 4, 8, 16] **Multiplication table.** Give the addresses the field structure with N elements and put X(a, b) = x(a * b). Then the big transform Y of X satisfies Y(u, v) = y(u * v) for (u, v) not (0, 0), and Y(0, 0) = y(0) + N * (sum of x), where y(t) = sum over s of Kl(s, t) x(s) and Kl(s, t) = sum over c not 0 of (-1)^Tr(c*s + t/c). Unless s = t = 0 this is the Kloosterman sum of the product s * t (values seen for s, t not 0: -1, 3 at N = 4; -5, -1, 3 at 8; -5, -1, 3, 7 at 16). In matrix form the map x -> y is H R H with R the relabelling c -> 1/c of the non-zero addresses, address 0 dropped [derived; the run checks the Kloosterman form]. So "field-multiplication shifts", the other embedding asked for, do not make the transform appear on the diagonal; they make the transform conjugate a free relabelling (inversion) into a convolution on the multiplicative group. That is the same kind of structure as Rader's trick for prime lengths, met here from the other side [guess as to how close the analogy is].

D. [derived] **At least k/2 flags.** Suppose the transform's spectrum i^(weight of y) is a product of r flag steps (each constant on the shells of a chain of subgroups). Take the flags one at a time; each cuts the current affine subspace by at most one condition while keeping its level constant, so on an affine subspace of dimension at least k - r all r levels are constant, hence the weight is constant modulo 4 there. A subspace a + V with constant weight modulo 4 forces any two vectors of V to share an even number of bits, so dim V <= k/2. Hence r >= k/2 rounded up: 1, 2, 2, 3, 3 for k = 2..6, exactly the first wave's table (one flag at 4 points, two at 8 and 16, "0 hits" for two at 32 and 64). [ran: `flagbound.out`, exhaustive for k = 2..7: the largest affine subspace with weight constant modulo 4 has dimension 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3; the step "each flag costs at most one dimension" stays a hand argument] With about 3N gates per flag step as built here (at least N in any case), flags give at best a constant factor against the textbook's N * k, never a change of order.

E. [derived; the 16-point case ran] The 4-point 7-gate program is the smallest case of the Clebsch form: "total minus twice the points where a quadratic form is 1". The number of points subtracted per output is N/2 - sqrt(N)/2 = 1, 6, 28, 120, the same sequence the codes developer found as an eigenspace dimension.

## 5. What failed, what the failure is made of, and how I went around

1. *Product identities gain nothing.* Made of Law 2. Went around: took the non-product identities (rook, Clebsch, two-flag). They do collapse to circuits nobody wrote down (7 gates out of 90), but all inside the census.
2. *The shift array is a poor embedding for non-product identities.* Made of position: the diagonal is tangent to their structure. Went around: all 35 subgroups and the sparse mirror; found the "general position" rule and the 20 / 15 split.
3. *No collapse can beat a direct program.* Made of Law 1, which is nearly a tautology in a gate count. Went around twice: (a) asked whether a symmetric big program could have more than half its gates "inside orbits" (Law 3): same question as the direct one; (b) changed the shift from xor to field multiplication: got identity C, a different transform (Kloosterman), not a cheaper one.
4. *Second half of the brief (pinch, direct sums fail for a fixed matrix times a generic one).* Identity B says the pinch of a shift array is trivial. The N shifted copies are a degenerate batch (N^2 wires carrying only N numbers), so they say nothing about N independent arrays, which is where a direct-sum saving would live. For independent arrays, the rank count behind "Strassen with a known factor" gives exactly four half-size products for the two-by-two split, the textbook; a gain has to come from changing the batch size between levels, which is the "batched calls" hatch of the floor note and is not touched here.

## 6. Where it could go (seeds, not verdicts)

- **The generator at 16 points needs a new kind of big identity.** At 4 points it reproduces the whole census from four identities. At 16 points out of 256 the families at hand are closed under collapse [derived, not run]: a product collapses to a factor (Law 2); "total minus twice a quadric" at 256 points, written on a subgroup where the quadratic form stays non-degenerate (in the program's own coordinates), collapses to "total minus twice a quadric" at 16 points, which is the Clebsch program again, because a quadratic form plus a linear form on K is a translate of the same form; "x minus twice a projection" on rows and columns collapses to the same formula one level down. So none of them can show a 16-point program that is not already known. What could: an identity at 256 points with at least four flags (Law D) or one that mixes two of these families. For that, the tool is ready: `collapse.py` for the wires, and the first wave's search programs could take the collapsed pool as their set of allowed helpers. Target under 56 (G).
- [ran: `window.py`, `window.out`, N = 4, 16, 64] **The shift array with a window.** Multiply row c of the shift array of a fixed vector v by x and transform it: G(c, d) = sum over s of v(s xor c) * (-1)^(d.s) * x(s). If v is unchanged by the transform up to the factor sqrt(N), then the transform acts on the N^2 numbers G as a signed transposition, G of Hx at (c, d) = sqrt(N) * (-1)^(c.d) * G of x at (d, c): a relabelling. Checked for the window "one at A plus one at the perpendicular of A" (support 3, 7, 15), which is not one of the codes developer's atoms. For the window "sqrt(N) at address 0 plus one everywhere", G(c, d) = sqrt(N) * (-1)^(c.d) * x(c) + (Hx)(d): every one of the N^2 numbers is one input plus one output. So there are N^2 numbers on which the transform is free, and any N of them that determine x and are closed under the swap give "analysis, relabel, synthesis". [derived] With these two windows the analysis is no cheaper than the transform (the first needs both a row pass and a column pass). [guess] The open part is windows with several bumps of different widths, where the analysis could be a pyramid; the codes developer's floor covers only windows that are atoms of one code.
- [guess] **Kloosterman side door.** Identity C says H turns inversion (free) into a cyclic convolution of length N - 1. Together with shifts (which H turns into sign changes, also free) inversion generates the fractional-linear group of the projective line (which has N + 1 points, one more than the array; I did not work out the correction). So every relation in that group becomes a relation between free matrices and the one dense matrix H R H. The codes developer's (T S)^3 = 1 has the same shape. Nobody has asked whether a long word in that group gives H R H more cheaply than two transforms. N - 1 is odd (15 = 3 * 5, 63 = 7 * 9, 255 = 3 * 5 * 17), which connects to the undeveloped idea 49 (odd radix as a source of bricks).
- For the engine people. [derived] Fed the shift array on a pair of digit columns, a rank-k block acts as one k-bit partial transform on a single column, and a relabelling applied alike to both columns keeps the array a shift array only if it is affine (xor-linear plus a constant). [guess] So the published unit's non-affine relabellings are what takes it out of the class where wires merge, and a unit using only affine ones would collapse to a single-column program at the same rate and could not save. Not checked against the unit.
