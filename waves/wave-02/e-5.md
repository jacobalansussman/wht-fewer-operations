# e-5-conserved: what "64" is made of, and the first gate that makes more

> Editor's note (11 October 2026): the second note corrected this report's "conservation law": it is one bit per operation for rotation-like gates, and the 60 operations of [e2-0](e2-0.md) break it (the line "Taken back or corrected" in [the second note](NOTE.md)).

**No circuit below the textbook count and no growth law below N log N was found.** What was found: the law behind the repeated 64, a proof of it at 4 points, the first gate that breaks it, and the two reasons why that gate has not yet produced a 63.

Two counts are used. **(O)**: OpenAI's, an addition, a subtraction and a multiplication by a constant cost 1 each. **(G)**: one gate is any a·u + b·v, scalings free. Every number below says which.

## 1. Where it ended, in seven lines

1. **4 points: 8 is a theorem in count (O), under all three scaling conventions** (outputs exact; outputs up to any scale; inputs and outputs both up to any scale). [ran + derived] The 7 of count (G) cannot be bought back by carrying a scaling debt.
2. **Of the candidate conserved totals only one is the same in every 64-program: additions + scalings.** Additions alone (64 against 56), scalings alone (0 against 8), the excess (34 against 26) and the sum of log2(support × co-support) (256 against 300.68) all differ. [ran]
3. **What is conserved is a rate: one bit per operation.** Give the program-so-far its entropy (defined in section 3). The finished N-point transform has exactly N·log2 N bits, 64 at 16 points. Every gate of the textbook and of the "sum of four" program makes exactly one bit per count-(O) operation. [derived] For programs made of butterflies and sum-of-four gates this is a theorem: at most one bit per operation, so N·log2 N operations at every N (section 5). In count (G) the sum-of-four gate makes 8 bits with 7 gates, and that is the whole 7/8.
4. **The law first breaks at a sum of FIVE points.** The gate "subtract from each of q points twice their average" costs 2q operations in count (O) and makes, on fresh data, 1.00 bit per operation at q = 4, 1.11 at q = 5, 1.14 at q = 6, 1.13 at q = 7, 1.11 at q = 8, and falls below 1 again at q = 12. [ran] On slightly prepared data the best I found is 1.20 (q = 8). [ran, floating point]
5. **But the rate is bounded (about 1.2), so this door is a constant factor, not a change of order**: at best about 0.83·N·log2 N in count (O) for this kind of gate. [derived from 4]
6. **And no whole program uses it yet.** At 8 points none exists below 24 in this class (the entropy count leaves seven shapes, the parity laws exclude all seven, and two of them were also enumerated in full). [derived + ran] At 16 points the necessary conditions leave room down to 56, but annealing found no finished program below 64 (its control found 64 every time), and the best entropy it reached with 62 operations was exactly 62 bits, without any 5- to 8-point gate. [ran, floating point; section 5]
7. **The published unit breaks the law in a different way and very far out.** Its cheap steps are additions of whole arrays, which are not rotations, so they have no bit rate at all; they let 120 of 56,835 transforms-along-one-direction be skipped. With about 19,400 whole-array additions per unit to pay for that, count (O) first dips at arrays of about 2^2,900 to 2^7,300 points. [read + ran + derived, rough] So between 16 points and there, every known program obeys the law.

## 2. Four points: 8 is exact, and debt does not help

- **Count (G) minimum is 7.** [derived + read] Six gates would mean: the first full output is a tree on the four inputs (3 gates, two of them helpers that are restrictions of that output), and each other output is one gate on wires already there. All ratios are then ±1, which is the range `i-play/n4.py` searched exhaustively: 0 programs at 6 gates, 228 at 7.
- **A count-(O) program of cost 7 is seven additions/subtractions and nothing else.** [derived] Deleting the scalings of a count-(O) program gives a count-(G) program with as many gates as it had additions, and count (G) does not change when rows or columns are rescaled. So fewer than 7 additions is impossible under every convention, and cost 7 leaves no room for a scaling.
- **No such program exists.** [ran, `o4.py`, exhaustive, 279,937 nodes] Wires are integer vectors kept up to sign (u − v and v − u are both one subtraction, so a sign can always be pushed to the next gate). Tested on every reachable set: four wires equal to ± rows; four wires proportional to rows; four full wires whose pairwise ratios are ± a constant with two plus and two minus (that is "rows of H·D up to scale", any diagonal D). None at 6 or 7; at 8 the textbook appears, and under the third convention also the form "sum minus twice each" with one input negated, in 8 additions and no scaling.
- **Why debt fails.** [derived] Every 7-gate program in count (G) contains a wire like (1, −1, 1, 1) = total − 2·x1. The factor 2 sits between the total and a single input, and both are also used at ratio 1 (the total is built from the inputs). A debt is a scale on a wire; here the misfit is between two uses of the same wire. So it is a property of the matrix "identity minus half the all-ones matrix", not of a convention.
- **At every size 4^k the same trade is exact.** [derived; table in `tab16.out`] The sum-of-four program in k passes has 7·(N/4)·k additions and (N/4)·k scalings: together N·log2 N, for every k. One scaling per addition saved, forever.

## 3. The table asked for, and the quantity that survives

`tab16.py`, exact, both programs replayed against their matrices [ran]:

| 16 points | additions | scalings | count (O) | count (G) | excess (G) | sum of log2(support × co-support) | wires on the curve support × co-support = 16 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| textbook, 4 butterfly layers | 64 | 0 | 64 | 64 | 34 | 256 | 64 of 64 |
| sum of four, two passes | 56 | 8 | 64 | 56 | 26 | 300.68 | 40 of 64 |

(Excess = gates − inputs − outputs + 2, first-wave pick 7. Support = how many inputs a wire depends on; co-support = how many outputs are needed to write it.) None of the listed candidates is conserved except the plain total. The floor note's pair-diagonal potential was not computed: it is defined for several arrays and blocks, and at 16 points on one array it has nothing to count.

**The entropy potential.** [derived; the idea is Ailon's, recalled] While a program runs in place and every step is a rotation or reflection, the program-so-far is an orthogonal matrix M. Put Φ(M) = −Σ M_ij² · log2 M_ij². It is 0 at the start and N·log2 N at the end (all entries ±1/√N), and nothing else has more.
- A butterfly (two points become their sum and difference): 2 operations, at most 2 bits, exactly 2 on fresh data. [derived, rigorous: a column with mass m on the two rows gains at most m bits, and the masses add up to 2]
- A sum-of-four gate (each of 4 points replaced by half the total minus itself): 8 operations in count (O), exactly 8 bits in both passes of the 16-point program. [derived]
- So both 64-programs make one bit per operation at every single gate. That is the conservation law, stated as a rate. Additions and scalings are not conserved separately because the potential does not see the difference; count (G) wins 1/8 because it does not charge the one operation in eight that is a scaling.
- The eigenspace and bent encoders of i-wild are not in-place rotations (they fan out), so the potential is not defined on their intermediate states; their 64 is not explained by this. I tested the nearest in-place reading instead (section 4).

## 4. The same matrix as commuting reflections: 96, not 64 [ran, exact]

A symmetric form of the transform is a reflection, so it is a product of commuting reflections along any orthogonal basis of its (−1)-eigenspace. A reflection along a vector with entries 0, ±1 on q points costs 2q in count (O) (q − 1 additions, 1 scaling, q subtractions), 2q − 1 in count (G).
`refl16.py` lists every 0/±1 vector in the eigenspace and the orthogonal bases of least total support:

| form at 16 points | eigenspace dimension | 0/±1 eigenvectors by support | cheapest orthogonal basis | count (O) | count (G) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| bent form (i-wild) | 6 | 16 of support 6, 30 of support 8, 6 of support 16 | six of support 8 | 96 | 90 |
| (J − 2I) ⊗ (J − 2I) | 6 | same | six of support 8 | 96 | 90 |
| plain transform | 8 (either sign) | 6, 32, 64, 48, 36, 10 at supports 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 16 | two of 4, six of 8 | 112 | 104 |

- By hand [derived]: each point must lie in at least two paid vectors (the diagonal entry of the projector is 3/8 or 5/8 and one vector gives 1/q), so this class costs at least 4N = 64 at 16 points; the run says the truth is 96. So the sum-of-four program (8 reflections that do NOT commute, for a reflection of rank 6) is cheaper than every eigen-decomposition. Using two more reflections than the rank is what pays.
- Anomaly: the 6-dimensional eigenspace has no sign vector of support 4, although the uncertainty bound allows 4. Its supports are 6, 8, 16 only.

## 5. The first gate above one bit per operation, and why there is no 63 yet

**The gate.** R_q = identity − (2/q)·(all ones) on q points, with free signs: "replace each point by itself minus twice the average". 2q operations in count (O).

(`bits.py`, `maxgain.py`; the last column is floating-point hill climbing over all unit vectors, so it is a lower estimate of the true maximum. The best inputs are "one point plus a faint copy of the sum".) One reflection on q points makes only about 8·log2 q bits, so large q is useless: the rate has a maximum near 1.2. **This gate can at best turn N·log2 N into about 0.83·N·log2 N in count (O).** A constant, not an order. [derived from the table]

**Greedy play at 16 points** [ran, floating point, `greedy16.out`]: always taking the best bits per operation, with reflections up to 6 points the program holds 51.5 bits after 48 operations, where the textbook holds 48; with gates up to 4 points it holds 47. Then every greedy run stalls near 56 bits, also the one that could have finished. So the lead is real for three quarters of the way and greedy says nothing about finishing.

**Two parity laws** [derived, unchecked by anyone]. The spinor norm (for a product of reflections: the product of the squared lengths of the vectors, up to squares) of the finished transform is 1: the textbook is an even number of butterflies, and the run of section 4 gives orthogonal eigenbases whose lengths multiply to 2^18 and 2^22. A butterfly has spinor norm √2·(1 + √2), a reflection R_q has q. Hence in any program of this class:
- the number of butterflies is even;
- the reflections on 3, 6 or 12 points come in even number; so do those on 5 or 10; so do those on 7 or 14. (4, 8, 9 and 16 are free.)
- For exactly two 6-point reflections along v and w with the program A between them, A must carry one of the two vectors to ± the other modulo 3 (then, and only then, the thirds cancel). [derived] That is a design rule: the stretch between the two gates has to be built for them.

**8 points: nothing below 24 in this class.** [derived + ran] Below 24 means at most 11 units (a butterfly is 1 unit, R_q is q). With the rates of the table only these multisets can reach 24 bits: {8, three butterflies}, {7, four butterflies}, {7, 4}, {6, five butterflies}, {6, 4, butterfly}, {6, 5}, {5, 5, butterfly}. Every one breaks a parity law. The last two were also enumerated in full (`n8class.py`, 1,792 and 301,056 products): none is flat; best entropies 19.03 and 20.24 of 24. Weak point: the rates in the last column of the table are estimates, not proved maxima.

**16 points: open, with a precise shape.** [derived] Below 64 means at most 31 units, 64 bits, at least 6 gates (the trace of a flat orthogonal matrix is at most 4, so it differs from the identity in rank at least 6), and the parity laws. That leaves, for example, three 8-point reflections with four butterflies (56 operations, at most 65.8 bits) or two 8-point reflections with three sum-of-four gates and two butterflies (60 operations). Of the gates above one bit per operation, only those on 8 or 9 points may appear singly.
**Annealing at 16 points** [ran, floating point; `anneal_ctl.out`, `anneal_62.out`, `linux/*.out`]. Maximise the entropy over programs of butterflies and sum gates within a budget of operations.
- Control, budget 64, gates of 2 and 4 points: all four runs reach 64.0000 bits inside 45 s. Three of them are seven sum-of-four gates and four butterflies (56 + 8 = 64), a mixed 64-program that was not on the brief's list.
- Budget 62, gates of 2, 4, 5, 6, 8 points (5 runs of 60 s on the Mac, 6 of 240 s on Linux, 2 more with 2, 4, 8): the best is **62.0000**, reached four times, always by seven sum-of-four gates and three butterflies with no larger gate. Runs that kept a 5-, 6- or 8-point gate ended between 53.6 and 60.6.
- Budget 60 (5 runs): best 56.87. Budget 56 with 2- and 8-point gates only (3 runs): 49.97, the same value three times, by three 8-point reflections and four butterflies. So that shape is far from flat although the count allows it.
- **The regularity: the best entropy found with B operations is B** (B = 62, 64), and it is reached without the efficient gates. The 5- to 8-point gates lead early (greedy: 51.5 against 48 at 48 operations) and lose late. [guess] The reason is the arithmetic above: their bits sit off the dyadic grid (entries 2/3 and 1/3) and must be brought back before the matrix can be flat; the parity laws say they can only be brought back in tuned pairs.
- A negative from annealing is weak: the control passes, but the space at 56 to 62 is much larger than at 64.

**The frontier curve at 16 points** [ran, floating point, one run per cell, so lower estimates; `frontier.out`, `greedy16.out`]: the most entropy found with at most B operations.

- **A small theorem** [derived]: a butterfly makes at most 2 bits for 2 operations and a sum-of-four gate at most 8 bits for 8 operations (4 points cannot hold more than 4·log2 4). So a program of these two gates makes at most one bit per count-(O) operation, and needs N·log2 N operations at every N. The first row is that bound, met exactly at every B. This is the conservation law as a theorem, for the gate set that the textbook and the sum-of-four program are made of (the other reorganisations on the brief's list were not rebuilt here).
- The second row starts above the diagonal and comes back to it near B = 54, about 85 % of the way. (The cells at 56 and 60 are annealing failures to do as well as the first row; the bracketed cells were reached by 2- and 4-point gates.) **The growth law of the lead is the result: it rises to about +4.7 bits at half-way and is gone before the end.** A program below 64 would be a curve that keeps a lead of at least 1 bit at B = 63.

## 6. The published unit against this law (part c of the brief)

- [read, `R/notes/for-network-coding-readers.md`, `R/README.md` section 6, `R/notes/e8-results.json`] The engine's game: W arrays; a *move* is the transform along one of m directions on one array and is the only thing that costs; additions of whole arrays "standing in the same frame" are free. Plain schedule W·m moves. Fourth result: W = 1,263, m = 45, 56,715 moves in place of 56,835: D = 120 = 4·120 − 5·72 (each exchange between twin pairs saves 4 moves; the scratch copies take back 360).
- [ran, `cert_count.py`, read-only on `tools/certificate/gcert1-e8-r783.json.gz`] One invocation holds 1,462 + 1,678 + 736 = 3,876 whole-array addition terms; five invocations about 19,380.
- [derived, rough: ignores scalings by 1/2, relabellings and the recursion] With f digit columns an array has 2^(45f) points, a move costs c·f operations per point (c = 1 for a plain butterfly layer, about 2.5 for OpenAI's kernel written out), an addition 1 per point. One level of the unit costs c·f·56,715 + 19,380 per point against c·f·56,835. **Count (O) first dips at f > 19,380/(120·c): 162 columns at c = 1, 65 at c = 2.5, that is arrays of 2^7,300 or 2^2,900 points, 1,263 of them.**
- **The step that is not conserved.** The whole-array addition. It is a shear (array A becomes A + B), not a rotation; it makes no bits and has no rate. The unit never makes a bit faster than the textbook: every bit is made inside moves, at the textbook's rate. It makes 0.21 % fewer bits' worth of moves because sums of arrays are transformed once and shared. The law of section 3 is a law about one array handled by rotations; the unit has many arrays and non-rotations.
- So the answer to the brief's question: **the 64-programs have in common that each is one array and every step is a rotation making one bit per operation. The published unit lacks both.** The law is not about 16 points being too small and not about count (O) as such. Inside one array with rotations and single sums it bends at a sum of five points and is bounded by a constant; with several arrays and shears it fails only at astronomical size, but there it changes the exponent.

## 7. What I learned

1. "64" is N·log2 N bits of one potential, made at one bit per operation by every program on the list. Additions and scalings are two currencies for the same bit.
2. The count-(G) record 7/8 is exactly the one scaling in eight operations that count (G) does not charge. It is not a second mechanism.
3. A scaling debt cannot be carried: the factor 2 is between two uses of one wire. Proved at 4 points by exhaustion.
4. The first gate that beats one bit per operation is a reflection through the sum of 5 to 8 points. Its rate is bounded near 1.2, so it is a constant-factor door.
5. Commuting reflections (eigen-decompositions) cost 96 at 16 points, more than the textbook; the cheap programs use more reflections than the rank and they do not commute.
6. Arithmetic (spinor norm) forces the sum gates on 5, 6 and 7 points to come in pairs that are tuned to each other modulo 5, 3 and 7. This is a reason why no 63 fell out of casual play, and a rule for building one.
6b. In annealing the best entropy reachable with B operations was exactly B, at B = 62 and 64, and the efficient gates were never part of the best program: they lead early and lose late. All three mixed 64-programs it found have an even number of butterflies (four), as the parity law says.
7. Anything that changes the order must contain steps with no bounded bit rate: shears, fan-out, several arrays. The published unit is of that kind; the single-array 64-programs are not.

## 8. Mutations (untested unless marked)

- **M1. Shears inside one array.** A shear has no bit rate, so the potential does not bound it. Ailon's later theorem, as I recall it [recalled, not re-read]: a speed-up by a factor b forces intermediate states whose condition number grows with b. Read backwards: measure the condition number of the published unit's intermediate states per level; then look for the smallest single-array program whose intermediate state is NOT orthogonal and whose count (O) is below N·log2 N. The sum-of-four gate written as "form the total, halve it, subtract" already passes through a non-orthogonal state (4 points and their total): at 4 points that buys nothing (section 2), at 5 to 8 points it buys the 11 to 20 %.
- **M2. A reflection through k sums at once.** One sum on q points makes about 8·log2 q bits. A reflection through k orthogonal sums that share partial sums costs about 2q + (cost of the k sums); with the k sums taken as a subgroup of sign patterns it falls apart into small sum gates [derived], so the new gates are the non-subgroup choices. Tabulate bits per operation for k = 2, 3 on 8 to 16 points. If the rate grows with k without bound, that is an order-changing gate; my guess is that it is bounded, and the table would show by what.
- **M3. Build a 62 at 16 points on purpose.** Use the parity laws as the design: two 6-point reflections along v and w, and between them a program A of sum-of-four gates and butterflies with A·v = ±w modulo 3. Search A first (small: it only has to move one vector modulo 3), then complete. Or three 8-point reflections and four butterflies (56 operations; the necessary conditions allow it with 3 % to spare).
- **M4. The same potential for several arrays.** For W arrays the finished state has W·N·log2 N bits. Define the potential on the joint state of all arrays and track it through the published unit: the shears must be exactly where bits are "copied" rather than made. The number to compute is bits created per move, array by array; the 120 skipped moves should show up as arrays that receive bits they did not make. That would be the saving "in a form small enough to exaggerate".
- **M5. Sizes where the 6-point gate fits.** The gate of 5 and 6 points is the efficient one, and powers of two do not divide by 5 or 6. Order 12 is out for a plain reason [derived]: a flat orthogonal matrix of order 12 has entries ±1/√12, and neither butterflies (√2) nor sum gates (rational) produce √3. The smallest order where a rational flat matrix, the 6-point gate and a Hadamard matrix all fit is 36 (entries ±1/6; N·log2 N = 186.1 bits). Question: is some Hadamard matrix of order 36 a product of 6-point reflections and butterflies in fewer than 186 operations? It is a different matrix from the transform, but the same law, at the first size built for the gate; a yes would give the pattern to carry back to 16 or 64.

## 9. What is weak here

- Nobody has checked the hand arguments (count (G) lower bound at 6 gates, the sign argument in `o4.py`, the parity laws, the mod-3 rule, the "at least two paid vectors" count).
- The entropy bounds use hill-climbed maxima for reflections on 5 to 9 points. If the true maxima are higher, the 8-point exclusion has a gap and the 0.83 moves.
- Everything in section 5 after the first table is floating point. A flat matrix found that way would be a candidate only; none was found below 64.
- The class (butterflies and single-sum reflections, in place) is narrow. "Nothing below 24 at 8 points" is a statement about that class, not about count (O).
- Section 6 prices one level of the unit with textbook children and ignores the recursion; the dip size is an order of magnitude, not a theorem.
- Annealing negatives mean little: the control passes at 64, but the space at 56 to 62 is far larger.
