# e-7-halftouch: one half-length call plus a sparse remainder

> Editor's note (11 October 2026): a critic corrected the reading of this report's result. Its obstruction bounds the average row of A, not each row. And the place it finds at one half-size call plus one quarter-size call, where "the obstruction is gone", is only where one argument stops; it is not evidence that such a recursion exists ([b-2](b-2.md); the line "Taken back or corrected" in [the second note](NOTE.md)).

**First line, as asked: no circuit below the textbook count and no growth law below N log N came out of this.** What came out: exact one-call identities at 4, 8, 16, 32, 64 points; a reading of what they are; five small lemmas that say what any such identity must look like; and one place where the floor note's impossibility argument provably stops applying (a half-length call plus a quarter-length call).

10 October 2026, 22:12 to 22:50 EDT. Private play, one agent. Nothing reviewed, nothing in Lean. Marks: [read] [ran] [derived] [guess]. Everything marked [ran] was replayed with exact fractions by `replay2.py`; the searches themselves use double precision and only propose candidates.

## Words

N = 2^k points. H or H_N: the plain transform, entries +1 and -1. *Child*: one call to a shorter transform; C is the child written as a matrix (H_(N/2), or several shorter transforms side by side); m is the total length of the children. The identity looked for is

    H_N = S + A * C * B

B (m rows, N columns) prepares the child's input, A (N rows, m columns) spreads the child's output, S is the *remainder*. *Sparse* here means: at most a non-zero entries in every row of A, at most b in every column of B (so A and B have at most a*N and b*N entries and cost O(N) gates), and S with few entries. I did NOT also limit the columns of A and the rows of B; that is a wider class than the one the floor note tried (it limited the columns of A to 3, which the eigenvector argument forbids outright). *nnz*: number of non-zero entries. Gate count (G): a gate is any a*u + b*v.

Why it matters: if S, A, B stay sparse at every N with ONE half-length child, the cost obeys T(N) = T(N/2) + cN, which is linear. The same holds for any children of total length rho*N with rho < 1 fixed, for example a half plus a quarter.

## 2. The 16-point identity, and what it is

File `linux/p16a.txt`. H_16 = S + A * H_8 * B exactly, with
- S = 4 times a signed partial relabelling: one entry +4 or -4 in each row and column except row 3 and column 10, 15 entries;
- A with at most TWO entries per row, each +1/2 or -1/2 (one row has a single -1), 31 entries;
- B with at most 3 entries per column, all +1 or -1, 46 entries;
- H - S has rank 7. A second run (`p16b.txt`) has 16 entries in S and rank 6.

[derived, by looking] This is the fact already in the notes: after one relabelling the 16-point transform is 4 * R1 * R2, two commuting reflections (R = identity minus twice a projection of rank 4), because the relabelled 4-point transform is 2I - u u^T with u = (1,-1,-1,-1). Expanding, 4 R1 R2 x = 4x - 2 (p1 tensor u) - 2 (u tensor p2) + (u.p1)(u tensor u), where p1 and p2 are the 4 + 4 signed block sums of x. Fold the last term into p1 and p2 and every output is "4 times one input, plus half of one number, plus half of another": two entries per row of A, a relabelling for S, rank 6. That is the shape the search found (16 entries and rank 6; dropping one entry of S gives 15 and rank 7). I matched shape, values and ranks; I did not match the two entry by entry. **So the 16-point hit is the two-level 4-point trick written in one shot, not a new mechanism, and the 8-point child in the middle is not doing a transform's worth of work** (the no-child column gets 16).

[ran, `reflect.py`, exact] At N = 4, 16, 64 the relabelled transform IS 2^j times a product of j commuting reflections; the remainder 2^j * (relabelling) has exactly N entries, of size sqrt(N); the rest has rank (N - sqrt N)/2 = 1, 6, 28. **So the remainder itself is linear at every N = 4^j. All the cost sits in A and B.** Written in one shot the rest needs, per output [derived]: 2^j - 1 partial sums (1, 3, 7) with 5^j - 4^j numbers through the middle (1, 9, 61); or, folding the smaller sums into the larger, only j sums per output but j * N/4 numbers through the middle (48 at 64 points, more than the 32 a half-length child carries). The second form is the textbook's N log N wires in one layer. Squeezing the middle down to N/2 numbers is what costs: Lemma 4 says at least N^(1/4)/2.83 entries per row, so this family costs between N^1.25 and N^1.5.

## 3. Lemma 1: what any such identity forces [derived; checked exactly on the 8- and 16-point results by `analyze.py`]

If H = S + A*C*B with A having m columns, let K be the set of vectors c with c^T A = 0. K has dimension at least N - m, and

    H c = S^T c   for every c in K.

(One line: c^T H = c^T S + (c^T A) C B.) Nothing about C is used. The mirror statement holds for the vectors killed by B.

In words: **the part of the transform that the child call does not reach is the transform on K, and on K the transform has to be the sparse matrix S itself.** K is cut out by the columns of A, which are sparse: it is what coding theory calls a code with sparse checks. So a one-call identity is exactly: *a half-dimensional space with sparse checks on which the transform acts as a sparse matrix.* That is the cheapest description of the unreached part that I can give, and it is the answer to "what would a solution have to look like".

Consequences [derived]:
- Every non-zero c in K has at least sqrt(N / s) non-zero entries, s = the most entries in a row of S (a vector and its transform cannot both be small; S^T c has at most s * nnz(c) entries). At 16 points: bound 4, observed 4.
- The squares of S's entries add up to at least (N - m) * N. With about N entries in S they are of size sqrt(N). At 16 points: bound 128, observed 240. This is the "big coefficients" of Morgenstern's theorem showing up in one place, and it means S changes with N: no fixed unit.

## 4. Three more lemmas: where sparse fails, and where the argument stops

**Lemma 2. Two per row is never enough [derived; the graph step is standard and sketched].** If every row of A has at most 2 entries, then nnz(S) is at least about N^2 / (4 log^2 N), whatever C and B are. Reason: the rows of A are then the N edges of a graph on N/2 points; such a graph has two cycles sharing a point inside O(log N) edges; those t rows of A are dependent, giving c in K with t entries; by Lemma 1 the rows of S at those places carry at least N/t entries; remove the heaviest and repeat N/4 times. [ran] with 2/2: 3, 12, 48 at 4, 8, 16 points, which is exactly 3 N^2 / 16 each time, and 192, 768 at 32, 64 by doubling (the free search at 32 points reached 204): quadratic, as the lemma says. The same argument works for children of any total length below N. The 16-point identity of section 2 has two per row in A, so its shape cannot continue.

**Lemma 3. Three per row is exactly where that argument stops [derived].** With three entries per row the pattern of A can be an *expander* (every small set of rows touches at least as many columns), and then no short dependency exists. So a solution's A must be an expander up to sets of size sqrt(N/s). This is the "3" of the proposed experiment, and it explains why 3 is the first interesting number.

**Lemma 4. The slack law for remainders that are a relabelling [derived once, not re-derived by anyone].** Let S = sqrt(N) times a signed relabelling Q with H Q^T symmetric (the eigenvector case S = sqrt(N) * I and the reflection case of section 2 are both of this kind), and let the middle carry m = rho * N numbers. Take any r + j columns of A, r = m - rank(H - S): their span contains j independent vectors w with "H Q^T w = -sqrt(N) w", all living on the rows T those columns touch; a space of such vectors has dimension at most |T|^2 / N; and the lightest r + j columns touch at most (r + j) * a / rho rows. Hence

    a  >=  rho / (2 * sqrt(rho - 1/2 + 1/(2 sqrt N)))      (roughly; the last term only for the best relabelling)

Read it at three places:
- rho = 1/2, S = sqrt(N) * identity: a is unbounded. This is the floor note's "does not exist".
- rho = 1/2 with the best relabelling (slack sqrt(N)/2): a >= N^(1/4) / 2.83. So this family costs at least N^1.25 and at most N^1.5 (section 2). **With a = 3 it is excluded only beyond N = 5,184. No search at 16 to 64 points can see this wall**; the small-size successes above are exactly what the lemma permits.
- **rho = 3/4 (one half-length child plus one quarter-length child, cost T(N) = T(N/2) + T(N/4) + cN, still linear): a >= 0.75. The obstruction is gone.** The floor note's eigenvector argument says nothing against "H = sqrt(N) * I + sparse * (half child and quarter child) * sparse". The columns of A no longer have to be eigenvectors; they only have to span a space that contains the eigenspace, with N/4 dimensions to spare.

**Lemma 5. The doubling rule and the volume law [ran exactly at 32 and 64 points by `tensor.py`; the law derived].** If H_N0 = S0 + A0 * H_(N0/2) * B0, then for every M

    H_(M N0) = (H_M tensor S0) + (I tensor A0) * H_(M N0 / 2) * (I tensor B0).

One half-length child still suffices and A, B keep their sparsity, but the remainder is now H_M tensor S0: as many transforms of length M as S0 has non-zero rows. That number is at least rank(S0), which is at least N0/2. So a FIXED identity reused at every scale has child volume at least N/2 + N/2 = N: never below the textbook. With the 16-point identity (15 rows) the volume is 23N/16 and the cost N^1.19. **Conservation law: (length of the child) + (rank of the remainder) >= N; a fixed identity pays the remainder's rank in calls.** This is the half-touch version of "64 came back in every count". The only way through is a remainder that is sparse in its own right at every N, which means S, A, B that change with N: one of the floor note's hatches ("units that change with n"), now with a precise job description (Lemma 1).

## 5. The two questions, answered as far as I got

**First question: how does the smallest remainder grow?** With one half-length child and at most 3 per row and column: 3, 6, 15 at 4, 8, 16 points, then at most 60 and 240 (doubling) at 32 and 64. Up to 16 points it looks like N, but that is an artefact: (i) at 4 and 8 points "3 of N/2" is hardly a restriction and the number is just the rank count; (ii) at 16 points the hit is the two-reflection structure, which needs sqrt(N) per row in general; (iii) a budget of 7N entries exceeds the textbook's N log2 N wires until N = 128, so nothing below 128 points can separate "N" from "N log N" by counting entries. The laws I can state: two per row gives N^2 (ran and derived); a relabelling remainder gives between N^1.25 and N^1.5 (derived); a fixed identity doubled gives N^2/17 for the remainder and N^1.19 or worse for the whole cost (ran, derived). For three per row with a general sparse remainder there is **no bound and no construction**: open.

**Second question: can the remainder be of the same shape one level down?** Charged to a fixed identity, no: Lemma 5, the volume is at least N. Taken as "half child plus quarter child plus sparse remainder" it is open and, by Lemma 4, it is the first place where the known obstruction vanishes. [ran] 3 at 8 points; 13 and 68 at 16 and 32 points are unconverged upper bounds.

**Which part does one half-size call fail to reach?** The transform on K (Lemma 1). In the language of positions and frequencies: the transform turns "position z, frequency w" into "position w, frequency z". A sparse matrix can do that swap only where it does not have to tell frequencies apart, that is on vectors balanced between the two (eigenvector-like, supports of size sqrt(N)); telling sqrt(N) frequencies apart on one block is itself a transform [derived, for the family built on one self-dual code; not for general K].

## 6. The better question this turned into

**Q\*. For which fraction e > 0 is there, at every N, a subspace K of dimension e*N such that (i) K is cut out by sparse checks (a matrix A with O(1) entries per row whose columns span the orthogonal complement of K) and (ii) the transform restricted to K equals a matrix with O(1) entries per row and column?** Then H = S + A * Y with Y a map to (1 - e)N numbers, and the remaining job is to make Y out of children of total length (1 - e)N.

- (ii) alone is a known kind of question, *rigidity*: how many entries of H must change to bring its rank down to (1 - e)N. Known to me: N changes for rank N/2 (N - sqrt(N)/2 with relabelling; [ran] 6 at 8 points, below N). Whether O(N) changes can reach rank N/4 or 3N/8 I do not know, and the general lower bound N^2/(4 rank) does not forbid it [read in the notes; not checked in the papers tonight].
- (i) and (ii) together are a code question: an LDPC-type code of positive rate on which the Fourier transform is local. Quantum error-correcting codes of the "CSS" kind are built from pairs of sparse-check codes exchanged by this same transform [guess: that literature may contain exactly the object, or a proof that it does not exist].
- At e = 1/4 with S = sqrt(N) * identity the question reads: **is there a quarter-dimensional subspace of the +sqrt(N) eigenspace of H that has sparse checks?** Lemma 4 does not forbid it. I have no example beyond 16 points and no proof.

## 7b. The wall, as a possible lemma, and what to try next

**Wall (conjecture; proved here only for two entries per row, and for relabelling remainders with one half-length child beyond N = 5,184).** For fixed a, b, s and fixed rho < 1 there is no identity H_N = S + A*C*B at large N with C of size at most rho*N, at most a entries per row of A, b per column of B, s per row and column of S. By Lemma 1 this is the same as: *no subspace K of dimension (1 - rho)*N, cut out by checks with boundedly many entries per row, on which the transform equals a matrix with boundedly many entries per row and column.* A proof would be a floor theorem for every "one layer of children between two sparse layers" program, a class the floor note does not cover (its cheap steps keep a diagonal; these do not). A counterexample would be a linear-size transform.

Mutations, most promising first:
1. **Half child plus quarter child, remainder sqrt(N) times the identity.** Lemma 4 leaves it open and nothing else here touches it. The exact question: at 64 points, is there a 16-dimensional subspace of the +8 eigenspace of H_64 whose orthogonal complement is spanned by 48 vectors forming a matrix with at most 3 (then 4) entries per row? This is a finite question about one explicit 32-dimensional space and can be attacked as a sparse-basis problem, without any search over A, B, C. It is only half of the job (the 48 numbers must then come out of the two children), but an empty answer would close the mutation and a hit would be the first object of the kind Q* asks for.
2. **Children in series.** I only tried children side by side (one layer). A program that feeds the half-length child's output, sparsely mixed, into the quarter-length child costs the same and is a larger class. Lemma 1 changes: the unreached space is cut out by A and by a second condition involving the first child. Nobody has written that down.
3. **Rigidity at linear rank.** Can O(N) changed entries bring the rank of H_N down to 3N/8 or N/4? [ran] 6 changes reach rank N/2 at 8 points, 3 reach rank 6. If the answer is no at rank (1/2 - e)N for every e, then a relabelling-sized remainder never helps beyond half and the whole idea is confined to rho >= 1/2.
4. **Read the code literature for the object of Lemma 1**: sparse-check codes on which the Walsh-Hadamard transform is local (pairs of sparse-check codes exchanged by the transform are the raw material of quantum CSS codes). One evening with the right survey.
5. **A better search.** Mine fails its calibration from 12 middle numbers on. A search that plants the structure (A an explicit expander, entries from 0, +1, -1, then exact linear algebra for the rest) would be faster and could reach 64 points.
