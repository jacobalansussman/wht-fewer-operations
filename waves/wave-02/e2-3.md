Words. N = 2^k points; the outputs are the rows of the +-1 matrix H. A *form* is a linear combination of the inputs; its
*support* is the set of inputs it contains. For a set S of stored forms, d(t) = (fewest forms of S that combine to output t)
minus one; the *potential* P = sum of d(t); a gate *drains* what it lowers P by (at most N). A *centre* is a +-1 vector
that is not an output; *bent* = at the same distance from every output.

## 1. The builder

`drain.c` (C, exact integers, forms kept up to scale). d(t) is replaced by an honest upper bound: a greedy "pursuit" writes
t as (a few stored forms) + (a few inputs) and cost(t) = forms + inputs - 1. So U = gates + P is the size of a real circuit at
every moment, and `replay.py` re-derives every stored form with fractions and checks each output lies in the span it names.
Each step: all pairs of stored forms, coefficient ratios +-1, +-2, +-1/2; a one-step screen for all, the full pursuit for
the top K; add the form with the largest drain, ties at random. Options: preloaded gates (a seed), early stop, a report of
how many outputs each candidate helps. No 40-gate cap. All numbers below are replayed exactly [ran].

**The plateau [derived, and seen].** The first 3N/4 gates all drain N/2 or 3N/4: the builder makes N/4 "quads", a
(1,1,1,-1) pattern on four inputs in three gates, each of which matches every output on three of its four inputs. After

**Gates draining N: none in any of my runs.** The cap N per gate is never met; the early cap is N/2 (first gate) and 3N/4.

## 3. The 21-gate circuit read as a star

`h8_21gates_drain.txt`. One centre c = (1,1,1,-1 | 1,1,-1,1), bent on each half, built by a chain inside each half (7 gates);
the chain passes through one "triple" per half for free ((1,1,0,-1) and (1,1,0,1) on three inputs). The 8 outputs come in
4 pairs. For each pair: one "stop" (c with one input flipped, 1 gate), one output = stop with one more input flipped (1 gate),
one output = stop with a triple of the other half flipped (1 gate if that triple is a node of the centre's own tree, 2 if
not). Two triples are free, two cost a gate: **7 + 4*3 + 2 = 21** [derived from the circuit, replayed]. So the brief's hope
"the forms the centre's own tree passes through can be chosen to be the corrections" is exactly what the machine found at
8 points, and it is worth exactly one gate. Inside this family 20 would need two different triples of one half for free,
which a tree on four inputs cannot give [derived]. None of the 4-point transforms of the halves appears in the circuit: it
does not factor through the tensor structure. Of its 21 gates, 7 add forms with disjoint supports and 14 cancel (section 6).

## 4. Experiment (b): after the centre, which form helps every output?

[ran, `r16/seed_bentchain_s1.jsonl`] 16 points, S = inputs + the 15-gate tree of the bent centre (-1)^(x0 x1 + x2 x3):
P = 76. Of all one-gate candidates, 52 help one output, 36 help two, 18 help three, 6 help four, **none helps more than
4 of 16**. (One-gate forms with ratios +-1, +-2, +-1/2, under the pursuit bound; forms needing two gates were not scored.)
The profile after the seed is 4, 3, 3, 3, ...: the "halving" appears at once.

**Why: the Parseval drain law [derived; checked on random sets at 4, 16, 64 points, `tower2.py`].** After a centre c, a
form that copies c on a set L of inputs helps output x by exactly |<c on L, x>| - 1 (matches minus mismatches minus the form
itself), and the sum over all outputs of <c on L, x>^2 is N |L|, whatever L is. So a form of support size s can help every
output by sqrt(s) - 1 at most; if it matches some outputs on all of L (a help of s - 1 each) there can be at most N/s
of them. The budget N |L| is all there is.

**The square-root law for many centres [derived].** With m centres and single inputs as corrections, each output using its
nearest centre: P >= N (N - sqrt(N m)) / 2. One bent centre is the best single centre (it maximises the total drain,
N^2/2 + N^1.5/2 - N, for N - 1 gates). To halve what is left you need m = N/4 centres. So m good forms cut the potential
by the factor (1 - sqrt(m/N)): far less than 2^m. In the Hamming world the supply runs out after the first centre.

## 5. Experiments (c) and (d): the peel tower and the read-out puzzle

Work in the form H = (J - 2I) x ... x (J - 2I), J the 4-by-4 all-ones matrix: the same transform after relabelling and sign
changes. Points are words in {0,1,2,3}^k; entry = (-1)^(number of places where two words agree). The all-ones vector is
then a bent centre, and output x = sum over sets A of coordinates of (-2)^|A| * (sum of the inputs agreeing with x on A).

(c) [ran, exact, `tower2.out`] **16 points: after the centre the row weight is 6; peeling the 8 row and column sums (rank 7
with the centre) leaves weight 1** (target was 2). **64 points: 28, then 10 after the 12 plane sums (rank 10; target was 12),
then 1 after the 48 line sums (rank 37).** E = (J - H)/2 is the incidence matrix of a design: row weight 6 and overlap 2
at 16, 28 and 12 at 64. But the tower stores 5^k numbers and reads each output from 2^k of them: it is the textbook again.

(d) **The 16-point version of the puzzle is solved** [ran, exact]: 4 row sums, 4 column sums, the total, and the 4 forms
"total minus twice a row sum": 31 gates; every output = P_i - 2 C_j + 4 x_ij, a combination of 3 stored numbers (24 numbers
are read: 16 inputs, 4 column sums, 4 P_i). Finishing costs 2 gates per output: 63 in all, not below 56.
**At 64 points the same recipe misses**: 125 = 5^3 stored numbers (under 128), but 183 gates and 8 terms per output.
What stops it [derived + ran]: H + 8I has rank exactly 28 (H - 8I: 36), so every output is -8 times its own input plus a
vector in a 28-dimensional space, and those 64 vectors are equiangular (all inner products +-16 against squared length 112).
Read-out from two families "p_a + q_b" spans at most 16 dimensions, three families of four at most 12; a parameter count
(each output on a line through two stored points of a 27-dimensional projective space: 26 conditions each, 27 freedoms per
stored form) says about 60 stored forms are needed in that space unless they sit in special position.
At 16 points the space has dimension 6 and rows plus columns fill it; at 64 no grid does.

## 6. What the failure is made of: a theorem

Call a gate *clean* if its two arguments have disjoint supports, *cancelling* otherwise.

**Theorem [derived tonight; short proof below; I did not search the literature, it may be folklore]. A circuit for the
N-point Walsh-Hadamard transform in which every gate is clean has at least N log2 N gates, in count (G), with any
coefficients. The textbook is exactly optimal among them.**
Proof. Fix an output x. Unfolding the circuit from x gives a binary tree whose leaves are the N inputs, each once (supports
are disjoint at every gate and x has full support); its N - 1 inner nodes are distinct gates, and the form at a node with
support L is a multiple of x restricted to L. A gate with support L of size s can therefore sit in the trees of only those
outputs that agree on L up to sign: at most N / 2^(affine dimension of L) <= N/s of them. Count pairs (output, node) with
weight s: each gate contributes at most s * N/s = N. Each tree contributes the sum of its subtree sizes = the sum of the
depths of its N leaves >= N log2 N. So N * (gates) >= N * N log2 N. QED.

Consequences.
- **Every gate saved below N log N is paid for by cancellation**: a gate that adds two forms sharing inputs and kills part
  of them. Counts [ran]: 4 points: 3 clean + 4 cancelling (saves 1). 8 points, 21 gates: 7 + 14 (saves 3). The 56 at 16
  points: 24 + 32 (saves 8) [derived]. The star at 16: 15 + 37 among its 52 built gates, 62 with the 10 finishing gates (saves 2).
- A clean form of support s can serve at most N/s outputs; that is Parseval again and it is the log. A cancelling form (a
  centre) can serve all N. In the brick four cancelling gates save one gate. A linear program has O(N) gates in all and
  must save N log N - O(N): its cancelling gates must save about log N each.
- [ran, `cdepth.py`] *Cancellation depth* of a gate = the largest number of cancelling gates on a path from an input to it.
  All 180 finished 21-gate circuits the builder kept have exactly 7 clean gates (one centre tree, N - 1 gates) and 14
  cancelling ones, and every output at depth 2, 3 or 4 (largest depth 3 in 163 of them, 4 in 17). The 4-point brick: 3 clean,
  depth 1. The other 21-gate circuit of tonight (e-2-density's listing, counted by hand): 9 clean, 12 cancelling, outputs
  at depths 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 3, 4. Brick towers: depth (log2 N)/2 and a saving of exactly (N/4) per level [derived].
  **[guess, fits every circuit I have: 7, 21, 22, 56, the brick towers] a circuit of cancellation depth D saves at most
  (N/4) * D gates below N log2 N.** It is tight for brick towers and slack for the 21 (saves 3, depth 3). If true, a
  linear program needs cancellation depth about 4 log2 N: every output must pass through more cancelling gates than the
  textbook has layers. The theorem above is the case D = 0.
- Prefix sums are the model: interval sums read as a difference of two overlapping stored forms. The 7-gate brick
  (output = total - 2 x_i) is the only place the transform is known to do this.

### 6b. What a gate that helps every output must look like: three regimes [derived, from the theorem plus two things recalled]

- No cancellation: exactly N log2 N (the theorem above). For the subset-sum matrix over GF(2) the same kind of statement
  is in the literature as "cancellation-free circuits" (Boyar and Find, about 2013, (1/2) N log N for that matrix)
  [recalled, not re-read]; I have not seen it stated for the transform.
- Cancellation with bounded coefficients (|a| + |b| <= 3 at every gate and the outputs produced at their true scale,
  entries +-1, as in the brick, the 21 and the 56): Morgenstern's volume argument still gives at least
  (N/2) log2 N / log2 3 = 0.32 N log2 N [recalled + derived]. (If forms are allowed to shrink and be rescaled at the end,
  the rescaling is the large coefficient; the statement needs a scale to be fixed.) The brick tower is at 0.875. So bounded cancellation can win a constant factor and nothing else, and every star, centre and peel in this
  report lives in that regime. That is why the supply of good forms ran out each time.
- So **a gate that helps a constant fraction of all outputs, again and again, has to be a cancelling gate whose
  coefficients grow with N**: the difference of two long, nearly parallel forms, each of them far from every output
  (a bent centre has squared cosine exactly 1/N with every output, no more than a single input has), whose difference is
  short and close to many outputs. In the potential's language: forms that look useless one at a time. Steepest drain can
  never pick them, which is the precise reason this builder cannot be the tool for linear size [derived].
- The one identity in the brief with coefficients as large as N is H = Z D Z^T (Z = subset sums, D diagonal with entries
  (-2)^(size of the subset)). There the growth is free (a diagonal) and all the cost is in Z, whose cancellation-free cost
  is (1/2) N log N while its cost with subtraction is, as far as I recall, open. That is where this idea points: the
  `zeta` pick, with subtraction used as cancellation between long partial sums, not as inclusion-exclusion.

## 7. The better question: layers of block reflections

The cancelling gadget of the brick is a reflection: J - 2I = -2 (I - 2 v v^T) with v = (1,1,1,1)/2. On d points
I - 2 v v^T costs 2d - 1 gates for any v. [derived] It has entries of equal size only for d = 4 (need 2/d = 1 - 2/d).
So take *layers*: a partition of the inputs into blocks, one reflection per block, any diagonal scaling between layers
(free in count G). A layer with blocks of size d costs under 2N. The brick tower is log4 N layers with d = 4.
Necessary conditions for a product of m layers with block sizes d_1..d_m to be a Hadamard matrix [derived; uses the
rigidity bound "changing fewer than N^2/(4r) entries cannot bring the rank to r", recalled from Kashin-Razborov, not re-read]:
- spreading: the product of the d_j is at least N;
- rank: the sum of the 1/d_j is at least 1/4 (the product is a diagonal plus rank sum of N/d_j);
- in general, for every subset S of the layers: 4 * (product of d_j outside S) * (sum over S of 1/d_j) >= 1.
With equal blocks this gives m >= d/4 and d^m >= N, so **at least about log N / log log N layers; the brick tower uses
log4 N. Bounded-size or polynomial-size blocks with one reflection each can never give linear size** (m >= d/4), and in a
grid arrangement (every pair of points joined by one path through the layers) flatness forces every d_j = 4 exactly
[derived for two layers; guess for more].
The open window is small and concrete: blocks of size about 4m in m layers, arranged so that pairs of points are joined
by many paths (not a grid), e.g. N = 256, four layers of 16-blocks (four parallel classes of lines of the affine plane of
order 16: rank bound met with equality), cost 4 * 16 * 31 = 1,984 against the brick tower's 1,792: no gain there. Four
layers of 16-blocks cost 7.75 N at any N up to 16^4, the brick tower 1.75 N per two bits (8.75 N at 1,024 points, 14 N at
65,536), so from about 1,024 points on the window would pay if it is not empty. [guess] It is empty: the rigidity bound
it leans on is not believed tight. Beyond it, the gadget must have rank more than one per block at cost O(block): that is the transform itself at
block size, so the question closes on itself. The classical family that does meet the rank count with commuting layers is
the net construction: sqrt(N)/2 parallel classes of lines of an affine plane of order sqrt(N) give a symmetric Hadamard
matrix I - 2(sum of line projectors) + J/sqrt(N) [derived; it is the rows-and-columns picture at 16], cost N^1.5.
