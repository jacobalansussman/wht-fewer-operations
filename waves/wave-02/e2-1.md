# e2-1 `dictionary`: route through a non-abelian group and choose the free bijection

**First line, as asked: no circuit below the textbook count and no growth law below N log N was found.** What came out instead is (1) a complete answer at 8 points, (2) two short theorems that say what the "exact additivity" is a theorem *about*, and (3) a better question.

Marks: [ran] computed tonight (files at the end), [derived] worked by hand here, [read] from tonight's notes, [guess].

Words. N = 2^k points; an *address* is a string of k bits. H: the plain ±1 transform; its rows are the *characters*. *Flat*: a coset of a subgroup of the addresses (a sub-cube in some basis). *Coset character*: a character kept on one flat and set to zero elsewhere; its *level* is log2 of the number of points of the flat (a single point: level 0; a whole character: level k). *Dictionary* σ: the bijection from the elements of the other group G to our addresses. *Bridge*: the matrix B with H = B × (transform of G) × (dictionary). *Monomial*: a matrix with one non-zero entry per row and column.

## 1. What the bridge is [derived]

Row y of the bridge is the G-transform of the ±1 function "character y read through the dictionary" (g ↦ χ_y(σ(g))). So the bridge is cheap exactly when every character, read through σ, is simple *for G*. For the extraspecial group of order 2^(2m+1), written as triples (a, b, c) with the big representation (−1)^c X^a Z^b, the transform of G is: one butterfly layer on c; on the sums, the ordinary transform in (a, b); on the differences, the ordinary transform in b only, separately for each a. With the dictionary that keeps the bits, this is literally the textbook with the layers "a, on the difference half" left out, and the bridge is those layers. **That is all the exact additivity is in the cases priced earlier: the group transform is a sub-product of the textbook's partial layers and the bridge is the complement.**

So t transpositions cost at most about t·N extra gates and, as far as seen, buy nothing.

## 4. (c) Pencil: the additivity is two theorems, about the VALUES a program holds

**Lemma (levels are neighbours)** [derived in general; ran for all triples at 8 and 16 points: 128 and 1,072 coset characters]. If three coset characters, no two proportional, satisfy a·u + b·v = w, their levels differ by at most 1. Proof idea: each support lies in the union of the other two, and a flat is never the union of two smaller flats unless they are its two halves.

**Theorem A** [derived]. A program all of whose values are coset characters (up to scale) needs at least k·N gates for the transform. Proof: fix a level d < k. Every value of level above d is made from values of level above d or from level-d values, so everything above level d lies in the span of the level-d values used. The N characters are independent, so at least N distinct values sit at every level 0, 1, ..., k; level 0 is the inputs, each other value is one gate. The textbook meets it exactly. The same holds after any fixed free sign pattern on the inputs.
Checked by brute force at 4 points [ran, `thmA4.out`, all 65,536 subsets of the pool]: the least program made only of coset characters has **8** gates; allowing the four vectors with the quadratic sign pattern (−1)^(x1·x2) it drops to **7**.
This covers: the transform of any group with monomial representations read through a dictionary that is affine on the cosets of its chain, every bridge computed by butterflies, and (the abelian shadow of a non-affine dictionary) **every partition of the addresses into flats of any directions**, guillotine or not: transform each flat, then the bridge is "N character sums over cosets", still inside the calculus. So the conserved quantity is the level, and the theorem is about *the calculus*, not the chain and not affineness.

**Theorem B** [derived, Morgenstern's volume argument]. If every value a program holds is, up to scale, a vector with entries 0, +1, −1, then the only possible gates are u ± v, u ± 2v, 2u ± v, (u ± v)/2 [derived by cases on how the two supports overlap; these four kinds are the ones `levels.py` enumerates], the largest sub-determinant grows by at most 3 per gate, and the transform needs at least N·log2(N)/(2·log2 3) ≈ 0.315·N·log N gates. Dictionaries are permutations and monomial group transforms have entries 0, ±1, so **no choice of group and dictionary changes the order unless the bridge is computed through values with several magnitudes**.

**The first step a non-affine dictionary breaks.** Exactly that hypothesis: the rows of its bridge have several magnitudes (at 32 points: 4, 8, 12, 16, 32 in one matrix [ran]), so neither theorem applies to the bridge. Nothing proved here forbids a cheap bridge; only the data of sections 2 and 3 speak against it.

**Why the 7-gate brick escapes Theorem A, and why it stops at 7/8** [ran + derived]. Allow a quadratic sign pattern on the flat (the 240 "stabiliser" vectors at 8 points). Then relations appear whose levels differ by **2**: "level d+2 minus twice a level-d piece is again level d+2" (448 + 1,792 of them at 8 points [ran]); at 4 points that is output = (sum of all) − 2·(one input). A sign pattern of degree r allows a jump of r levels, and the counting of Theorem A weakens to about k·N/r [derived, rough]. But at the top level the targets are characters, and [derived; ran at 8 and 16 points, `hubs.out`: every quadratic hub serves exactly 4 targets or none, every cubic hub exactly 1 through a flip of codimension 3] a hub serves exactly 4 targets through quadratic flips and at most 1 through a flip of degree 3 or more (two flats of codimension 3 cannot differ by a half-space). So higher-degree sign patterns give no better brick, and Theorem B caps the whole 0, ±1 world anyway.
The known 22 at 8 points falls out of this count [derived]: a hub is the sum of 4 pair-pieces (3 gates), each of its 4 targets is one flip (4 gates), the 4 pieces cost 4 gates: 11 per 4 targets; the other 4 targets need the pairs with the opposite relative sign, which are different pieces, so 11 again. I tried three ways to share between the two halves (second hub from the first by one flip; pieces from pieces; a second pair direction, which is forced to be the same direction) and each comes back to 22. **[guess] 22 is the exact minimum at 8 points for programs made of the 240 stabiliser vectors, and (7/8)·k·N is the constant of that calculus; the counting bound in it is only about k·N/2.** That gap, between 1/2 and 7/8, is a finite question at 8 and 16 points.

**What the extraspecial route is, in other words** [derived]. Read the "difference half" f(a, b) as the coefficients of an operator M = Σ f(a, b)·X^a·Z^b on m two-level systems (the Pauli basis). The group transform gives the matrix entries of M; the full transform of f gives M's values on the "phase-point" operators (its discrete Wigner function). Matrix entries, in any of the many stabiliser bases, are a halfway house exactly m levels from the input and m levels from the output. Changing the halfway house is a free relabelling with quadratic phases on the Pauli side. So this route lives inside the relabel-and-phase class of generalize 4, which is closed at exactly k whole layers by its rank count. Same wall, third description.

**Piece economy** [derived]. In the 0, ±1 world a gate "flip the signs of a held pattern on a set S" needs the piece (the pattern restricted to S) in hand. If r different targets are produced with one and the same piece, S has at most N/r points; and a hub that is not a character has at most 1/ε² targets within (1 − ε)·N/2 flips (Parseval). So target-producing gates cannot share pieces, the family of pieces does not thin out from one level to the next, and that non-thinning is the log. A linear program needs the pieces to thin out geometrically (N, N/c, N/c², ...), which Theorem B says is impossible while all magnitudes are equal.

## 5. (d) A splitting A + V of the 6-bit addresses with neither set periodic [ran]

A = {2, 3, 5, 7, 8, 9, 20, 22}, V = {0, 8, 16, 24, 32, 41, 50, 59} (addresses as integers). Every address is a + v exactly once; neither set has a period. Distinct rows up to sign of H restricted to the columns in A: **32**; to the columns in V: **32**; against 8 and 8 for subgroups. Reason [derived]: the number of distinct rows is 2^(dimension of the affine span), and a non-periodic factor spans more than its size. So for this measure a non-periodic tiling is four times worse, not better. Built by the standard switching of periodic pieces; whether tilings that are not of this kind behave differently (they need 10 bits [read, generator's memory]) was not looked at.

## 6. Where the idea goes ("yes, and")

1. Fail: non-affine dictionaries make the bridge bigger. Yes, and: what they do to it is add rank-one pieces with large entries. So ask the question in that shape: **is H = (monomial-group transform through an affine dictionary) corrected by a low-rank matrix plus a sparse one**, with the low-rank part cheap? This is the non-rigidity of H seen relative to a group transform instead of relative to zero.
2. Fail: every flat partition is inside Theorem A. Yes, and: the theorem names its own exits. A linear program must hold values that are (i) not constant in size on their support, with ratios that grow with N (Theorem B), and (ii) related three at a time across many levels (Theorem A). The subset-sum form H = Z·D·Z^T has (i): all the volume sits in the free diagonal. The brick has (ii) with a jump of 2. **Nobody has a family with both.** Candidates to test at 8 and 16 points [guess]: rows of the family t^(Hamming distance) restricted to flats (magnitudes t^j, composition by velocity addition); sums over nested flats with weights (−2)^level.
3. The curator's "chain of dictionaries, k, k/2, k/4": inside the 0, ±1 world it is impossible by Theorem B; it would have to be a chain of bridges with growing coefficients, each a cheap unit-triangular matrix times a free diagonal.

## Limits

Nothing lifted from a prime to the rationals. The least cost of the group transforms themselves was not searched. Only the plus-type extraspecial group at 32 points; the minus type and Heisenberg groups not run. Theorem A's lemma is proved for real characters; with fourth roots of unity it was not rechecked. The k·N/r statement for degree-r sign patterns is a rough count, checked only through the jump sizes at 8 points.
