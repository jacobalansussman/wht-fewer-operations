## 1. The claim, stated precisely

Words used. An *array* is one working copy of the data. A *unit* is the small fixed program the engine repeats at every scale. *Block size* m: every array must be transformed along m directions. A *move* is one direction on one array. *Old letters*: the unit may only mix whole arrays by a constant matrix, shift addresses, and make moves. *Live* arrays (W of them) carry the data in and out; *scratch* arrays start empty and are ignored at the end.

The model [read: e2-2.md section 2, which cites the Lean lemmas `dir_phase`, `shift_phase`, `wrap_lum`; I did not re-read the Lean]. In a suitable basis the unit falls apart into *hidden sectors*, one for each quadratic form q on m bits without constant term (2^(m(m+1)/2) of them). In the sector q a move in direction z is "change the sign of one array" if q(z) = 1 and nothing if q(z) = 0; the mixing matrices are the same in every sector. The unit is right only if, in every sector, the product, read on the live arrays, is +1 when q(I) = 0 and −1 when q(I) = 1 (q(I) = the sum of q over the m coordinate directions).

Say q *selects* the moves with q(z) = 1. Call q with q(I) = 1 *tight* if it selects exactly W moves.

**Lemma** (for a right word, any number of scratch arrays).
(a) Counting: every q with q(I) = 1 selects at least W moves.
(b) Tight pattern: number the moves in word order; let u_i be the live part of "what array l_i looks like from the start" and w_i the live part of "what it looks like from the end" (two vectors with W entries, the same in every sector). If a tight form selects moves i and j, then w_i·u_i = 1, and w_i·u_j = 0 when i comes after j.
(c) Clique: a set of moves, any two of which are selected together by some tight form, has at most W members.

**Uniqueness claim.** The fewest moves that counting allows are 15 for (block size 4, 4 arrays), 30 for (4, 8), 16 for (5, 4), and in each case exactly one count vector reaches it: every direction once; every direction twice; every odd-weight direction once. All three have every pair of moves joined, so (c) excludes them.

## 2. Is the proof right? Yes

[derived, my own expansion] Write the word as g_0 D_1 g_1 … D_k g_k with D_i = 1 − 2 e e^T on array l_i. Multiplying out, a term keeps a chain of selected moves t_1 < … < t_r and contributes (−2)^r u_{t_1} c_{t_1 t_2} … c_{t_{r−1} t_r} w_{t_r}; the sum over chains is a finite geometric series because the chain matrix C is strictly triangular. Hence live part = live(G) − 2 U_A (1 + 2 C_A)^(−1) W_A^T. The form q = 0 gives live(G) = 1. For a tight form the three factors are W × W and their product is the identity, so W_A^T U_A = 1 + 2 C_A, which is (b). For (c): the table of w_i·u_j over a pairwise-joined set T is triangular with ones on the diagonal, so has rank |T|, and it is a product through W dimensions. ∎

What the proof uses: the sector q = 0 and the tight sectors, nothing else. It does not use that the mixing matrices are invertible, nor anything about scratch arrays except that the answer is read on the live ones.

[ran: `lemma_own.py`, exact rationals] The expansion holds on 2,352 (word, subset) cases with up to 4 scratch arrays, no failure. For (b) I built 643 words with generic, non-commuting constants and 0 to 2 scratch arrays in which a set of W sign changes turns the live part from +1 to −1 (the last constant is solved for); the pattern held in every entry. This is a different test from the authors' (theirs used plain schedules in disguise).

**One addition** [derived]. The lemma applies to words with larger blocks through their single moves: if a form selects exactly W single moves, counting on blocks forces those moves into W different blocks, each selected; so W + 1 pairwise-joined single moves give W + 1 pairwise-joined blocks. Every exclusion below is therefore an exclusion of the *total number of moves* (sum of block ranks) of any old-letter word.

**The growth law** [derived; three instances ran]. Let c(m, W) be the fewest moves counting allows and f(m, W) the fewest after the lemma. A count vector that passes counting for W + 1 arrays has no tight form for W arrays, so it survives. Hence

c(m, W) ≤ f(m, W) ≤ c(m, W + 1) ≤ c(m, W) + m.

The lemma is worth at most m moves in total, not per array. Observed: +1 for 4 to 7 arrays and +2 for 8 to 13 and 16 arrays at block size 4 (14 and 15 not run); at least +3 for 4 arrays at block size 5. Meanwhile the discount counting allows grows with the batch (one move per 4 arrays at block size 4). So per array the lemma's effect goes to zero like 1/W, and the floor on the exponent returns to the counting value log(4 − 2^(2−m)) / log m.
Checked instances with no tight form at all [ran, `run4.out`]: (block size 5, 19 arrays) 80 moves against 95; (6, 7) 32 against 42; (5, 7) 32 against 35; (4, 19) 75 against 76.

## 5. Is it known?

- The core without scratch arrays is classical: in the general linear group the least number of reflections whose product is a given map equals the codimension of its fixed space, and shortest factorisations are rigid. Huang, Lewis, Reiner, "Absolute order in general linear groups", arXiv:1506.03332 (2015; J. London Math. Soc. 2017) [read: abstract only]; for orthogonal groups Brady and Watt, "A partial order on the orthogonal group", Comm. Algebra 30 (2002) [read: citation only], extended in arXiv:2103.02507 (2021). "W reflections of a W-dimensional space with product −1 commute" is the smallest case.
- The version with scratch arrays (the condition is on a corner of the product) and the use against a covering design I did not find. Two searches only; treat "new" as unproved.
- What the known theory predicts for "linear or not": nothing. It says shortest factorisations look like the plain schedule, so any saving must come from forms served by more than W moves through relations that do not commute. That is the same message as section 4.

## 6. In the user's terms

- No circuit, no size formula, no exponent. The lemma is an obstruction.
- What is now excluded for old letters (given the model of section 1): any saving at all with 11 or fewer arrays at block size 4 (the move count is forced to be plain, and a plain count gives no saving because a block of rank r costs at least r/m of a transform); and 16, 17, 18 moves for 4 arrays at block size 5.
- What is not excluded: a saving of 1e-1. Smallest shapes that pass both tests, with the saving they would give if they could be built from single moves: (block size 6, 7 arrays, 32 moves) 1.5e-1; (5, 19, 80) 1.07e-1; (5, 7, 32) 5.6e-2; (4, 16, 62) 2.3e-2; (4, 12, 47) 1.5e-2. For comparison the published saving is 8.76e-4. **None of these is built; passing two necessary conditions is not existence.**
- Linear size: no bearing. The floor note already argues the engine cannot be linear, and this lemma lives inside the engine with old letters only.

## 7. What fails in e2-2, and the nearest true statements

1. "None of these can be realised … every discount that counting allowed dies on the lemma" holds only for the count vectors at the exact minimum. Nearest true statement: the lemma removes the designs in which the lightest forms are tight, and costs at most m extra moves in total (section 4). The question e2-2 leaves open, "does it take away every rung at every block size", has the answer no.
2. "17 would be a saving of 1.0e-1 and is open": closed, 17 and 18 are excluded.
3. Moves and savings are not the same thing once blocks have rank above 1. The move floors hold for all words (section 2). The savings quoted beside them, log(moves / W) / log m, hold for words of single moves. A block of rank r costs (r/m)^z, less than r single moves, so for words with larger blocks the only saving bounds proved are "none when the move count is plain" and the counting bound of e2-2's first table (1.58e-1 at block size 5, floating point, not re-run by me). A block-level version of the search was not done by anyone.
4. Not checked by me: that the Lean engine's old letters really reduce to the model of section 1. Everything here is conditional on that reading.
5. The clique test is the weakest consequence of the proof. The full statement is: for some ordering of the moves there is a table of rank at most W with ones on the diagonal and zeros below it at every pair joined by a tight form. The survivors at 12, 13 and 16 arrays pass the clique test with equality (joined set exactly W) and may well fail the full statement. Not tested.

## 8. The exchange-rate theorem (e2-2 section 3)

Statement: with a(N) the cost per point of infinitely many N-point arrays served together, C(n) / (n log2 n) tends to the infimum over N of a(N) / log2 N (n and N powers of two).

[derived: I re-did the proof line by line] Correct. (i) one transform of N·M points is M arrays of N points then N arrays of M points; (ii) the same with t copies gives a(NM) ≤ a(N) + a(M), so a(2^k)/k converges to its infimum; (iii) a ≤ c gives the lower half; (iv) batches of size N^(j−1)·M are eventually large enough for the cost of N-point arrays to be within ε of a(N), and M can be taken from k consecutive powers of two so that every large n is reached. No gap.

Known? The two halves are the standard tensor-power argument (the same shape as Strassen's asymptotic rank and Schönhage's sum inequality) [guess: folklore]. I found no statement of it for this transform; the nearest recent work is Alman and Li, "Kronecker Powers, Orthogonal Vectors, and the Asymptotic Spectrum", arXiv:2509.14489 (17 September 2025), which applies Strassen's theory to depth-2 circuits [read: abstract only].
What it gives: no discount and no circuit. It says exactly what would have to be found: one batch, at one size N, whose cost per point per address bit is below 7/8 (count G) lowers the constant for a single array; the order changes only if a(N)/log N keeps falling as N grows. It does not give linear size from any bounded amount of information.
