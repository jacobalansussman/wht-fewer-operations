Gate count: **(G)** (a gate is any a·u + b·v, scalings free) wherever gates are counted. Sections 1 and 2 count the engine's own unit, *moves*, not gates.

Words. An *array* is one working copy of the data. A *batch* is several arrays served together; a *discount* is a batch that costs less than its arrays served one by one. The engine (the Lean-checked method of the release) repeats one fixed small program, the *unit*, at every scale. In a unit every array must be transformed along m *directions* (m = *block size*); one direction on one array is a *move*; a *block* is a run of r moves on one array, done as one call to a smaller transform (r = its *rank*). Additions between arrays are free in the engine's count (they cost n, not n log n).

## Where it ended

The idea turned into sharper questions, inside the engine and outside it, and into one new lemma (the clique lemma, section 2).

1. **Inside the engine the discount has a one-line law** [ran]: saving ≈ (fraction of moves saved) / (fragmentation), 8.76e-4 = 2.11e-3 / 2.41 for the published unit. The published design saves a quarter of a move per data array out of 45; a saving of 1e-1 needs about 10 moves per array at the same block shapes, a factor 100. In the three published units the discount per array goes *down* as the batch grows, because the block size grows with it. The other knob is the block size: one move saved per array at block size 5 would already be 1.4e-1.
2. **Counting does not forbid that** (for the old letters; I re-derived a bound that a sibling report already has, and extended it) [ran]: at block size m the covering bound allows about 4 moves per array at every m, i.e. an exponent as low as log 4 / log m. The smallest batch at which it allows anything is 4 arrays: 15 moves in place of 16 at block size 4, **16 in place of 20 at block size 5**. Realised, the last would be a saving of 1.4e-1. **None of these can be realised** [derived + ran]: a new lemma (the *clique lemma*, section 2; it holds with any number of scratch arrays; one exact test passed, no second reader) excludes every count vector the solver found. With old letters, 4 arrays need all 16 moves at block size 4, and at least 17 of 20 at block size 5 (17 would be a saving of 1.0e-1; open). The lemma says what a unit must look like: almost no hidden form may be served with the minimum number of blocks.
3. **Outside the engine the ladder is an exact statement** [derived]: the best constant in front of n log2 n for ONE array equals the best cost per point per address bit of INFINITELY many arrays served together. So "one array in o(n log n)" and "many arrays at o(log N) each" are the same problem, in both directions. The lowest rung is: can infinitely many 4-point transforms be done in fewer than 7 gates each? Between 4 and 7; nobody knows.
4. **Paul's discount, made explicit** [ran, integer arithmetic]: for a typical matrix it starts at a batch of 32 arrays of 32 points. On the transform the same recipe makes the products almost free and loses everything in its additions.

- **The law** [derived, then ran]. The engine's condition is: sum over the unit's blocks of (rank/m)^z < W [read: `allotB_bound`]. A block of rank r is the fraction (r/m)^z of a whole transform, which is more than r/m when z < 1. So a batch pays for cutting transforms into pieces. To first order, saving = δ / F, with δ the fraction of moves saved and F = (1/W) · sum over blocks of (r/m)·ln(m/r), the *fragmentation*. The table confirms it to three digits.
- **The shape of this discount** [read + derived]. Every paid step is private: one block is one call on ONE array [read: `BMove.block (l : ρ) …`]. The sharing is done by the free letters: a constant rational matrix that mixes whole arrays (it acts on the batch index alone) and address shifts on one array [read: `Move.localM`, `Move.shift`]. So the calls work on mixtures: 62 % of all moves of the E8 unit are helper moves (35,235 of 56,715), and a helper holds a signed sum of several sources [read: README section 6]. In the brief's words: the encoders and decoders are the free additions, the core is thousands of private calls on mixtures. This is Paul's shape with one difference that matters: the brief says an encoder on the batch index alone commutes with the copies and gains nothing. True for a step done on all arrays alike. The engine's paid steps are done on one array at a time, and a batch mixing does not commute with those. That non-commutation is the discount (section 2).
- **Which single number would have to change** [ran]. Moves saved per array. With the same block shapes: 0.095 now; 0.98 for a saving of 1e-2; **9.7 of 45 for 1e-1**; 32.8 of 45 for z = 0.5. The published design cannot do it: it saves at most one move per data array (D = 4v − 5·cst, README section 7), so even with no helpers and no scratch copies δ ≤ 1/m and the saving stays near 1e-2.
- **The other knob is the block size** [derived from the same condition]. With k single moves per array the exponent is z = log k / log m. One move saved per array is worth 1/m of the work at m = 45, but at m = 5 (4 moves in place of 5) it is z = log 4 / log 5 = 0.861, a saving of 1.4e-1.
- **Does the discount grow with the batch?** In the published units, no [ran]: per data array they save 0.25, 0.58, 0.46 moves, always under one, while m is 45, 110, 80. A bigger family of pairs needs wider labels, m grows, and a move is worth less. The smallest unit has the largest saving.

## 2. Where the discount is made: one hidden quadratic form (old letters), and what counting allows

**Not new in substance.** After deriving this I found it in `wht35/wider/w-size.md` section 5 (Lemma A, and a linear programme with the values (2^m − 1)/2^(m−2)) and in `wht38/tiny/b4.md` line 105 ("the group ring of the 1,024 symmetric 4 × 4 bit matrices"). My numbers agree with theirs. What is added here: blocks of every rank with the exponent; the ladder in the batch size; the equality case; the reading as reflections.

**The picture** [derived; the facts used are the Lean lemmas `dir_phase`, `shift_phase`, `wrap_lum`, read]. Scope: words of the OLD letters only (mixing, shifts, directions). I did not check which letters the fourth unit uses; its engine also allows address maps and phases, and there this picture does not apply as it stands.
- All old letters are diagonal in one basis. A basis vector is a list of f labels x_1 … x_f (one per digit column). A direction z multiplies by i^(number of columns with z·x_k = 1), a shift by a sign, the whole kernel by i^(total number of ones).
- Take the basis vectors on which every shift is +1. There the only thing a word can see is one *quadratic form* q on the m-bit vectors (a polynomial of degree two without constant term over bits; q(z) = half the number of columns with z·x_k = 1). The products q = (a·z)(b·z) occur from f = 7 on [derived: columns a, b, a+b, a, a, b, b]; forms add when column lists are joined, so every q occurs once f is large enough, and a unit has to be right at every f [read: floor note].
- A block with directions z_1 … z_r multiplies its array by (−1)^(q(z_1) + … + q(z_r)). Write S = z_1 z_1^T + … + z_r z_r^T, a symmetric m × m bit matrix of rank exactly r with a non-zero diagonal, and q(S) for that sum. The kernel is S = I.
- So a unit is this: constant matrices and sign changes of single arrays, the sign of a block being (−1)^q(S); for EVERY q the product must be (−1)^q(I) on the W live arrays. In each case a sign change of one array, seen from the start of the word, is a *reflection* (an involution that negates one direction of the W-dimensional space of arrays). For every q, the reflections that q selects, in their order, must multiply to +1 or −1.
- **Counting** [derived; same argument as Lemma A]. If q(I) = 1 the product is −1 on W arrays, and each selected block changes rank by at most one: every q with q(I) = 1 selects at least W blocks.

**What counting allows** [ran: `lp.py`, outputs `lp2.out` … `lp5.out`; floating-point linear programme (HiGHS), reduced by coordinate permutations]. Minimise the sum over blocks of (rank/m)^z per array, subject to "every q with q(I) = 1 selects at least one block per array". All legal blocks of all ranks are allowed.

| block size m | best value at z = 1 (plain = 1) | floor on z | saving at most | what the optimum uses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 1 | 1 | 0 | plain schedule |
| 3 | 1 | 1 | 0 | plain schedule (this is Lemma A) |
| 4 | 0.9375 = 3.75/4 | 0.9534 | 4.7e-2 | every one of the 15 directions, 1/4 move per array each |
| 5 | 0.7750 = 3.875/5 | 0.8416 | 1.58e-1 | every one of the 31 directions, 1/8 move per array each |

- Blocks of rank 2 or more never enter the optimum: the best the count allows is single moves spread evenly over ALL directions. Reason [derived]: the lightest forms with q(I) = 1 are products (a·z)(b·z) with a·b = 1; they are 1 on a quarter of all directions.
- Closed form [ran at m = 4, 5; guess beyond]: (2^m − 1)/2^(m−2) moves per array and z ≥ log(4 − 2^(2−m)) / log m: 0.765 at m = 6, 0.602 at 10, 0.500 at 16, 0.364 at 45, 0.295 at 110. **The count allows about four moves per array at every block size.** It is a necessary condition only. Nothing here builds a unit.
- For comparison the floor note (`wht38/floor/NOTE.md`) has z > 0.380 at block size 3 and 8.4e-32 at 110 for the whole engine; for old letters this bound is far stronger. With address maps free the sibling report says the same argument gives 2 blocks per array, i.e. about log 2 / log m.

**The ladder in the batch size** [ran: `milp.py`, `milp4.out`, `milp5.out`; integer programme: the covering condition, with and without the parity condition "the labels sum to W·I modulo 2" that the determinant gives when there is no scratch]. Fewest single moves the count allows, block size 4:

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| plain | 4 | 8 | 12 | 16 | 20 | 24 | 28 | 32 |

All eight values are proved optimal by the solver, with and without parity. So in this game the smallest batch for which counting allows a discount is **4 arrays at block size 4**, and the allowed discount does not grow with the batch: it saturates at 3.75 per array at multiples of 4.

Block size 5 [ran: `milp5_first.out`, `milp5b.out`, `milp5_W4.out`]: W = 1, 2, 3 allow nothing (5, 10, 15 moves, proved optimal without parity; the plain schedule meets parity). **W = 4 allows 16 moves in place of 20** (proved optimal, with and without parity). Realised with single moves that would be z = log 4 / log 5 = 0.861, a saving of 1.4e-1; 17 moves would be 1.0e-1. W = 5 and up: not finished. [derived by hand, agrees] For W = 2 the determinant makes the labels sum to zero; multisets of 8 or 9 rank-one labels with sum zero span at most 4 of the 5 dimensions, so some form with q(I) = 1 selects nothing.

**The design the solver found is a clean one: every direction with an odd number of ones, once** (16 directions at m = 5) [ran: `milp5_W4.out`]. [derived] It works at every block size: take each odd-weight direction W/2^(m−3) times; a form with q(I) = 1 cannot vanish on the odd-weight directions (the forms that do are b·z + (1·z)(b·z), and they have q(I) = 0), and a non-zero polynomial of degree two on that half-space is 1 on at least 2^(m−3) points. Total: **exactly 4 moves per array at every block size** (z = log 4 / log m if it could be realised). The odd-weight directions are the ones whose move is a reflection of the label space itself for the ordinary dot product (z·z = 1). [guess] A unit of this shape would have the whole orthogonal group of the m-bit labels as symmetry.

**But that design cannot be a unit, with or without scratch arrays: the clique lemma** [derived tonight by hand; its formula tested exactly on small words, `lemma_test.py`; no second reader; applied by program: `clique.py`, `clique.out`, exact].

*Lemma.* Old letters, any number of scratch arrays. Call a form q with q(I) = 1 *tight* if it selects exactly W blocks. A set of blocks, any two of which are selected together by some tight form, has at most W members.

*Proof.* Number the blocks 1 … k in the order of the word; block i acts on array l_i. Let u_i be the column l_i of the product of the constant matrices before block i, read on the live arrays, and w_i the row l_i of the product of those after it, read on the live arrays (both are vectors with W entries), and c_ij (i < j) the entry (l_i, l_j) of the product of the constants between blocks i and j. Expanding the word for a form q that selects the set A gives: live part = 1 − 2·U_A (1 + 2 C_A)^(−1) W_A^T, with U_A the matrix of the u_i, W_A that of the w_i, C_A the strictly upper triangular matrix of the c_ij, i and j in A. So the condition for a unit is U_A (1 + 2 C_A)^(−1) W_A^T = 1 when q(I) = 1, and = 0 when q(I) = 0. If q is tight, U_A and W_A are square, so W_A^T U_A = 1 + 2 C_A: for i, j in A, w_i·u_i = 1 and w_i·u_j = 0 whenever i comes after j. Take a set T of blocks, any two together in a tight form. The table of all w_i·u_j with i, j in T is then triangular with ones on the diagonal, so it has rank |T|. But it is a product of a |T| × W matrix and a W × |T| matrix, so its rank is at most W. ∎

(Without scratch arrays this is the statement that W reflections whose product is −1 commute, which is how I found it; the lemma does not need that.)

- At block size 4 the even design is the only count vector at the bound for W = 4, 8, 12 [ran: `uniq.py`, `uniq.out`; forcing any one direction off its even count costs one more move, proved optimal]. Together with the lemma: **with old letters, scratch arrays or not, 4 arrays at block size 4 need all 16 moves and 8 arrays need at least 31 of 32.** For W = 5, 6, 7 only the solver's own count vector was tested; other vectors with 19, 23, 27 moves may exist.
- Block size 5, 4 arrays [ran: `uniq5.py`, `uniq5.out`]: the odd-weight design is the only count vector with 16 moves (one direction of each weight forced off the design: 17 moves proved in five cases, solver's lower bound 18 in the other three). Together with the lemma: **with old letters, scratch arrays or not, 4 arrays at block size 5 need at least 17 moves.** 17 (a saving of 1.0e-1), 18 and 19 are open.
- Blocks of rank 2 or more do not escape [derived]. Cutting a block into its single moves can only increase what each form selects, so the move counts above are floors for words with blocks too. And a word whose single moves form one of the three designs just excluded cannot group two of them into one block: some tight form selects both directions, the block would then be selected by an even number and drop out, and that form would be served by fewer than W blocks.
- So every discount that counting allowed in these tables dies on the lemma. Counting plus the lemma is a stronger floor than counting; how strong it is at large block size is not known. It could be added to the integer programme as a constraint on the pattern of tight forms. `pairs.py` [ran] shows why it bites: for m ≥ 4 every two directions lie together in the 1-set of one lightest form (105 of 105 pairs at m = 4, 465 of 465 at m = 5, all pairs up to m = 7; at m = 3 only 12 of 21).
- What a unit must therefore look like [derived]: few tight forms. Almost every form with q(I) = 1 must select MORE than W blocks, and get its −1 from a relation among reflections that do not commute. This is the opposite of the lightest design, and it is what the published units do (16 to 25 blocks per array).

**What this says about the published discount** [derived + guess]. A unit is a set of relations among reflections, one relation for each hidden form. The saving is possible only because the reflections do not commute. [recalled] The 120 reflections of the E8 root system each fail to commute with exactly 56 others; [read] the E8 unit has 120 pairs and each "knows" 56 others. [guess] That is why a root system "worked where nothing said it should": root systems are the standard supply of reflections with many short relations. Other suppliers of such relations (complex reflection groups, the 2-transposition groups) are candidates for families. Unchecked.

**A lead for the full game** [guess + recalled, nothing read tonight]. With address maps and phases the free and paid letters together are the Clifford group acting alike on all f columns [read: header of `Letters.lean`, "the free letters generate every affine address permutation times such a phase"]. What a word can see is then whatever commutes with that action. For the Clifford group this is known: it is described by self-dual codes (Gross, Nezami, Walter, "Schur-Weyl duality for the Clifford group", 2017) [recalled]. That object would play the part that the quadratic form q plays above, and the same covering programme could be written for the full game. It also ties in with the first wave's pick "several self-dual codes".

## 3. The ladder, made exact (count G; the same proof works for count O)

Definitions. C(N) = fewest gates for one N-point transform; C_t(N) = fewest gates for t arrays of N points together. Per point: c(N) = C(N)/N, c_t(N) = C_t(N)/(tN). The *amortised cost* a(N) is the limit of c_t(N) as t grows (it exists and is the infimum, because two batches side by side cost at most the sum).

**Statement** [derived, six lines, unchecked]. Let α = infimum over N of a(N)/log2 N. Then C(n) / (n log2 n) tends to α.

Proof. (i) The transform of N·M points is M arrays of N points, then N arrays of M points: c(NM) ≤ c_M(N) + c_N(M) ≤ c_M(N) + c(M). (ii) The same with t copies: a(NM) ≤ a(N) + a(M), so a(2^k)/k has a limit, which is α. (iii) a(N) ≤ c(N), so c(2^k)/k cannot fall below α in the limit. (iv) Fix N and ε; for every M beyond some size, c_M(N) ≤ a(N) + ε, so c(N·M) ≤ a(N) + ε + c(M); repeat j times: c(N^j M) ≤ j·(a(N) + ε) + c(M). Divide by the number of address bits. ∎

What it gives.
- The generator wrote "one direction only". For rates per point it is both directions: **one array in o(n log n) if and only if the amortised cost per point is o(log N).** Any batch, of any size, at any N, whose cost per point per address bit is below the best known constant (7/8 in count G) lowers the constant for one array.
- The rungs of the brief are statements about how large a batch must be before c_t(N) is near a(N): batch N^γ gives log log n, batch polylog N gives log* n, bounded batch gives linear, each provided a(N) is bounded.
- **The lowest rung** [derived]. t butterflies cost exactly 2t (every output is its own gate): a(2) = 1 per point, no discount ever. For the 4-point transform every output again needs its own gate, so 4 ≤ (amortised gates per copy) ≤ 7. I found no argument that excludes anything in between: after setting the other arrays to zero each array must keep 7 working gates, 4 of them its outputs, but one helper gate can be a working gate for every array at once. And 4 copies in fewer than 28 gates would give 16 points in fewer than 56.
- [derived] Fixed-size discounts only move the constant: at a fixed N a batch can never gain more than the factor log2 N, because each output needs a gate (a(N) ≥ 1 per point). The order changes only if a(N)/log N keeps falling as N grows.

## 4. Paul's discount, explicit (the nearest problem that can be solved)

[ran: `paul.py`, `paul.out`; exact integers] n arrays of n points through a typical n × n matrix A known in advance. In count (G) products by known numbers are free and sums of blocks of A are free, so Strassen's recipe costs only its additions on the array side: T(n) = min(n·n(n−1), 7·T(n/2) + g·(n/2)²) with g = 13 (Strassen) or 11 (Winograd's variant). Separately: at most n(n−1) per array, at least (n−1)² [derived by counting free parameters; not a proof anyone read].

| n | 16 | 32 | 64 | 256 | 1024 | 4096 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| θ = batch / separate (Winograd), between | 1.00 and 1.07 | **0.936 and 0.966** | 0.849 and 0.863 | 0.672 and 0.675 | 0.520 and 0.521 | 0.400 |

- The crossover is n = 32: a tie at 16, a certain discount (below the lower count of separate copies) from 32 on. Recursing all the way down to single entries only pays from n = 1024.
- **On the transform** [read: j-manyatonce; + derived]. With the coordinate splitting, three of the seven products vanish and four are multiples of H. With the 4-point brick 2I − J split into 2 × 2 blocks [ran, `paul.out`]: four of the seven left factors are signed permutations (free) and three have rank one. The products for four 4-point arrays cost 6 gates in all; the recipe's own additions cost 44 to 52; four bricks separately cost 28.
- Lesson [derived]. Paul's discount is paid for by products that are expensive (n per output). The transform's products are nearly free, so a recipe is judged by its additions alone, and those must stay under one gate per point per level: the single-array problem again. Also, for the transform at least 4 half-size products are forced by counting outputs, so no recipe can win on the number of products. The discount, if any, is not of Paul's kind.

## 5. The other two experiments of the brief

- **(c) Encoder search at 4 copies of the 4-point transform: not run, for a reason** [derived]. Conjugating by a map that only relabels and rescales (all three encoders named in the brief are of this kind) cannot change a gate count in (G): a circuit for E·M·E⁻¹ is a circuit for M with the wires renamed. The first encoders that can matter are "mix arrays, relabel one array, mix back". That is the engine's device (it swaps two arrays on part of the data) and it costs a fixed number of gates per point per use, so it can only pay against log N: 16 points is the wrong size to look for it.
- **(d) Structured batches: answered** [derived; the identity ran exactly for all pairs of linear functions at N = 4, 16, 64: `twist.py`, `twist.out`]. Take one array x and the batch of columns D·x, D a sign pattern of degree two. For the pattern (−1)^(a1·a2) the identity (−1)^(a1 a2) = ½(1 + (−1)^a1 + (−1)^a2 − (−1)^(a1+a2)) turns into: H·D = ½(1 + shift_1 + shift_2 − shift_12)·H. So once H·x is known, the transform of D·x is one 4-point brick along a plane of H·x: 7 gates per 4 points, i.e. 1.75 gates per point for the whole extra column, at every N. The same holds for any pattern that is a product of two linear functions of the address, and patterns can be chained (each step changes the pattern by one such product). So yes: a batch of g such columns costs one transform plus 1.75 gates per point for each further column, against (7/8)·log2 N per point separately. But this is the engine's own basic step (a move between two frames) seen as a batch, and it is cheap because the batch has only N free numbers. It is not a discount for arrays that are free. A pattern of full rank (a bent function) is a whole transform again.

## 6. Answers to the brief's questions

- *Where is the published discount made?* In the free mixing of whole arrays between private calls: reflections of the space of arrays that do not commute (section 2), paid for by fragmentation (section 1).
- *What stops it from being bigger: batch size, unit size or coefficients?* Not the batch size (the counting bound saturates at W = 4; the published discount shrinks as the batch grows) and not the coefficients (nothing found). In the published design: one move per data array at most, at block sizes 45 to 110. In general: counting alone would allow four moves per array at any block size, and the clique lemma is what forbids the designs that meet it: a form served by exactly W blocks forces those blocks into a rigid triangular pattern, and too many such forms contradict each other.
- *Smallest batch with θ < 1 for the plain transform?* In count (G): unknown; 2 arrays of 4 points is excluded modulo 3 [read: j-manyatonce]. In the engine's old-letter game the count excludes W = 1, 2, 3 at block sizes 4 and 5 and allows W = 4; the clique lemma then excludes W = 4 at block size 4 altogether, and at block size 5 it leaves 17, 18 or 19 moves for 4 arrays.
- *Ladder or saturation?* In the engine, as far as counting goes, saturation in the batch size and a ladder in the block size (z as low as log 4 / log m). The clique lemma takes away every rung of it that was tested (block sizes 4 and 5). Whether it takes away all of them at every block size is the next question.

- Section 2 covers old letters only, and its core repeats a sibling report. The tables are necessary conditions from floating-point solvers; no unit was built or searched. The closed form beyond m = 5 is a guess.
- The clique lemma is half an hour old and no second reader has seen it. One test was run [ran, exact rationals: `lemma_test.py`, `lemma_test.out`]: the expansion formula of the proof holds on 40 random words (right or wrong, with scratch arrays; 1,384 word-and-form cases, no failure); and on 30 words that are right (plain schedules hidden behind random mixing, some with a scratch array) the 720 tight forms all show the triangular pattern, and no set of more than W blocks is pairwise joined. The right words tested are all plain schedules in disguise, because no other right word is known at these sizes; so the last step of the lemma (rank) is tested only where it is not surprising.
- `frames.py` is the first, weaker version (no scratch arrays; "all pairs commute"); `clique.py` replaces it.
- `refl.py` (a floating-point search for reflections that realise a count vector, no scratch) was written before `frames.py` showed its target to be excluded. It was started by mistake beside another job, stopped after 50 seconds, and produced no result. It would be the right tool once it allows scratch arrays.
- The theorem of section 3 is short and unchecked. It turns the ladder into a statement, and gives no discount.
- The lower count (n−1)² in section 4 is a parameter count.
- The E8 remark is a coincidence of two numbers plus a guess.

## 8. What I would run next

1. **Have the clique lemma read by someone else**, then **put it into the integer programme**: minimise the moves subject to counting and to "no W + 1 blocks pairwise joined by tight forms". At block size 5, W = 4 this decides whether 17 moves (a saving of 1.0e-1) survives; at block sizes 6 to 8 it gives the first floor that knows about non-commutation.
2. **The same lemma for the full game** [guess]. The proof only used that, in each hidden sector, a block changes one array by a sign. With address maps and phases the sectors are those of the Clifford group (lead in section 2). If a block acts there by a change of rank one on the arrays, the lemma carries over, and it would be the first constraint beyond counting for the targets of the tiny-unit hunt (3 arrays in 8 moves at block size 3; 2 arrays in 6 moves at block size 4). I did not try.
3. **Amortised 4-point transform**: 3 and 4 copies of the brick in fewer than 21 and 28 gates, by `j-manyatonce/ds.c` modulo 3 with the output gates fixed (4 per copy are forced) so that only the 3t − 1 helpers are searched.
4. **Reflection families**: for a root system or a 2-transposition group, list the relations among its reflections and solve for labels S (symmetric bit matrices) that turn them into a unit; compare with the E8 unit's 120 labels.
