# e-3 cutoff: a cheap wrong answer, then the tails

**First line.** No circuit below the textbook count and no growth law below N log N for an EXACT program came out of this.
What came out: (1) the cheap wrong answer is real and counted: 99 % of entries for 4.45 sqrt(k) N additions; (2) the leftover is
not a smaller problem, it is the SAME problem, exactly, in three precise senses (identities A, B, C below, each run on small cases: A and C in exact arithmetic, B modulo a prime);
(3) a new pair of objects: a matrix R that is zero on most entries and whose inverse is the transform up to a tiny corner.
Private notes, nothing in Lean. Programs and outputs: `waves/wave-02/e-3-cutoff/`
(`poly.py`, `mat.py`, `count.py`, `count2.py`, `dual.py`, `regular.py`, each with its `.out`; `count.py` stopped on a
print overflow after its part 4, and `count2.py` is its remaining part).

Words. k = number of address bits, N = 2^k. Overlap t of two addresses x, y = number of positions where both have a 1; the
transform H has entry (-1)^t. "Cut-off sums" = for every set S of at most d positions, the sum of the data over all addresses
containing S (M = C(k,0)+...+C(k,d) numbers). "Cut-off form" = cut-off sums, one scaling per S (by c_|S|), then the
transposed step ("spread"). Its matrix has entry p(t), any polynomial p of degree d. "Window" = the d+1 consecutive overlaps
where p(t) = (-1)^t. "Leftover" R = H - (cut-off form), entry r(t) = (-1)^t - p(t), zero on the window. "Light / heavy"
address = weight at most d / more than d. Gate counts: every count below is ADDITIONS, the same number in (G) and (O); the M
scalings are free in (G) and M extra in (O), and M is negligible against N.

## 1. The first experiment, as asked

**Polynomials only** [ran, `poly.out`]. Window = narrowest holding 99 % of pairs.

| k | window | degree d | largest coefficient c_j | leftover just outside the window | leftover at t = 0, k/2, k |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 16 | [0, 8] | 8 = 2.00 sqrt k | 9 bits | 2^9 | 0, 0, 22 bits |
| 64 | [8, 25] | 17 = 2.12 sqrt k | 37 bits | 2^18 on both sides | 37, 35, 64 bits |
| 256 | [46, 81] | 35 = 2.19 sqrt k | 111 bits | 2^36 on both sides | 111, 112, 168 bits |

- The coefficients alternate in sign strictly and are about 0.43 k bits, not d log k. [ran]
- The leftover one step outside the window is exactly +-2^(d+1) on BOTH sides. [ran; derived: it is the (d+1)-st difference of (-1)^t]
- Closed forms, asserted in the run: above the window r(t) = (-1)^a sum_{i>d} (-2)^i C(t-a, i); below it
  r(t) = (-1)^t - (-1)^a sum_{i<=d} 2^i C(a-t+i-1, i), a = left end of the window.
- Same thing in the "distance" frame (Hamming distance between x and y, where the transform is (-i)^distance up to free
  phases; the two parities of the distance treated separately): half the degree gives about the same share
  (k = 256: degree 17 gives 97.5 %, degree 35 gives 99.999 %). It costs two passes instead of one, so no gain, same law. [ran]

**(a) The cut-off sums as a counted program** [ran, `mat.out`, k = 8 and 10, every d, outputs compared with the matrix].
Exact law: additions = sum over i < k of C(i, <=d) 2^(k-i-1). That is (d+1) N for d well below k/2 and it SATURATES at
k N / 2 (the full subset-sum transform). The spread costs exactly (sums) - N + M (the transposition rule, seen on every row).
Whole form at k = 10: d = 1: 2.99 N; d = 2: 4.92 N; d = 3: 6.70 N; against 10 N.
Growth law at the 99 % window [ran as a count, `count.out`]: 14.8 N, 35.0 N, 71.0 N, 143 N, 285 N at k = 16, 64, 256, 1024, 4096,
that is **4.45 sqrt(k) N** against k N. At 99.99 %: 6.73 sqrt(k) N. The waist M is 2^144 of 2^256 at k = 256.
[derived] For d = 1 the program is optimal in (G), to the gate: the spread is "all subset sums of k numbers plus a constant",
N outputs of which only one is an input, pairwise not proportional, so at least N - 1 gates, and the program uses N - 1 (255 at
k = 8); transposing a circuit with n inputs and m outputs changes the gate count by m - n, so the sums need at least
2N - k - 2, and the program uses 2N - k - 2 (502 at k = 8). So even the first layer of the cut-off costs 2N, not N. For d >= 2
I have no lower bound beyond that.

**(b) The leftover by pairs of weight classes** [ran, `mat.out`, k = 8, window [1,3], all 45 class pairs]. The overlaps that
occur for weights (w, v) are every value from max(0, w+v-k) to min(w, v). The leftover block is zero only for extreme pairs
((1,8), (2,7), (2,8), (3,6), (3,7), (3,8)); elsewhere it has full rank except (5,6): 8 of 28 (there the leftover happens to be
linear in t) and (4,4): 35 of 70. One own form of degree min(w, v, k-w, k-v) is exact
on a class pair, which is degree about k/2 for the central pairs.
Anomaly [ran]: H on (class of weight w) x (the middle class, weight k/2) has rank C(k-1, w), not C(k, w): 7 of 8, 21 of 28,
35 of 56, 35 of 70 at k = 8. [derived] Complementing y multiplies the column by (-1)^|x| and maps the middle class to itself,
so the middle class behaves like a cube with one bit fewer.

**(c) Adding up.** There is nothing to add up, and the reason is the first real finding:
- [derived] Windows do not add. A form exact on one window is garbage (huge numbers) outside it; two forms with two windows
  sum to one polynomial of the larger degree. Low-degree polynomials have no "partition of unity" on 0..k.
- [ran, count] Class pairs thin enough for one degree-d form hold 49 % of entries at k = 8 (d = 2) but 4.5e-4 at k = 64 and 7e-34 at k = 256.
- [ran, count] Giving every class pair its own window lowers the 99 % degree from 2.19 sqrt k to 1.25 sqrt k (k = 64, 256). Still sqrt k:
  the spread of the overlap is intrinsic, no splitting by weights makes it narrow.
- [derived] No splitting into classes can do it with forms of low degree: a degree-d polynomial agrees with (-1)^t on at most
  d+1 consecutive overlaps, and central class pairs contain k/2 consecutive overlaps. What makes a class pair cheap is that the
  PARITY of the overlap is simple there (cosets of a self-orthogonal code: rank one), not that the overlap is confined.

## 2. Is the leftover the same problem on fewer addresses? Three exact answers

Write Z for the full subset-sum matrix; H = Z^T diag((-2)^|S|) Z. Every function of the overlap is Z^T (diagonal by level) Z.

**A. Lower window [0, d]: the leftover is N times the INVERSE of the transform on the heavy addresses.** [derived; ran at k = 8, every d: all rows for d >= 3, 12 random rows for d <= 2]
R is zero on every light row and column, and on heavy x heavy it equals N (H_heavy,heavy)^-1. (Block elimination: the cut-off
form is the unique matrix of rank M agreeing with H on the light rows and columns; the leftover is the Schur complement.)
Complementing addresses turns heavy x heavy into the ball of radius k-d-1 with signs. So "pay for the tails" = "solve a system
with the transform restricted to the complementary ball".
Corollary, pretty [derived from A; the identity itself ran at k = 8, where at d = 4 the inverse has 79 % zeros]: delete from H the rows and columns of weight at most b; the inverse of what is left is ZERO
on every pair with overlap at most b. At b = k/4 + 1.1 sqrt k that deletes a 2^(-0.19k) share of the addresses and the
inverse is zero on about 99 % of pairs [derived, not run at that size], while the inverse of H itself (H/N) has no zero at all.
The exact two-piece program (cut-off + inverse on the ball, both by subset sums) was counted for every d at k = 16, 64, 256
[ran, `count.out`]: between 1.000 k N (d = k-1, trivial) and 1.5 k N (d = k/4). Never below the textbook.

**B. Rank law.** [derived; ran mod 2^61-1 at k = 8, six windows] rank(leftover) = N - (sum of C(k, j) over the levels j <= d
where c_j = (-2)^j). Lower window: N - M. The top coefficient is c_d = (-1)^a (-2)^d [derived], so a window starting at an even
overlap a loses the top level, rank N - C(k, d) (seen at k = 6, window [2,4]: N - 15), and a window starting at an odd overlap
had full rank N in every case tried ([1,3], [1,4], [1,5] at k = 8). So a centred cut-off removes almost no rank.

**C. Centred window: the inverse of the leftover is the transform up to a tiny corner.** [derived; ran exactly, k = 6 by
outright inversion over the rationals, k = 8 by multiplying out all 65,536 entries, `dual.out`]

  N R^-1 = H + N F,  F nonzero only on pairs (x, y) whose UNION has at most d elements.

At k = 8, window [1,3]: R has 78.6 % zeros, F has 277 nonzero entries of 65,536. Window [1,5]: 89.6 % zeros, 7,459 entries.
Dually [ran, k = 6]: in the Walsh basis, H T H is nonzero only on pairs with union at most d, so the leftover there is H
itself with a corner altered. So **the transform is, up to M-sized corners, both "the sparse matrix R" seen in the Walsh
basis and "the inverse of the sparse matrix R"**.
**The level picture behind A, B, C** [derived; the instances above ran]. Every function of the overlap is
Phi(D) = Z^T diag(D_j on the sets of size j) Z, a (k+1)-parameter family; H is D_j = (-2)^j, the all-ones matrix is D = (1,0,0,...),
"disjoint or not" is D_j = (-1)^j, a cut-off form is D supported on j <= d, and the leftover just changes the levels j <= d.
Inversion is a symmetry of the family: Phi(D)^-1 [x, y] = (-1)^(|x|+|y|) Phi(D~)[complement of x, complement of y] with
D~_j = 1 / D_(k-j). Levels are reversed and inverted; the union of x and y is k minus the overlap of the complements. H is the
fixed point. That is the whole content of C: the low levels of R become the high levels of its inverse, which only pairs
with a small union can see.
The cleanest window is [1, d+1] (it misses overlap 0 only): its leftover has levels (-1)^j 2^(d+1) for j <= d, constant size
[derived; ran `regular.out`, k = 8, 10, 13], and the corner of its inverse is the leftover function of the LOWER window of
degree k-d-2 read backwards: N F(u) = (-1)^k r_low,k-d-2 (k-1-u) [ran, k = 6, 8, 10, 13, d = 2, 3, 4; a binomial identity I did
not prove]. Example k = 8, d = 2: (-352, -128, -32) = r_low,4 at t = 7, 6, 5. For windows starting at 3 or 5 the corner has
fractions and no such match [ran]; windows starting at an even overlap are singular (top level 0) [ran].

## 3. The wall, named, and what it is made of

[derived] **The tails cost as much as the whole.** R = H - T and H = R + T, so
  cost(H) - cost(T) - N <= cost(R) <= cost(H) + cost(T) + N.
With cost(T) = 4.45 sqrt(k) N: any program for the leftover of size o(N log N) IS a program for the transform of that size, and
conversely. "Pay only for the tails" pays full price unless the tails are done by something that is not "apply R".
The same for tensor powers [derived]: H on mk bits = R^(tensor m) plus 2^m - 1 terms that each pass through a waist and cost
about 2(d+1)N. So the transform is equivalent, within about 2^(m+1) (d+1) N, to a matrix of density (1 %)^m (rough count, not run).
What the wall uses: (i) the cheap part is a SUMMAND; (ii) cut-off forms have rank M = 2^(o(k)), so no sum or product of fewer
than N/M of them (or of identity-plus-them) has the rank of about N/2 that H - (any multiple of the identity) has; (iii) polynomials do
not localise (1c).
Went around twice, "yes, and":
1. Other frames (complement x, y or both; overlap replaced by |x|-t, |y|-t, k-|x|-|y|+t; weight scalings outside). On a
   central class pair every such form is still a degree-d polynomial in t. Same failure. [derived]
2. Multiply instead of add: identity C came out of this. It turns "apply a sparse matrix" into "solve with a sparse matrix"
   and puts the cheap part in a corner on both sides. It does not lower a count yet.

**What huge coefficients do here** [derived]: nothing that bounded ones cannot, for the cheap part: only M numbers are scaled,
and M 0.43k doublings is negligible. And det R = product of the level entries = det H up to a factor 2^(o(N)), so the
leftover carries the whole volume of H; in the bounded-coefficient model it is under the same Morgenstern bound as H.
The cut-off moves no volume. This is the precise sense in which the idea alone cannot change the order.

## 4. Things found on the way worth keeping

- [ran, `count2.out`; derived] **A family of full-rank +-1 matrices with linear-size circuits**: H restricted to the ball of
  radius w (size M = C(k, <=w)) costs 2 sum_{j<=w} j C(k,j) < 2 w M additions by subset sums on the ball (k = 8: 1.78 M, 3.46 M,
  4.99 M for w = 1, 2, 3, outputs checked). For fixed w that is linear in M; at k = 1024, w = 8 it is 0.25 M log2 M.
  log2 |det| is w M, so no volume bound is offended. This is the floor note's hatch "blocks run only on known zeros" with a
  concrete block: data known to be zero off a ball, outputs wanted on a ball. The centres are free [derived]: H between ANY two
  Hamming balls of radius w is the same matrix up to sign flips of rows and columns. I tried to tile the cube by radius-1 balls
  (a perfect Hamming code) and use this; the couplings between a ball coordinate and the other ball's centre cost k per output,
  so that particular tiling gives 2 k N. Not a wall, one try.
- [derived] The cut-off form of the lower window is P H, with P the projection "restrict the OUTPUT to the light ball, extend as the unique
  polynomial of degree d". So the cheap wrong answer is the low-degree extension of the light outputs, and the classical
  fact behind it is that a parity of k/2 bits agrees with a polynomial of degree sqrt k on most points.
- [derived] In the distance frame the transform is convolution by the "chirp" (-i)^|z| on the cube, which is its own Walsh
  transform up to scale (the relation (phase . H)^3 = constant). The cut-off is the low-pass part of the chirp; the leftover
  is convolution by a kernel living on the 1 % of z with unusual weight.
- [read, `readers/lit-lower-bounds.md`] The polynomial-in-the-overlap matrix is the Alman-Williams non-rigidity
  construction (arXiv 1611.05558); there the degree is a fraction of k and the sparse part has N^(1+eps) entries. Nothing I read
  uses identity C (the inverse of the leftover). Not searched on the web tonight.

## 5. The better question

The target is not H. It is ANY member of the class "H plus something cheap", or the inverse of any member:
**which member of {H - T : T cheap} or {(H - T)^-1} is easiest?** Candidates produced here: R (zero on the window pairs,
density down to roughly 2^(-(log N)^0.49) by tensoring [derived, rough]), its inverse (H plus a corner), the heavy block inverse of A.
Concrete next steps:
1. Nothing that touches entries can work: R has N^2/100 nonzero entries. What is left is structure: R and its inverse both
   live in the (k+1)-parameter level family of section 2, and the only programs known for a full-rank member are the two
   subset-sum passes (k N). A member with all levels nonzero and fewer gates would be news. [guess] At 16 points the family has
   5 parameters: run the exhaustive small-circuit search of `d-0-zeta/` (mincirc) over the levels (D_0..D_4) and see which
   full-rank functions of the overlap are cheapest, and whether H = (1, -2, 4, -8, 16) is the worst or the best of its family.
2. Explain the regularity N F(u) = r_low,k-d-2(k-1-u); it smells of a second duality (window [1, d+1] against lower window
   [0, k-d-2]) that would pair each centred problem with a ball problem.
3. True cost of the cut-off sums for d = 2: is 3N optimal, or is there a (1+eps)N scheme? d = 1 is settled at 2N.
4. Hand the ball family of section 4 to whoever works the hatch "blocks run only on known zeros".
5. [guess] sqrt(k) appears twice and may be one fact: the cut-off needs degree 2.2 sqrt k, and in the symmetry basis of the
   pick "spin" the transform falls into blocks of typical size sqrt k. Both say that the part of H that depends only on
   overlaps is worth sqrt(k) per point; the rest is the change of basis there and the tails here. Ask the spin developer whether
   the tails matrix R is simpler than H in that basis (it has the same symmetry, so it has the same blocks).
