# h-5 ladderbound: programs whose wires are entangled on at most two bits

**FIRST LINE. No circuit below the known counts and no growth law below N log N. What there is: a lemma that answers the brief's
"1/b or 2^(-b)" from the lower side, and an exhaustive run at 8 points in a finite part of the class.**
(1) [derived; checked on 14,055 gates] A gate can "give birth" on at most as many address bits as its more entangled parent is
entangled on. Hence **a program whose wires are entangled on at most b bits each (the b bits free to change from wire to wire)
needs at least N log2 N / b gates**, in count (G). So c(b) >= 1/b: the price cannot fall like 2^(-b), and **linear size needs
gates that read a wire entangled on about log2 N bits, not log log N**. (2) [ran, exhaustive] At 8 points, among programs whose
wires all lie in the pair class and have entries 0, +1, -1: **no program with 17 gates or fewer (333 million helper sets); 20 is reached (e2-0's staircase); 18 and 19 are open** (the run for 18 needs about four hours with tonight's pruning).
(3) The brief's number 18 = (3/4) N log2 N is therefore not excluded, but nothing I ran or designed gets below 20, and the form that fits every known case, the exact minimum 7 at 4 points included, is (3/4) N log2 N + N/4.
(4) [ran] The wire every record circuit turns on (the 4-point hub; the two flat wires of g-1's 19) is the real part of ONE complex product wire with factors (1, i) and (1, -i).

## 0. Words

N = 2^k points; an address is k bits; H is the Walsh-Hadamard matrix, its rows are the outputs. A *wire* is a linear form held
by the program, up to a scalar; written as a vector of length N it is a tensor with one index per address bit.
A wire is a *product across bit i* when it is (a 2-vector on bit i) times (something on the other bits). The bits where this
fails are the bits the wire is *entangled on*; their number is its *width* e(w). Width 0 = product wire. The *pair class* = width at most 2.
*Pure* 2-vector: (1,1) or (1,-1), written + and -. e0 = (1,0), e1 = (0,1). Inputs are products of e's, outputs are products of pure vectors.
K(i,t), for a bit i and a sign t: the forms that are "t on bit i, anything on the rest"; dimension N/2; it contains no input and
exactly the N/2 outputs with sign t on bit i.
*Slice*: all wires with a given factor on a given bit (for example "(1,1) on bit 0").

## 1. The count of births  [derived]

**Lemma 1 (any program).** Take a gate w = a*u + b*v with a, b not zero. If w is in K(i,t), then u and v are both in K(i,t) or
both outside it. Call the gate a *birth at (i,t)* when w is inside and the parents are outside. The dimension of
span{held wires in K(i,t)} starts at 0, must end at N/2, and rises (by one) only at a birth. So every (i,t) needs N/2 births, and
with c(g) = number of births of gate g (at most one sign per bit, so c(g) <= k):

    sum over gates of c(g) >= N log2 N.

**Lemma 2 (width).** c(g) <= max(1, e(u), e(v)): a gate gives birth on at most as many bits as its wider parent is entangled on.
Proof. Let S be the set of bits where g is a birth. (i) If some i in S is a product bit of both parents, kill the factor of w on
bit i with a functional: the two parents then have proportional "rests", so the three wires differ on bit i only and S = {i}.
(ii) If some i in S is entangled in u and not in v: w and v are products across i, so u = (factor)x(rest of w) + (factor)x(rest of v),
and both rests are contractions of u on bit i; a contraction keeps every factor u has outside its entangled bits; so w has the
same factors as u outside the entangled bits of u, and S lies inside them. (iii) Otherwise S lies inside the entangled bits of both parents.

**Theorem (free blocks).** If every wire is entangled on at most b bits, the program has at least N log2 N / b gates. More
exactly, for every program: sum over gates of max(1, width of the wider parent) >= N log2 N.
b = 1 is j-cross's product-wire lemma. j-cross had N log2 N / b for a FIXED partition into blocks; this is the same bound with
the block free to change from wire to wire, which is the class the brief asks about.
The statement holds in every basis of the address space at once (relabel the addresses by an invertible bit matrix; the program
still computes the transform), with widths measured in that basis.

**What it says about linear size.** A program with C*N gates must have gates whose wider parent has width log2 N / C on average.
Wide wires are not expensive in themselves (x0 + x7 has width 3 and costs one gate), so this is a necessary shape, not a wall.
The one-flip hub of e2-0 (leak 2), r = y_0 - 2 e_a, is the extreme case: a sum of two product wires, width k, and each of its N
output gates is a birth on all k bits at once; its cost is in the partners.

## 2. The pair class (b = 2), taken apart  [derived; each statement checked on the pool of section 4]

**Three kinds of gate, and no other.** In a program of pair-class wires every gate is
- type I: along one bit (the three wires share everything else);
- type II: along one pair of bits (the three wires share a product on the other bits; on the pair they are three collinear 4-vectors);
- type III, the *triangle*: on three bits a, b, c the parents are entangled on {b,c} and {a,c} and the result on {a,b}.
  Example at 8 points (bits written b0 b1 b2): (e010 + e001 + e011) + (e100 - e001 + e101) = (e01. + e10.) x (1,1).
  Two disjoint pairs, or a chain of three pairs, cannot meet in a gate. [ran, `gatetypes.out`: of the 4,672 gates of the pool, 480 are of type I, 3,168 of type II, 1,024 triangles, none of another kind.]

**The ledger.** Let n_0, n_1, n_2 be the numbers of gates that raise the sum of the 2k dimensions of Lemma 1 by 0, 1, 2 (never
more, by Lemma 2). The sum ends at exactly N log2 N, so

    gates = N log2 N - n_2 + n_0.

A *double* (n_2) is a gate whose result is (pure on bit i) x (pure on bit j) x (rest) and it needs, held at that moment, a wire
entangled on exactly {i,j} with the same rest (the *hub* of its slice), plus a partner. A *zero* (n_0) raises nothing.
**A saved gate is a double minus a zero.** Further facts: the first entangled wire of a program is a zero; with exactly one zero
there is exactly one entangled wire, at most 4 doubles, so at least N log2 N - 3 gates; an entangled wire that is not a zero is a
copy of a hub into a pure slice from two other slices, or a triangle.
- 4 points, the brick two ways [derived]: (n_2, n_1, n_0) = (2, 4, 1) with the pair sums p, q, and (4, 0, 3) with the two
  diagonal sums x0 + x3, x1 - x2 in place of p, q. Both 7.
- 8 points, e2-0's staircase [ran, `meters2.out`]: (8, 8, 4): 24 - 8 + 4 = 20. Each of its 4 hubs is a zero and pays for two doubles.
- The conjecture "at least (3/4) N log2 N + N/4 in the pair class" is the same statement as **4 n_0 + n_1 >= 2 n_2 + N**
  [derived]. The two bricks and the staircase all sit on it with equality (8 = 8, 12 = 12, 24 = 24).
  The brief's form, (3/4) N log2 N without the N/4, is not tight at 4 points (it says 6; the exact minimum is 7); the form with N/4 is. So the number to test at 8 points is 20 rather than 18.
- Why two doubles per hub and not four, in every design I tried by hand (five of them, section 5): a hub's slice holds 8 units of
  room (2 per bit of the pair and sign); the wires that had to be brought into the slice to build the hub and its partners already use half of it.

## 3. Outside the class: g-1's 19 on the same ledger  [ran, `meters2.out`]

With wider wires the ledger reads gates = N log2 N - (sum over gates of (raise - 1) when positive) + n_0. The 19-gate program has
6 gates that raise three dimensions at once (each has a parent of width 3, as Lemma 2 demands), 2 doubles, 2 singles and 9 zeros:
24 - (12 + 2) + 9 = 19. Exchange rates: staircase 8 extra units for 4 zeros (2.0 per zero); the 19: 14 for 9 (1.56 per zero).
So the 19 wins by having almost no singles, not by a better rate. Linear size needs the rate (extra units per zero gate) to grow like log2 N.
In this language j-cross's guessed law L1 (gates saved <= over-limit wires) reads "extra units minus zeros <= over-limit wires": the staircase has 8 - 4 = 4 with 4 such wires [ran], the 19 has 14 - 9 = 5 with 5 (g7 to g11) [ran, `meters2.out`]. Equality both times.
Second reading of the same file: widths depend on the basis. The centre of the 19 (+ - - - + + - +) has width 3 in the standard
bits and width 2 after a relabelling of the addresses; its first four wires (x0 + x7 and so on) have width 3 in the standard bits and
width 0 after relabelling; only g8 and g9 (s*s' = 8N) have width 3 in all 168 bases. So the 19 is NOT in the pair class in any single basis.

(c) **Sums of two product wires in different partitions.** Not covered by any of this, and for a reason: the sum of two product

g10 = Re((1+i) (1,i) x (1,i) x (1,-i)). The 4-point hub is the same object one size down: (1,1,-1,1) = Re((1-i) (1,i) x (1,-i)).

So the wire that every record circuit turns on is, over the complex numbers, a product wire plus its conjugate: width k for the
real meter, a sum of only two products for the complex one. (1, i) and (1, -i) are the two eigenvectors of the 2 x 2 kernel written as a
rotation, so this is the "wire flat on both sides" of the earlier reports, now with its tensor shape.

that number of points is the size of the partner (at 8 points the partners x1 + x6 and so on of the 19). It is the old anomaly 1, 6, 28, 120 of PICKS.md once more, now as the price list of this hub.

## 6. Which meter prices a wire: entangled bits, or s*s'?  [ran at 4 points]

They disagree already at 4 points, in both directions, and the gate count follows neither alone:
- (1,0,0,1) = x0 + x3: entangled on two bits, yet tight (s = s' = 2, s*s' = N).
- (1,2,0,0) = (1,0) x (1,2): a product wire, yet over the limit (s = 2, s' = 4, s*s' = 2N).
- [ran, `tp4_p5.out`, `tp4_p7.out`, `tp4_p11.out`] 4 points, pool = all product wires plus all tight wires (so both kinds above are allowed):
  no program with 7 gates modulo 5, 7 and 11 (4,960, 34,220 and 447,580 helper sets), 8 reached; with every wire allowed 7 is found at once,
  and its hub (1,1,1,-1) is entangled AND over the limit. (Modulo a prime "none" is not "none" over the rationals; three primes agree.)
So a saving needs a wire that fails both tests at once. [guess] The union lemma "every wire is tight or a product => N log2 N" is true; neither a-2's proof nor section 1 gives it.
At 8 points the three meters separate further (section 3): x0 + x7 is tight, has width 3 in the standard bits and width 0 in another basis.
The meter that is the same in every basis is s*s'; the meter that bounds births is the width. A wire pays only when both are large.
