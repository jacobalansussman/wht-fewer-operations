# h-1 staircase3: the three-bit brick taken apart, interleaved, and why the saving does not double

**FIRST LINES. No circuit below the best known count and no growth law below N log N.** Count (G) throughout (a gate is any
a*u + b*v, scalings free). What came out of the hour:
1. The 19-gate circuit at 8 points is not a three-bit hub. Written in the staircase's language it is **a two-bit hub on two address
   bits that are NOT adjacent (bits 0 and 2), with the middle bit riding along**, plus two wires entangled on three bits whose
   only job is to be the shared first half of two different two-point sign flips. 19 = 21 - 2, and the 2 are those two shared
   halves [ran: the factorisation of every wire; derived: the count 21 for the same design without three-bit wires].
2. Interleaving it does not give 7/12. Nothing below (3/4) N log2 N was found: 16 points stays at 52, the half problem at 16 points
   (which is the 32-point test) stays at 44, 8 points stays at 19 [ran, annealing, upper bounds only; numbers in section 4].
   The annealer did interleave the 19 on its own, from 54: it lands on **a second 52-gate circuit of a different shape** (two
   unfinished 19s under a top level with wires entangled on all four address bits), exact, `h-1-staircase3/slp16_52_from54.txt`. A tie.
3. Why, in one sentence: a flip that changes a character bit two levels below the top needs pieces three levels down **for every
   character and for both signs**, and that demand is exactly twice what a thin level can supply [derived, section 5].
4. A lemma that turns e2-0's wall into an exact count: **in the staircase's own class (pieces and two-piece hubs on one chain of
   halvings) the levels cost at least 3N/4 gates each on average, so (3/4) N log2 N + N/4 is the exact optimum of that class**, mixtures with
   textbook steps included [derived by hand, section 3; not machine-checked].
5. Level-dependent hub M = A + t*B (e2-0 mutation 5): t = 1 or -1 is forced. For any other t every one of the four partners has
   two terms and the 4-point brick costs 11 gates, not 7 [ran, exact fractions, `mut5.out`].
6. The rate as the window grows: in every circuit of tonight a wire that is not a piece stands in for at most two pieces the
   program skips (exactly two in the designed circuits). With that, a window of w bits can at best reach **1/2 + 2^(-w)** (3/4, 5/8, 9/16, ...): the limit is 1/2, the
   floor of law L1, **not zero**; and w = 3 already misses its ideal 5/8 [derived, section 5]. No change of order in this class.

## 0. Words

N = 2^k points, address bits numbered 0 (pairs) to k-1 (top). A *block* of size 2^j is the set of addresses with the top k-j bits
fixed. A *piece* is one character (a row of signs following a parity rule) on one block, zero elsewhere; pieces are the wires the
textbook holds. I write a wire as one symbol per address bit: `0` or `1` = that bit is fixed (the wire lives on that half),
`+` or `-` = the wire is the even or odd character along that bit. A piece of size 2^j is `c c .. c | z z .. z`: signs on bits
below j, fixed bits from j up. A *hub* is a wire that is not such a product: on two bits p, q it carries one of the four sign
patterns with an odd number of minus signs, for example `[+++-]`. The 4-point brick is the identity
(output on bits p,q) = +-(hub) +- 2*(input on bits p,q), four outputs from one hub, each with a single input as partner.
*Over the limit*: s*s' > N, where s and s' count the non-zeros of the wire written in the inputs and in the outputs.
A *flip* of a sign wire v on a set A is v - 2*(v restricted to A): one gate when "v restricted to A" is held.

## 1. First experiment (a): the 19 in the staircase's language  [ran, `anat.py`, exact integers]

Relabel the addresses of g-1's circuit by z0 = x0, z1 = x0+x1, z2 = x1+x2 (an invertible bit matrix; it permutes the outputs).
Then every wire is a product of one-bit factors except five (`anat.py seed8_19.txt 3 1 3 6`):

| wires | in the chain language | s, s' | what e2-0 has in that place |
|---|---|---|---|
| g1..g4 | four pieces of size 2: `+ z1 0`, `- z1 1` (sign on bit 0 tied to bit **2**) | 2, 4 | four pieces of size 2 with the sign tied to bit 1 |
| g5, g6 | two pieces of size 4: `+ - 0`, `- - 1` | 4, 2 | four pieces of size 4 and two 4-point hubs |
| g7, the centre | **hub on bits 0 and 2, `-` on bit 1**: `[+++-]` on (0,2) | 8, 4 | nothing |
| g8, g9 | sign vectors entangled on all three bits (the centre with one input flipped) | 8, 8 | nothing |
| g10, g11 | hubs on bits 1 and 2: `- [+++-]`, `+ [+---]` | 8, 4 | **the same pair** (hub11, hub16): one hub on bits (1,2) for each sign on bit 0, other sign patterns |

So the two circuits end with the same two hubs on bits (1,2). The 20 builds them from below (4 pieces of size 4, which cost 2
hubs on bits (0,1)): 10 gates under the top. The 19 builds ONE hub on the non-adjacent pair (0,2) from a bare tree (4 + 2 + 1 = 7
gates), takes its own four outputs (those with `-` on bit 1), and reaches the two (1,2)-hubs from it by flipping two inputs each.
- *Pieces of which characters on which blocks*: one character per pair, one per block of four, chosen so that the two halves
  carry characters that differ in bit 0, the bit furthest from the join.
- *The witness*: in e2-0 a witness is a piece two levels down. Here the centre differs from each of its outputs on two single
  inputs that are not a pair of the tree, so the witness is two pieces three levels down (two single inputs), taken one after
  the other. The wire in between (g8, g9) is the only genuinely three-bit object.
- *Where the gate is saved*: with two-bit hubs only, each "flip two inputs" costs 2 gates (make the two-input piece, flip): 21.
  g8 is the first half of two such flips at once ({7,1} gives an output, {7,6} gives hub g10); g9 the same. Two shared halves: 19.
- Meter: 5 gates saved, 5 over-limit wires (centre and two hubs at 4N, g8 and g9 at 8N): law L1 holds with equality again.
- Gates by size of support: the 19 has 4, 2, 13; the 20 has 4, 6, 10. The 19 is thin below and fat on top.

## 2. The level picture (the tool for everything below)  [derived]

Fix a block B of size 2m with halves L, R. A full-support wire on B that is a character on each half is (alpha on L) + s*(beta on R),
alpha, beta characters of the half: a point (alpha, beta, s). alpha = beta is a piece of B (a *target*); alpha != beta is a hub.
Gates on B, each one new wire:
- *join*: (alpha on L) and (beta on R) held;
- *top move*: change the top bit of alpha (or beta) by flipping a quarter of B; partner = a piece two levels down;
- *second move*: change the second bit from the top by flipping two eighths of B, two gates through a three-bit wire; partners =
  two pieces three levels down. Two second moves that share an eighth share the middle wire (the 19's trick);
- *half move*: change s when alpha on L or beta on R is held.
A level's cost is the number of points it visits. Targets are forced; everything else is overhead.
e2-0's level: join -> hub -> two top moves: 3 gates per 2 targets. The 19's top: 1 join, 2 middle wires, 2 hubs, 8 targets.

## 3. Lemma: 3N/4 per level is exact for the staircase's class  [derived; bottom-up programs]

Class: pieces and two-piece hubs on one chain of halvings, any gates among them. I checked by hand that the only gates are
joins, top moves, half moves and textbook steps (a hub and a piece, or two hubs, differ by a piece only in those cases).
None of these changes the character below the top bit of the half. Call a *cell* the set of points of B with that lower part
fixed. (i) Every cell that contains a target contains a join, so both halves hold a piece of the cell. (ii) At the top every
cell has targets; by (i), going down, every cell at every level has targets with both values of its top character bit.
(iii) A cell with t targets costs t gates if all four half pieces of the cell are held, and t + 1 otherwise. (iv) A cell with
all four half pieces forces one more target in each of two cells one level down. So with a fraction x of such cells the cost
per cell is 3 + x, least at x = 0: 3 per cell, N/4 cells per level, **3N/4 per level**; the top level needs 5 per cell.
Total (3/4) N log2 N + N/4, which is gen.py's count. e2-0 marked this "a guess for mixtures with textbook steps"; this covers them.
Not covered: programs that make a small piece by subtracting from a larger one (I assumed bottom-up), and I did not machine-check it.
The 19 leaves the class twice: its hub's two characters differ in a bit that is not the top one, and it has three-bit wires.

**A second 52, of a different shape** [ran; `lx16_b.txt`, replayed exactly by `check.py` into `slp16_52_from54.txt`]. Started
from the tensored 19 (two 19s and a textbook top, 54 gates) the annealer interleaved by itself: each half keeps the 19's tree,
centre and flipped wires but finishes only 4 of its 8 outputs, and the top level takes over with 6 full-support wires that are
not outputs (4 entangled on all four address bits, s*s' = 8N; 2 on bits 1 to 3) and the 16 outputs. Gates by size of support:
8, 4, 18, 22 (the staircase: 8, 12, 12, 20). It ties the staircase to the gate by another route, with 16 over-limit wires for
12 gates saved (L1 with slack 4; the staircase has 12 for 12). So "interleave the three-bit brick with overlap two" has an
answer at 16 points: **52 again**.
*Overlap by two bits* is the 16-point run (windows on bits 0-2 and 1-3): a tie, nothing below. *Overlap by one bit* first shows at 32 points;
the half problem at 16 points is its inner part.
**Half problems at 8 points, a regularity.** A top level with hubs on bits (2,3) asks each 8-block for the four pieces of size 8
with one fixed top sign, and for one witness of size 4 per character, on a four-block f(character) that the hub's sign pattern
lets one choose freely. For all 16 choices of f and both signs: **16 gates when f puts two witnesses on each four-block, 17 for
three and one, 18 for four and none**, never less. e2-0's D(8) = 16 is one of six balanced choices and all six tie. Read with
`anat.py`, the solutions for the other balanced f are the staircase again in another order of the address bits (for f by bit 0:
pairs along bit 1, four-point hubs on (0,1), eight-point hubs on the non-adjacent pair (0,2)): 4 pieces, 2 + 2 hubs, 8 targets.
[derived, afterwards] That tie explains itself: the six balanced f are the affine functions of the character, and an invertible
relabelling of the two low address bits carries any one to any other. The content of the table is the other half: an unbalanced
witness pattern costs one gate per witness moved (17, 18), so the staircase's pattern is the cheapest one, not just one of many.
**Calibration of the tool, against it.** In the last row above the annealer missed, from the textbook seed, a 44 that is known to
exist, and stopped 4 gates higher. So at 16 points "not found" from a far seed means little; the runs seeded AT the record
(no 51, no 43, tens of thousands of visits to the record count) are the ones to weigh, and they are local statements.
(Without witnesses the four pieces alone cost 11 = 4 + 7: transform the top bit first, then one brick. The witnesses are the price.)
By hand [derived, not replayed]: e2-0's top over two 19-shaped halves is 2*17 + 20 = 54; a 19-shaped top over staircase bottoms is 58.

## 5. First experiment (c): the recursion, and why three-bit windows do not compound  [derived by hand; I did not extend gen.py]

In a recursion the "inputs" of a window are pieces that cost gates, so what matters is how many pieces each level must hold.
A piece of size 2^j is asked for in three roles: as a half piece one level up (joins), as a quarter piece two levels up (top
moves), as an eighth piece three levels up (second moves). The third role is the hard one: the context of a second move is the
WHOLE character of the eighth, so second moves in every context ask for every character on the eighths they use.
- *The 19's centre at every level* ("skip hub": a piece is held when its second character bit from the top equals the side of
  its block). Consistent in all three roles, needs no top moves, but each level must deliver both signs of every piece it makes
  (the third role asks for them): join, 2 second moves (4 gates), 2 half moves = 7 gates for 4 targets per 8 points.
  Recursion P(m) = 2 P(m/2) + 7m/8: rate **7/8**. Worse than the staircase.
- *Thin levels* (2 targets per 8 points, 5 gates: hub and two second moves) would give 5/8, but each character would have to
  sit on two eighths of the same half and it sits on one: the demand is exactly twice the supply. With sign pairs added it is the line above.
- *Mixtures* of thin and staircase levels, three different ones counted by hand: (6), (8 and 4), (6, 4, 5, 9) gates per 8 points
  per level. **Each averages 6, that is 3/4 again.** I did not check all roles in the last two.
- Counting resources alone (pieces asked for <= pieces held, in each role) allows 2/3: two thirds thin levels, one third
  staircase levels, 16/3 gates per 8 points. I found no pattern that realises it; each one I wrote down fails in the third role.

**The ideal ladder** [derived]. Count per level: gates = (pieces held) + (wires that are not pieces). Call a piece *skipped* when
the textbook holds it and the program does not. Then gates saved = skipped - non-pieces, and
**skipped = 2 * non-pieces exactly** in the four designed circuits [ran on the counts: brick 2 and 1; the 20: 8 and 4; the 19: 10 and 5;
the staircase's 52: 24 and 12]. The annealer's second 52 has 28 and 16; the two 21-gate circuits of the second wave have 9 and 6,
14 and 11 [read: j-cross's counts]. So: never more than two skipped pieces per non-piece wire, and exactly two in the clean designs.
This is law L1 read from the other side: a non-piece wire is a sum of two pieces standing in for two missing ones (the hub
(alpha on L) + (beta on R) replaces "alpha on R" and "beta on L"; a stepping stone replaces two more).
If a level holds a fraction f of its pieces and the identity holds, the level costs (1 + f) N / 2.
A window of w bits can in principle get by with f = 2^(1-w) (one half piece per half and per context), which would give
**r*(w) = 1/2 + 2^(-w): 3/4, 5/8, 9/16, ... with limit 1/2**, not zero. w = 2 reaches its ideal (e2-0). w = 3 does not: the
thin level (hub + two second moves, 5 gates for 2 targets per 8 points, 3 non-pieces for 6 skipped) is the ideal 5/8, and it asks
for 4 pieces three levels down per 2 it delivers. So in this class the limit of r(w) is at best the L1 floor (1/2) N log2 N.
A change of order needs a non-piece wire that stands in for a number of missing pieces that grows with N; the three-bit wires
of the 19 are not that (each replaces two, like a hub).

**The rate r(w)** (leading constant of N log2 N, count (G)):

| window | plain tensor | interleaved |
|---|---|---|
| 2 bits | 7/8 | 3/4, and exact for its class (section 3) |
| 3 bits | 19/24 = 0.792 | ideal 5/8, not reachable (counting); counting allows 2/3; found: mixtures 3/4 (hand count), skip-hub pattern 7/8 |
| w bits | not known beyond 3 | ideal 1/2 + 2^(-w), limit 1/2 |

The curator's guess 7/12 (the saving doubles) is not what happens: the three-bit brick is thin below and fat on top (4, 2, 13),
and interleaving needs the opposite, a level that is thin on top.
*Does r(w) tend to zero?* No, in this class: to a positive constant, at best 1/2 (the ideal ladder), and the realised value
has not moved from 3/4. A move that changes the character bit i levels below the top costs 2^(i-1) gates and asks for pieces
i + 1 levels down in every context; the ideal ladder assumes those pieces are there, and at w = 3 they are short by a factor 2.
Only the linear term moved tonight (N/8 at 8 points against N/4).
*Coefficients.* The best rate here, 3/4, is far above Morgenstern's 0.315 for coefficients 1 and 2, so no window reached tonight
forces the coefficients to grow. And they cannot grow on this skeleton: with M_t = (1,1,t,-t) the partner of each output is
proportional to (1-t, 1+t) on a pair, a single input only when t*t = 1 [ran, `mut5.py`, t = 2, 3, 1/2, -2, 5/3, 7: 11 gates each].
So growing coefficients need a different witness, not a different t.

## 6. What I learned, as statements

1. [ran] The 19 = one hub on non-adjacent bits + two shared half-flips. Its only three-bit wires are stepping stones read by two gates each.
2. [derived] (3/4) N log2 N + N/4 is exact for pieces and two-piece hubs on one chain.
3. [ran] 8 points: no 18 in 34 million moves in the full 0, 1, -1 wire space. 16 points: no 51 in 84 million, and two different 52s. Half problem at
   16 points: no 43 in 31 million. Half problems at 8 points: 16, 17, 18 by the balance of the witnesses.
4. [derived] The obstruction to a thin interleaved level is a factor of exactly 2 in one role (both signs of every character on
   the eighths). An obstruction with a factor 2 in it is the kind that a cleverer pattern sometimes removes.
5. [ran] The level-dependent hub is rigid: t = +-1.
6. [derived on the counts] skipped pieces = 2 * (wires that are not pieces) in the brick, the 20, the 19 and the 52. With it a level
   that holds a fraction f of its pieces costs (1 + f) N / 2, the ideal ladder is 1/2 + 2^(-w), and the limit is 1/2, not 0.
7. [guess] "6 gates per 8 points per level" is conserved over the mixtures the way "64" was conserved over butterflies.

## 7. Mutations (generation; not judged)

1. **Decide the pattern question exactly.** A translation-invariant design is a yes/no rule on 6 bits (the top three character
   bits of a piece and the three address bits above its block), plus a finite list of level shapes. "Is there a rule with average
   level cost below 6 per 8 points?" is a finite problem for an integer programme or a SAT solver: 2/3 by counting, 3/4 known.
   It is the whole three-bit question in one run.
2. Rules that are not translation invariant but have period 2 or 3 in the level (the 19 itself has period 3 and gives 19/24; a
   period-2 rule could alternate skip hubs and adjacent hubs so that the sign pairs one asks for are the witnesses of the other).
3. Break "both signs" directly: a second move whose two eighths lie in DIFFERENT halves (a flip set that is a coset of another
   subgroup). That is e2-0's leak 1 (pieces on a quadric); the factor 2 is its natural target.
4. Hubs on bits (j, j+d) for growing d with the bits between as riders: the 19 says d = 2 is cheap below; what does d = 3 cost on top?
5. The half-problem table 16, 17, 18: prove 16 is least for 8 targets (4 pieces of size 8, 4 of size 4); it is a small exact
   statement (mincirc-sized) and it is the staircase's lower bound one level up.
6. Give the annealer a level-aware move (swap a whole cell between "staircase" and "thin") and run 32 and 64 points on the Linux machine for hours.
7. The same three roles for the published unit: which role is the scarce one there?

## 8. Criticism, last

- Every "no 51", "no 43", "no 18", "16" is an annealer's best, not a minimum. The warm 16-point runs (temperature 0.35 to 0.40)
  spent most of their time 5 to 8 gates above the seed and are weak evidence; the cooler run (0.27) and the half-problem run (0.30) are the better ones.
- The annealer cannot start from nothing (it needs a seed), like g-1's. Everything found is near e2-0's and g-1's circuits.
- Only wires with entries 0, 1, -1 were searched. Wall M says such pools cannot change the order anyway.
- Section 3 is one hand derivation; the case check of gate shapes was done on paper; "bottom-up" is an assumption.
- Sections 2 to 5 live on ONE chain of halvings. With the bit order free, thin targets are cheap in another order (the four
  pieces of size 8 with one top sign cost 11, not 15); the annealer searches all orders at once and still found nothing, but the
  hand argument does not cover designs that change the order from context to context.
- Section 5's counts (7/8, the three mixtures at 6, the bound 2/3, the ladder) are hand counts. None was built and replayed; I did not extend
  gen.py, so first experiment (c) is answered by recursions on paper, not by a program.
