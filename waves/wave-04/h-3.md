Counts. **(G)**: a gate is any a*u + b*v, scalings free. **(O)**: OpenAI's count, every addition, subtraction and multiplication

Words. N = 2^k points. H is the matrix of signs (-1)^(number of common 1-bits of row and column); "H x exactly" means with no
factor in front. A *window of w bits* is a small circuit for the 2^w-point transform, applied along w address bits at a time.
A *bit* (e-5's meter) is a unit of the entropy of the squared entries of the program-so-far; the finished transform has N log2 N of them.

## 1. What OpenAI's statement asks, and what a halving costs  [read]

- **It wants the transform exactly, with no factor.** `R/OAI/Computability/FourierTransform/Goal.lean`, lines 18-19: `dft n x` is
  the plain sum of root^(j k) x_k; line 58: the result must EQUAL `dft n x`. The project's own Walsh-Hadamard statement
  (`R/README.md`, lines 615-617) asks for exactly `wht k x` in Sylvester order. Neither accepts H x / sqrt N.
- **A halving is one unit, like an addition.** `RAM.lean` line 163 (`scale`, a prepared scalar times a data number), line 188
  (billed `Bill.one`), lines 186-187 (`add`, `sub`, also `Bill.one`). The scalar 1/2 has to be made once: the machine has no
  instruction for a constant (header, lines 27-29), only `cone` (the number 1), `add` and `inv` (lines 160, 161, 164): 1, then
  1 + 1, then its inverse, three units once per program. Doubling needs no scalar: x + x is one `add`.
- **OpenAI's circuit count is count (O) exactly.** `R/OAI/Computability/FourierCircuit/Core.lean` lines 9-17: a gate is `add`,
  `sub` or `scale c` for any complex c, one unit each (the skeptic b-0 read the same lines; I agree with b-0 section 2 throughout).
- **But the statements are blind to constants.** `Core.lean` lines 47-50: the circuit statement asks for size below c n log2 n
  for EVERY c > 0. `Goal.lean` lines 75-77: the machine's time bound is "W = O(...)" twice and "W = o(n log n)".
  And the machine charges every composition, every array read and every loop step too (`RAM.lean` lines 229-245), so one
  operation of count (O) is some fixed number of machine units, not one; the header says such constant rescalings "are
  immaterial". So count (O) is an idealisation of the machine's bill (the scalar part only). A constant in front of N log N,
  5/6 or 3/4 or 1/100, is not something the Lean statement measures. [read + derived]
- **Could the engine use a unit that returns H x / sqrt N?** In principle yes, and it would not matter. [derived from
  `R/README.md` section 6, not checked against the engine's code] A move transforms one array along one direction; additions
  are between arrays with equal labels, and arrays with equal labels have had the same moves, so the same known power of two
  sits in front of both. One multiplication per point at the very end (N units, lower order) removes it; the engine already
  multiplies by 1/2 in places. What it buys is a constant in the cost of a move. It does not touch the exponent: OpenAI's own
  exponent (`Goal.lean` lines 25-29) is log(M - Delta/W) / log M, three fixed numbers (`paperM`, `paperW`, `paperDelta`;
  [guess] the network's directions, arrays and moves saved) and nothing else; the cost of a small transform does not appear
  in it. [read] I did not open the project's own formula for 8.7625e-4. This bears on the question b-0 left
  open in its section 6 ("unless the engine's exponent depends on the unit's cost"). And a cheaper move moves
  the size where the published saving shows in a real count OUT by the same factor (e-5's rough dip, "more than
  19,380 / (120 c) digit columns", grows when the cost c of a move falls).

## 4. What the (O) price of a circuit is made of  [derived; `oprice.py`, `oprice.out`]

**Pricing rule.** Keep a (G) circuit's wires and gates. Let each wire be held at one or more scales. A gate w = u + 2v or u - 2v
runs for one addition if v is held at twice the scale of u; a gate u + v or u - v if both are at one scale. Every further
scale of a wire costs one multiplication. So **(O) price = gates + extra copies**. A search over the scale of every gate
[ran] gives: 4-point brick 7 + 1 = 8 (search complete) for H x / 2 and 11 for H x; g-1's 19 gates: 19 + 1 = 20 for H x / 2;
e2-0's 20 gates: 20 + 6 = 26; e2-0's 52 gates at 16 points: 52 + 8 = 60 for H x / 4. The last three searches stopped at
their node limit, so they are upper bounds (b-0 priced e2-0's family the same way with an integer programme and has the same
numbers); for g-1's unit the "+1" is exact by hand: with no extra copy the seven tree
gates put the centre at the scale of the inputs, and the gate "centre - 2 x7" needs it at half that scale.

[ran, `oprice2.py`, `oprice2.out`] The four other 19-gate circuits that g-1's annealer ended on (`sa8_known0..3.out`; each
wire has exactly one way to be written as a gate) all price the same: 20 for H x / 2, 28 for H x itself, 28 for 2 * H x.
(A faster, restricted search; upper bounds for the rule.) So among the five 19-gate circuits on file none has another scale structure.

**Two worlds.** In g-1's unit the wires fall into two sets: the inputs and the six pieces of the merge tree, held at scale 1,
and the centre with everything made from it (four walk wires and the eight outputs), held at scale 1/2. All twelve gates with
a coefficient 2 join the two worlds, so the 2 is the ratio of the worlds and costs nothing. The only wire born in one world
and used in the other is the centre: one halving. Five additions saved for one multiplication. e-5's sentence "one scaling per
addition saved, forever" is true of the brick (1 for 1), half true of the interleaved family (2 saved per halving) and false
here (5 per halving). For H x exactly the factor must be given back at N more multiplications, and then the trade is again
worse than one for one until 512 points (960 additions saved, 704 multiplications).

**The determinant bound with scalings charged** [derived, Morgenstern's argument re-run for (O)]. Let a be the number of
additions and subtractions and c_1, ..., c_m the constants multiplied by. The largest minor of the matrix of all wires at
most doubles at an addition and grows by at most max(1, |c_i|) at a multiplication. Hence, for outputs c * H x,
a + sum of log2 max(1, |c_i|) >= (N/2) log2 N + N log2 |c|.
For H x exactly with halvings only: a >= (N/2) log2 N. For H x / sqrt N: the right side is 0, no bound at all. And the N
final multiplications by sqrt N carry exactly (N/2) log2 N bits between them. So in (O) the determinant obstruction is a debt
of N operations, payable at the end, and the order question is entirely the orthonormal transform with halvings, where no
determinant bound applies. (j-cross's wall M, "all wires sign vectors up to scale", is a different statement and still
gives 0.315 N log2 N for every program in the table.)

## 5. "1/1.2 = 5/6": one object or a coincidence?  [ran, `link.py`, `link.out`, exact fractions]

Relatives, not the same matrix, and the two numbers are not equal.
- Same skeleton: one signed sum of 8 points (7 additions), halved once, then subtractions. e-5's gate R_8 subtracts it from
  each point (8 more operations, 16 in all, 17.74 bits on fresh data, an unfinished transform). g-1's unit walks from it
  through 12 flips (20 in all, 24 bits, the finished transform). e-5's 1.205 is a hill-climbed floating-point estimate for the
  unfinished gate on prepared data; 24/20 = 1.2 is exact. Near-coincidence of numbers [guess].
- **Exact identity** for g-1's centre c = (+ - - - + + - +): H_8 = M * R_8(c), where four rows of M are rows of H_8 (the four
  characters orthogonal to c) and four rows are two-point differences with coefficient 2. So after the reflection and one
  layer of butterflies, half of the outputs are finished and the other half need a 4-point transform.
- That looked like a linear recursion, T(N) = 3N + T(N/2). **It is the textbook in disguise** [derived]: the identity says that
  R_8(c) is "butterflies, then H_4 / 2 on the differences, then butterflies back", and H_(N/2) times 4/N is orthogonal only at
  N = 8. By that route the 8-point transform costs 32, not 20.
- **Why one centre is an 8-point object** [ran at 4, 8, 16, 32; derived in general]: by Parseval a sign vector can have
  correlation N/2 with at most four characters, so one reflection finishes exactly four rows at every size (4 of 4, 4 of 8,
  4 of 16, 4 of 32; a bent centre at 16 points finishes none).
- **Many centres at once fall apart into the known gates** [ran, `block.py`, `block.out`, 8, 16, 32 points; derived]: the
  reflection through N/4 centres (the hub pattern times every character that ignores two chosen bits) is orthogonal, has
  4 non-zeros per row, entries 1/2 and -1/2, and finishes all N rows: it is one layer of 4-point bricks. Through N/8 centres
  it is one layer of e-5's gate R_8 and finishes half the rows. So "a reflection through k sums" (e-5's mutation M2), with the
  sums taken as a subgroup, is nothing new.
- **The primitive both are made of** [derived]: *reflect one held wire in another held wire*. If w and p are wires and the
  projection of w on p is t * p, then w - 2t * p is the mirror image of w, for one subtraction once the copy 2t * p is at
  hand. e-5's gate reflects the 8 inputs in the centre (one scaled copy of the centre, 8 subtractions). g-1's walk reflects
  the halved centre in tree pieces, and there 2t = 1 every time: a sign vector at scale 1/2 projects on a piece it agrees
  with as exactly half the piece. That is the whole reason the twelve flips need no multiplication. One primitive, two uses.

## 6. The question to keep asking: is there a ceiling on bits per operation for every gate?

- **No ceiling holds for every gate, and the proof is the published theorem** [derived]: its work is o(N log N) in a count that
  charges every addition and every scaling, and the finished transform has N log2 N bits, so bits per operation grow without
  bound along that program (like (log N)^(8.76e-4)). What exists is a ceiling for each width: call r(w) the largest
  (bits made) / ((O) operations) over all programs that act as one rotation, up to a factor, on w points.
- Trivial ceiling [derived]: r(w) <= log2 w (w points hold at most w log2 w bits, and each point that the gate changes is
  the result of at least one addition). Linear size is the statement that r(w) stays above a constant times log2 w.
- Known values: r(2) = 1; r(4) = 1 for the flat gate (e-5's exhaustive search: 8 is the minimum); r(8) >= 1.2 (g-1);
  r(16) >= 64/56 = 1.143; r(64), r(512) >= 1.2 [ran]. A tensor product never lowers the rate below the worse factor, so
  the ceiling over all widths up to w never falls.
- **e-5's fall from 6 to 12 points is a fact about one gate shape** [ran + derived]: a single reflection changes the matrix by
  rank one and makes about 8 log2 q - 2 bits for large q (exact values 8, 17.74, 27.22 at q = 4, 8, 16) for 2q operations.
  The one-centre walk of g-1 has the same rise and fall (1.0, 1.2, and at most about 1.0 at 16 points by g-1's search), for the
  Parseval reason above. Two shapes, one cause: a single full-support sum is near only four outputs.
- Nothing above 1.2 is known at any width below the astronomical sizes of the published program. Between 8 points and there,
  the rate is flat as far as anyone has built.

## 7. Literature, dated  [read 2026-10-10, about 23:55 EDT]

Alman and Rao, arXiv:2211.06459 (v1 11 November 2022, v2 14 June 2023), HTML version, first 100,000 characters read through
a summarising tool, not by eye. Theorem 1.1: H x in at most (23/24) N log N + (13/12) N field operations, namely
(22/24) N log N + N/12 additions and subtractions, (1/24) N log N divisions by 2 and N - 1 multiplications by a power of 2;
their count is "field additions and field scalar multiplications", the same as (O). Their 8-point kernel has 23 operations.
A second algorithm: (7/8) N log N + O(N) additions and subtractions and (1/8) N log N divisions by 2, which "does not use
fewer total operations" than the textbook. They write that they could not improve further with a decomposition of H_16 and
that o(N log N) is open. Two web searches for a later improvement of the constant (2024 to 2026) returned only this paper
and a November 2024 seminar abstract that still calls 23/24 the best known to the speaker. Not found is not the same as
does not exist; I did not search citation indexes.

## 8. What stays open, and mutations

- **Exact minimum in (O) at 8 points: not settled.** For any one factor it lies between 15 and 20; for H x exactly between 15
  and 24. [derived] An (O) program with at most 19 operations needs either a (G) circuit with at most 18 gates (open, g-1) or
  a 19-gate circuit in which every gate is a plain sum or difference of the wires as held; the second kind computes an
  integer multiple of H, and the determinant bound allows only the multiple 1. Inside g-1's one-tree model the minimum in (O)
  is exactly 20 (its minimum in (G) is 19 and the centre always crosses worlds). An exhaustive search over all programs was
  out of reach in the hour.
1. **Additions and subtractions only, 8 points: is 24 the minimum?** e-5 proved the 4-point case (8). If yes, "every saving
   needs a wire held at two scales" is a theorem at 8 points and the textbook is optimal for H x exactly in (O) unless
   multiplications help. A pool of integer vectors with entries 0, 1, -1, 2, -2 and the lines u + v, u - v only; g-1's
   annealer `sa8.py` with that line set is the tool.
2. **Gates saved per world crossing** is the (O) meter: 1 (brick), 2 (interleaved), 5 (window of 3). Design for it: a
   16-point circuit with two worlds and at most two crossings and fewer than 54 gates would beat 56.
3. **Wires with two magnitudes.** Every record uses sign vectors only. The rows of R_8 (entries 3 and -1 up to scale) cost
   the same and are outside every pool searched tonight; in (O) a magnitude ratio of any size costs one operation.
4. Interleave windows of 3 (the wave's `staircase3`) and price the result with the two-worlds rule before believing its (G) count.
5. **Reflections of wires in wires, searched in (O).** State = a set of held wires, each at one scale; move = reflect a wire
   in a wire (cost 1 when the coefficient is 1 at the held scales, 2 otherwise) or add two orthogonal wires. This is e-5's
   class and g-1's class in one, and it is the (O)-native pool nobody has searched. [recalled, not re-read, not run: at 8
   points the sign vectors at scale 1/2 together with the pairs x_i + x_j, x_i - x_j are the shortest vectors of the two E8
   lattices (even and odd number of minus signs; flipping one input passes from one to the other), and a full sign vector at
   scale 1/2 is as long as a pair only when N = 8. So the 4- and 8-point records sit at the two sizes with a reflection
   group behind the pool. I could not turn that into a count: the free flip itself, coefficient exactly 1, holds at every N.]
6. **An "up" unit.** g-1's unit sends scale 1 to scale 1/2. A unit for 2 * H_8 x, alternated with it, would give H x exactly
   with no multiplications at the end; any up unit of at most 27 operations puts H x itself below the textbook at 64 points
   (8 * (20 + 27) = 376 against 384). Not found: all five 19-gate circuits on file price at exactly 28 for output scale 2
   [ran, upper bounds], which is the down unit followed by eight multiplications by 4, one too many. Transposing the
   circuit gives a down unit again [derived: the transpose of H/2 is H/2, and the scales invert]. The determinant bound asks
   an up unit for 20 bits of determinant (an addition makes at most one, a multiplication by 2^t at most t) and the down
   unit for only 4. A clean yes or no at 8 points settles whether H x exactly can have the 5/6 without the extra N.
7. Read the published certificate with the same rule: its additions with coefficient 1/2 are world crossings; count moves
   saved per crossing there. It is the only program known where bits per operation exceed every fixed bound.
