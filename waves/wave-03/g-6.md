## 0. The short version

1. **The equality cases of the block rank bound are now classified, for every size, with a three-line proof** (Theorem 1).
   rank H[S,U] = |S||U|/N happens exactly when S is a union of cosets of some subgroup W and U is a union of cosets of the
   perpendicular subgroup. So non-coset equality cases exist in quantity (unions of cosets), but every one of them is "periodic".
   Checked exhaustively at 8 points (64,516 pairs) and at 16 points (1,966,020 pairs), zero disagreements. [derived + ran]
2. **Two tree steps force every pair of blocks to be almost an equality case** (Lemma A). For every node S of the first tree and
   every node U of the second, a number nu(S,U), which is 0 exactly in the equality case and about min(|S|,|U|) for random sets,
   must be at most 4. This is a condition on pairs of SETS only: scalings and relabellings have disappeared. [derived; the rank
   inequality behind it checked on 10,800 random cases]
3. **In the periodic shape, two steps stop.** If the blocks of the two trees are cosets of W and of its perpendicular, crossing in a
   grid, then every block of each step is forced to be the smaller transform with scaled columns plus a rank-one term, a few
   "exceptional" columns apart, and counting the exceptions gives: **no two-step word from 64 points on**; at 32 points the count
   comes out exactly tight (12 needed, 12 available) in the shape 8 x 4 and is not excluded in the shape 16 x 2. [derived by hand;
   the count "a tree on 8 points needs 3 exceptions" then checked exhaustively by machine; the rest is the weakest part of this report]
4. **32 points is the last size where sets that are not periodic pass the test**: a pair of quadrics has nu = N/8 exactly, that is
   4, 8, 16 at 32, 64, 128 points [ran, exact]; the reason is that the two sign diagonals are Clifford elements whose commutator
   is a controlled-NOT [derived].
5. For **three** steps the same argument gives a dictionary and no bound (section 5). The idea changes shape: the question
   "is L(N) bounded" becomes a question about how nearly two families of projections can commute (section 7).
6. **An anomaly kept** (section 6): at 8 points the float hunt finds one true two-step word, several slow descents towards zero,
   and misses that settle on whole numbers (2.000, 6.000, 8.000) while the terms of the product grow. The slow descents are most
   likely exact words at the edge of my parametrisation, not limits. Separately, and as logic rather than evidence: Lemma A survives
   limits of two-step products, the floor of section 4 does not. [ran, floats, one configuration followed in detail]

## 1. Words

N = 2^k points, H the +-1 transform, H[x][y] = (-1)^(x.y). Q = H/sqrt(N).
A *tree* is any way of splitting the N positions into two parts, each part again into two, down to single points; a *node* is one of
the parts. Not necessarily balanced, not from subgroups. A tree with wider splits is covered: split a wide node in two in any order.
A *tree step* is a matrix A, together with a tree and a matching of rows to columns, such that for every node V the block
A[V, outside V] has rank at most 1 and so has A[outside V, V]. The typical member is A[i][j] = a_i * F(lowest common node of i and j, side) * b_j
off the diagonal, with a free diagonal; members with some zero couplings are limits of these (this matters in section 6).
"Sum up the tree, scale each node, spread down" (d-3) and "letters" (d-2) are the special case
where F does not depend on the side; free diagonal scalings on both sides are included. About 4N gates (G) [derived: N up, 2N across
and down, N at the leaves; not optimised]. In numerical linear algebra this class is called hierarchically semi-separable of rank 1
[recalled].
H = A.B, two tree steps: outputs X, middle wires Z, inputs Y. A node S of the first tree is a set of outputs and, through the
matching, a set S' of middle wires. A node U of the second tree is a set of inputs and a set U' of middle wires.
Stab(S) = the set of shifts d with S + d = S. It is a subgroup. W-perp = all y with y.d = 0 for every d in W.

## 2. Theorem 1: the equality cases of rank H[S,U] >= |S||U|/N

**Theorem 1.** [derived] For non-empty proper S, rank H[S,U] = |S||U|/N if and only if U is a union of cosets of Stab(S)-perp.
In words: there is a subgroup W with S a union of W-cosets and U a union of W-perp-cosets. Then rank = (number of W-cosets in S) times
(number of W-perp-cosets in U), and the rank-one pieces are "a character on a coset".

Proof. Let P = the projection on the coordinates in S and M = Q (projection on U) Q^T. The matrix P M P has eigenvalues in [0,1] and
trace |S||U|/N, and its rank is the rank of H[S,U]. So the rank is at least the trace, with equality exactly when every eigenvalue is
0 or 1, which for two projections means exactly that P and M commute. The entry (x,x') of P M - M P is
([x in S] - [x' in S]) * f(x + x') / N, with f(d) = the sum over y in U of (-1)^(y.d). So they commute exactly when f vanishes on every
d = x + x' with x in S, x' not in S, and those d are exactly the shifts that do not fix S. f vanishing off W = Stab(S) says that U is
periodic under W-perp. End.

[ran, exact integers, `tight.py`] Test "E(E - N) = 0 with E = H[S,U] H[S,U]^T" against the predicate of the theorem:
- 8 points: all 254 x 254 = 64,516 pairs: 308 equality cases, 0 disagreements. 112 of them have S or U not a coset.
- 16 points: one S from each of the 30 classes under affine maps, all 65,534 U: 1,966,020 pairs, 292 equality cases, 0 disagreements.
  Example that is not a coset: S = {0,1,2,3,4,5}, U = the even numbers, rank 3.

So the answer to "is there an equality case that is not a coset" is yes, trivially, and to "is there one that is not periodic" it is
**no, at every size**. d-3's guess ("subgroup-shaped blocks only") is right once "subgroup-shaped" is read as "periodic".
This also explains d-2's only solved random case at 16 points ("two complementary 4-point block systems, each the annihilator of the
other" [read]): that is the pair (W, W-perp).

## 3. Lemma A: two steps force near-equality everywhere

**nu(S,U)** = the number of eigenvalues of P M P strictly between 0 and 1 (the "undecided directions").
[derived] nu = rank H[S,U] + rank H[S,U-complement] - |S| = rank( E (N - E) ), and
rank H[S,U] + rank H[S,U^c] + rank H[S^c,U] + rank H[S^c,U^c] = N + 2 nu. nu = 0 is Theorem 1's case.

**Lemma A.** [derived] If H = A.B with two tree steps, then for every node S of the first tree and every node U of the second,
rank H[S,U] <= |S' meet U'| + 2, and therefore nu(S,U) <= 4. (For steps whose blocks have rank r1 and r2 instead of 1: nu <= 2(r1 + r2).)

Proof. H[S,U] = A[S,S'] B[S',U] + A[S, outside S'] B[outside S', U]; the second term has rank <= 1. Split the rows S' of B[S',U] into
those inside U' and those outside; the outside ones form a block of B[outside U', U], rank <= 1. So the rank is at most |S' meet U'| + 2.
The same for the three other blocks; the four intersections add up to N; the four ranks add up to N + 2 nu. End.

[ran, `lemma_check.py`, modulo 2^31 - 1, random data] 12 random pairs of random tree steps at 16 points, 10,800 pairs of nodes: the
inequality always holds and is attained 1,367 times. This checks the lemma's linear algebra, not anything about H.

**The same thing in Fourier words.** [derived; the identity and the bound checked exactly on six pairs at 32 points,
`fourier_check.py`; on the quadric pair below the bound is attained, 1024 = 1024] With f_U as in the proof of Theorem 1,
sum over d != 0 of f_U(d)^2 * |S minus (S + d)|  <=  N^2 nu / 4  <=  N^2.
For |U| = N/2 the weights f_U(d)^2 add up to N^2/4, so: *the first tree's sets must be almost invariant (boundary 4 on average,
against N/4 for a typical shift) under the shifts that carry the Fourier weight of the second tree's sets.* This is the exact form
of "the requirement sits on the rank bound" (d-3 section 4), and it holds at every level of both trees at once, not only for a
quarter of the rows.

What the condition lets through [ran, `nu.py`]:
- 16 points, 8-point sets (4 affine classes of S, all 12,870 U): nu = 0: 72; nu = 2: 2,442; nu = 4: 11,090; nu >= 5: 37,876.
  At 16 points the test is weak. Every nu <= 4 was recomputed exactly; larger values are floating-point ranks.
- 32 points, 16-point sets: 300 random pairs have nu between 8 and 16 (floats). Hyperplanes a-perp, b-perp: nu = 0 if a.b = 0 and 16 if
  a.b = 1 [exact]. Six descents from random starts: five end at nu = 0 (always "hyperplane against a set with one period"), one at
  nu = 4 with stabilisers of 4 and 2 elements [exact recheck].
- **An anomaly to keep.** S = {x0 x1 + x2 = 0}, U = {y1 y3 + y4 = 0} at 32 points: nu = 4 exactly, neither set periodic in the needed
  way. Reason [derived]: the two sign diagonals are Clifford elements and their group commutator is a controlled-NOT, which fixes 3/4 of
  the space; so the commutator of the projections has rank N/4 and nu = N/8. [ran, exact, `quad.py`] nu = 4, 8, 16 at 32, 64, 128
  points (rank of the block 10, 20, 40 against the bound 8, 16, 32). At 32 points that is on the threshold; at 64 it fails for tree
  steps, but passes for steps with rank-2 blocks.

So for flags from subgroups at 32 points Lemma A reads [derived]: with first-tree splits a, a' and second-tree splits b, b' (top two
levels), one needs a.b = 0, a.b' = 0, a'.b = 0; a'.b' is free. Many of d-2's 270 flag pairs pass; d-2 found none of them solvable in
floats. So Lemma A is necessary and far from sufficient at 32 points; it becomes rigid as N grows.

## 4. The periodic shape: block law, exceptions, and a floor

Hypotheses (name them, they are the to-do list): **(P)** for some subgroup W of w elements the W-cosets are nodes of the first tree and
the W-perp-cosets (c = N/w elements each) are nodes of the second; **(G)** every first-tree block meets every second-tree block in exactly
one middle wire. Write x = (xi, omega), y = (eta, gamma), z = (xi, eta); then H = (-1)^(xi.gamma) (-1)^(omega.eta), and on the block
(xi, eta) the transform is the rank-one matrix chi_eta (in omega) times chi_xi (in gamma).
u_xi = the one column direction of A[block xi, all other middle wires]; q_eta = the one row direction of B[all other middle wires, block eta].

**Block law.** [derived; its first line, which holds for any two tree steps and any two nodes, checked on random data modulo a
prime: 2,352 pairs of nodes, `lemmaB_check.py`] For each (xi, eta), with a = the column of A at the wire (xi,eta) inside block xi and
b = the row of B there:
chi_eta x chi_xi = a x b + (something) x q_eta + u_xi x (something). Removing u_xi on the left and q_eta on the right:
- if chi_eta is not proportional to u_xi and chi_xi is not proportional to q_eta ("good pair"):
  **a = lambda chi_eta + s u_xi with lambda != 0, and b = chi_xi / lambda + r q_eta.**
  So each block of A is the w-point transform with scaled columns plus one rank-one term, and each block of B likewise. This is
  d-2's "two calls" lemma (both blocks are forced to be the half-size transform) for arbitrary trees, with the rank-one term as the
  only new freedom.
- otherwise the pair is exceptional, and then a is proportional to u_xi (kind E1) or b is proportional to q_eta (kind E2).
  Because A and B are invertible: **at most one E1 per xi and at most one E2 per eta**, so at most c + w exceptional pairs in all.

**Nesting kills the rank-one freedom.** [derived] Let R be a node of the first tree inside block xi. All columns of A[R, .] outside R'
are proportional to one vector, and that vector is u_xi restricted to R whenever the block talks to the outside. For a good column this
forces chi_eta restricted to R to be proportional to that same vector. Characters that are proportional on R differ by an element
perpendicular to R - R. Hence: **the number of good columns outside R' is at most w / 2^rho(R)**, rho = the dimension of the smallest
affine subspace containing R (rho >= 1 for 2 points, >= 2 for 3 or 4, >= 3 for 5 to 8, ...). Without exceptions this says H_w itself is
one tree step, so w <= 4; that is the pair word at 16 points and nothing else.

**Counting the exceptions a tree needs.** [derived]
- w = 8: a leaf pair needs 2 exceptional columns outside it, a 3-node needs 3, a 4-node 2 (if it is a plane; 3 if not), a 5-node 2,
  a 6-node 1. Two leaf pairs with the same direction force 4. Every tree on 8 leaves has a 3-node or a 4-node, and a 4-node made of
  two pairs is a plane with two pairs of the same direction. **So every tree on 8 leaves needs at least 3 exceptions**; a caterpillar
  (leaves split off one at a time) gets by with 3.
  [ran, exhaustive, `emin8.py`, 5 s] Over all trees on the 8 rows, all matchings of rows to columns and all sets of at most 2
  exceptional columns (37 sets): none satisfies the rule "good columns outside R' lie in one coset of the subgroup perpendicular to
  R - R" at every node. With 3 it is satisfiable, for example by a caterpillar. So the hand count is right for 8 points.
- w >= 16: one leaf pair alone needs w/2 - 2.
- Budget: (number of blocks) x (exceptions per block) <= c + w.

| shape w x c | needed | available c + w | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 x 4 (16 points) | 0 | 8 | the pair word [read: d-2, i-hatch] |
| 8 x 2 (16 points) | 2 x 3 = 6 | 10 | not excluded by the count; d-2 found none in floats for subgroup flags [read] |
| 8 x 4, 4 x 8 (32 points) | 4 x 3 = 12 | 12 | **exactly tight** |
| 16 x 2, 2 x 16 (32 points) | 2 x (between 6 and 9) | 18 | not excluded; the best tree I found needs 9 per block |
| 8 x 8 (64 points) | 8 x 3 = 24 | 16 | excluded |
| 16 x 4, 4 x 16 (64 points) | 4 x 6 = 24 | 20 | excluded |
| 32 x 2, 2 x 32 (64 points) | node sizes 3 to 9 impossible unless one block takes 20 of the 34 | 34 | excluded |
| any shape, N >= 128 | c (w/2 - 3) > w, or the node-size argument for c = 2 | | excluded |

**Floor.** [derived, under (P) and (G), by hand in this hour] *Two tree steps of the periodic shape, with arbitrary trees inside the blocks,
arbitrary scalings and arbitrary matchings, do not make the transform at 64 points or beyond.* The commuting floor of d-3 gives the same
number (3 steps at 64); the point is that this one does not assume the steps commute, and it says what a counterexample at 32 points
must look like:

**The 8 x 4 hatch at 32 points, fully specified.** Every one of the 4 blocks of the first step has exactly one column proportional to
its outgoing direction (E1) and exactly two columns that are free because, in the second step, the row at that wire is proportional
to the incoming direction of its 4-point block (E2); every one of the 8 blocks of the second step is used for this exactly once; the
tree inside each 8-point block is caterpillar-like, with all leaf pairs in different directions. A count that comes out 12 = 12 is either
the last tie of the night or a brick. I could not decide it.

## 5. What the same argument gives for three steps

[derived, sketch] H = A.B.C. For a node S of the first tree and a node U of the third: H[S,U] = A[S,S'] B[S',U''] C[U'',U] + rank 2,
where S' and U'' are arbitrary sets from the middle step's point of view. So rank H[S,U] <= rank B[S',U''] + 2, and conversely when the
diagonal blocks of A and C are invertible. **The middle step must reproduce the block ranks of the transform, between two trees that
are not its own, to within 2.** A tree step has rank-one blocks only for its own nodes, so this is satisfiable and gives no bound.
It confirms i-play's sketch ("with three factors the same argument gives nothing") and says why: the rows of a two-step product are
not coordinate subspaces any more. Equivalent form: three steps exist exactly when, for some tree step A, the family "span of the rows of
A^-1 in V" (V a node of the second tree) is nearly commuting with the frequency projections of the third tree. Lemma A is the special
case A = identity. So the three-step question is Lemma A with coordinate subspaces replaced by subspaces of the form "one layer of a
many-scale basis".

Reading, with the caution it needs:
- **Start 2 is most likely an exact word that my parametrisation can only approach**, not a limit with growing coefficients: the
  unknowns grow eightfold while the middle terms move by 7 per cent. The class defined by ranks is closed; the chart
  a_i F b_j misses its members with some zero coupling, and the solver creeps towards such a member. (A first version of this
  section called it a "limit word"; the middle-term column was added to test that, and it does not support it.) [guess]
- **The whole-number misses do seem to be approached only with growing terms** (start 1: middle terms double over six legs while the
  residual stays at 6.000). A candidate explanation: the best the class can do is the transform minus a few whole "undecided
  directions" of blocks, and in a block of the transform these all have the same squared singular value. This would also explain
  d-2's unexplained 8.000 at 8 points and 32.00, 48.00 at 16 points [read: d-2 section 6, item 4]. [guess]
- What stays true whatever the reading [derived]: a rank condition on H is closed, so **Lemma A holds for limits of two-step products
  too**; the fine print of section 4 (A and B invertible, lambda != 0, at most one exception per block) is not closed, so **the
  floor of section 4 is for exact words only**. Whether the transform at 32 or 64 points is a limit of two-step products is therefore
  not excluded by anything in this report, and a limit of cheap programs converts to an exact one at a constant factor
  [read: i-cross W4, d-5]. Tonight's runs give no evidence for such a limit; they show that the question is distinct.
- Practical lesson for the next hunt: parametrise tree steps so that zero couplings are reachable (for example by the up-and-down
  sweep itself, with a free 2 x 2 at each node), otherwise true words on the boundary look like slow failures.
- The 16-point runs I managed on the loaded machine (shape 8 x 2: 5 caterpillar configurations with residuals 82 to 97 of 256, and
  7 with random trees, 79 to 88) say nothing either way.

## 7. Mutations ("yes, and")

0. **Two-step products in the limit sense.** The floor of section 4 does not cover them. Hunt at 16 and 32 points, in the tree pairs
   that pass Lemma A (periodic 8 x 4, 16 x 2, and the quadric pair), for residuals that fall while a rescaling-proof size (the middle
   terms) grows like a power; exactly: unknowns that are Laurent polynomials in t, solved modulo a prime. A limit at 32 points would
   be the first object that beats k/2 steps in any sense. No evidence for one tonight.

1. **Decide the 8 x 4 hatch.** Finite and exact: 32 points, the structure of section 4 fixed, unknowns are the scalings. If empty,
   two steps of the periodic shape stop at 16 points, as the commuting floor says. If not empty it is the first word that beats k/2.
2. **Stability of Theorem 1** is the missing half of a real floor: if nu(S,U) <= 4 and both sets are large, must S and U be within a
   bounded number of points of a periodic pair? At 32 points no (the quadric pair). For N >= 64 I do not know one counterexample.
   A proof would remove hypothesis (P); hypothesis (G) fails only on at most c + w cells [derived: an empty cell is an exceptional pair].
   The inequality of section 3 is the tool: it is the "time and band limiting" operator of signal theory with at most 4 eigenvalues in
   its transition region [recalled name: prolate / Slepian].
3. **Wider steps.** Blocks of rank r cost O(rN) to O(r^2 N) gates and relax the test to nu <= 4r. The quadric pair (nu = N/8) says
   trees cut along quadrics, not along subgroups, are the first non-periodic candidates. Define L_r(N); bounded L_r for any fixed r is
   linear size.
4. **Trees on quadrics for three steps.** Section 5 asks for a middle step with transform-like block ranks between two foreign trees.
   The Clifford pairs are exactly sets whose projections commute up to a controlled-NOT; a middle step built on them was never tried.
5. **Use nu as a search filter.** Any future hunt for short words (two or three steps, any size) can discard tree pairs by a rank
   computation on sets before any solver runs.

## 9. Limits, criticism last

- Sections 4 and 5 are hand derivations made inside one hour. The first line of the block law is machine-checked on random data.
  The step from it to "a = lambda chi_eta + s u_xi", the two kinds of exception, and the bound "good columns outside R' <= w/2^rho"
  are by hand: I had no two-step word with a non-zero rank-one term to test them on. The exception
  count for trees on 8 leaves is machine-checked (given the rule); the counts for 16 and 32 leaves were done by cases, by hand, and
  the rows 16 x 4, 32 x 2 and "N >= 128" of the table depend on them.
- The floor is for the periodic grid shape only. Lemma A does not force that shape at 32 points (quadrics pass) and I have no proof
  that it forces it at larger sizes.
- Nothing here lowers a gate count, and the floor it gives (3 steps from 64 points on, in one shape) is weaker than k/2. Its value is
  that it does not assume commuting steps, subgroup trees or unitary scalings, which are exactly what d-3's lemma assumed.
- The quadric law nu = N/8 is checked at three sizes for one pair;
  the sets used at 64 and 128 points are the 32-point sets with extra free bits, so this is one example, not a family.
- The 16-point census used floating-point ranks for nu >= 5.
- I did not run experiment (3) of the brief (three steps at 64 and 128 points). Section 5 says why I expect no cheap obstruction
  there; it is not a substitute for the run. Experiment (2), 32 points as a polynomial system, was not run either: I replaced it by
  the necessary conditions of sections 3 and 4, which say which tree pairs are worth solving for.
- Section 6 is floating point, real numbers only, at 8 points, one configuration followed, six legs. Both readings in it are
  guesses from a residual curve; no exact identity was written down or replayed. I first read the slow descent as a limit word
  and withdrew that after measuring the middle terms. The conversion factor for limits is quoted from other reports, not checked.
- No literature was read tonight. Theorem 1 is the equality case of a standard uncertainty-type bound and is very likely known
  [guess]; I did not look for it.
