Words. N = 2^k points. A *wire* is a linear form the program holds, up to a scalar. s = number of non-zeros when the wire is
written in the inputs, s' = number of non-zeros when it is written in the outputs. *Tight*: s s' = N (the least possible; such
a wire is a character on a coset, that is, plus and minus signs following a parity rule on a sub-block of the addresses).
*Over-spread*: s s' > N. A *sign vector* has entries 1 and -1 only. To *flip* a sign vector v on a set A is to make v - 2(v restricted
to A): one gate, if the wire "v restricted to A" is already held.

## 0. The circuit

```
g1 = x0 + x7     g2 = x1 + x6     g3 = x2 - x5     g4 = x3 - x4          (four pairs)
g5 = g1 - g2     g6 = g3 + g4                                            (two blocks of four)
g7 = g5 - g6                         the centre: signs + - - - + + - + ; not an output
g8 = g7 - 2 x7   g9 = g7 - 2 x5      the centre with one sign flipped
g10 = g8 + 2 x6  g11 = g9 - 2 x4     the centre with two signs flipped
y7 = g10 + 2 g4  y1 = g10 + 2 g3     y6 = g11 + 2 g2   y0 = -g11 + 2 g1   (flip a pair that the tree already holds)
y2 = g8 + 2 x1   y3 = g9 + 2 x3                                          (flip one more input)
y4 = y2 + 2 g6   y5 = -y3 + 2 g5                                         (flip a block of four that the tree already holds)
```

19 gates: 7 to build one sign vector by merging (pairs, fours, all), then 12 flips, each on a single input or on a piece the
merge tree left behind. Every partner is free or already paid for. That is the whole trick.
How it was found [ran]: annealing over sets of helper wires inside a pool of 304 wires (section 4), started from the 22-gate
circuit; all four runs fell to 19 within seconds. I did not expect it; I checked it by hand line by line as well.

## 1. Referee of a-2 (the proved lemma)

Proof written again, independently [derived]. Tight wire: s = 2^d, s' = N/2^d, d its level. In a gate each of the three forms is
a combination of the other two, so its non-zero set lies in the union of the other two, in the inputs and in the outputs alike.
Hence the largest s is at most the sum of the other two, and the same for s'. With levels a <= b <= c: 2^c <= 2^a + 2^b forces
c <= b + 1; N/2^a <= N/2^b + N/2^c forces a >= b - 1 and, if a = b - 1, c = b. So the three levels differ by at most one.
For each d < k the gates whose output is above level d with an input at or below d have outputs spanning every wire above d
(induction along the program), so there are at least N of them; a gate does this for one d only. Total N log2 N. I agree with a-2.

## 2. The ledger has a second side [derived, ran]

a-2: sum over gates of the rise of log2 s (from the smaller input to the output) is at least N log2 N. The same argument read in
the outputs: call a gate t-dropping if its output has log2 s' < t and one input has log2 s' >= t. Inputs have log2 s' = k,
outputs 0, a wire below t is not an input, so by the same induction the outputs of t-dropping gates span everything below t:
at least N for every t in (0, k]. Hence

  **sum over gates of the drop of log2 s' (from the larger input to the output) >= N log2 N.**

Both sums must be paid. Measured (`ledger.out`, `ledger19.out`):

| circuit | gates | N log2 N | sum of rises | sum of drops | gates crossing each whole threshold of log2 s' |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| textbook, 4 to 64 points | N log2 N | | = N log2 N | = N log2 N | N each |
| 7 gates, 4 points (chain) | 7 | 8 | 12.58 | 9 | 4, 5 |
| 22 gates, 8 points | 22 | 24 | 33.17 | 26 | 8, 8, 10 |
| 56 gates, 16 points | 56 | 64 | 100.68 | 72 | 16, 20, 16, 20 |
| 336 gates, 64 points | 336 | 384 | 604.08 | 432 | 64, 80, ... |
| **19 gates, 8 points** | 19 | 24 | 35.00 | **24.00** | **8, 8, 8** |

Anomaly to keep: the 19-gate circuit meets the output-side ledger with equality, every threshold crossed by exactly N gates.
The bricks waste one eighth there. The input-side ledger is the loose one in every winner.
And the input-side ledger alone sees almost nothing [derived]: a bare chain of N - 1 additions (one output) already has rises
log2(N!) = N log2 N - 1.44 N, which is 93% of the whole ledger at a million points. Cheap over-spread wires exist in bulk; what
is expensive is to make the s' side fall at the same time.

|---|---|
| 7 at 4 points | (4,4) x 1, read by 4; chain version also (3,4) x 1, read by 1 |
| 22 at 8 points | (4,8) x 2, read by 4 each; (3,8) x 2 |
| 56 at 16 points | (4,16) x 4 and (16,4) x 4, read by 4 each; (3,16) x 4, (12,4) x 4 |
| 336 at 64 points | 16 each of (4,64), (16,16), (64,4), read by 4 each; plus the chain's partial sums |
| 19 at 8 points | (8,4) x 3 and (8,8) x 2, **each read by 2 gates only** |

Gate types that carry the rise. Bricks: "input + hub -> a tight wire", rise 2 and drop 2, four per hub. The 19-gate circuit:
"input + full wire -> full wire" six times, rise 3 each (the largest possible at 8 points), two of them landing on an output
with drop 3; four "pair + hub -> output" (rise 2, drop 2); two "block of four + output -> output" (rise 1, drop 1).
So the saving does not come from one wire serving many gates. It comes from a **walk**: full-support wire to full-support wire,
one cheap flip at a time.

## 4. Using it

**The pool.** Coset sign vectors: plus and minus signs on a coset of a subgroup of the addresses, zero elsewhere. Written (C, g),
g a 0/1 function on C. g affine = tight. g quadratic = "a tight wire times a quadratic sign vector on a sub-cube", the brief's
pool, s s' = 4N at 8 points; these are the 240 real stabiliser states. g cubic at 8 points: a character with one sign flipped,
s = s' = 8. Census at 8 points [ran, `pool_k3.out`]: 304 wires, 6224 collinear triples (each is a possible gate three ways).
The pool contains gates the bricks never use: "input + cubic -> quadratic", "input + quadratic -> cubic", rise 3.

**A hub cannot serve many tight wires cheaply** [derived; ran on every vector with entries 0, 1, -1 and every vector with entries
1, i, -1, -i at 4 and 8 points, `flat.out`]. If one wire v is one gate from two outputs, with partners of supports a and a', then
a + a' >= N/2 (v agrees with two different characters off the partners' supports, and two characters agree on at most half the
points). The same for any two tight wires of one support size s: a + a' >= s/2. Consequences: a rise above 2 can land on at
most one tight wire per hub; rise exactly 2 on at most four, and then the hub is the 4-point brick's hub. Census [ran]: the 224
forms at 8 points with entries 1, i, -1, -i that are flat on both sides need partners of support 5 for every output; the 448
bent sign vectors at 16 points need 6. A wire flat on both sides is a poor hub for outputs. This is j-failproof's Parseval
remark made exact. It answers the brief's question one way: **toward tight wires, one over-spread wire serves a bounded number
of gates.** Toward other over-spread wires there is no such limit, and that is where the 19 gates live.

**The search** [ran, `sa8.py`, `sa8_known*.out`]. State = a set of helper wires from the pool; a state is a circuit when every
helper and every output lies on a line through two wires derived before it (exact collinear triples, so nothing to lift).
From the 22-gate circuit, four runs, 5.4 million moves in all: 22 -> 21 -> 20 -> 19 within seconds in every run, then no 18.
Around the 19-gate circuit each run ended on, exhaustively: remove any one helper, or remove any two and add any one of the
288 others: 15356 states each time, no circuit with 18 gates. From an empty start the same annealer found no circuit at all (my energy does not guide
from nothing); the result needed the known circuit as a seed.

**What the circuit is: one merge tree and a flip walk** [derived from the circuit; ran, `flipwalk.py`]. Fix a sign vector F0 and
a chain of subgroups. Build F0 by merging pairs, fours, ...: N - 1 gates, and every node A of the tree leaves the wire
"F0 on A". Then walk among full sign vectors: a step flips a single input (always free) or a tree node on which the current
vector equals F0 up to sign (the tree's wire is the partner). Outputs = the N characters. In the coordinates
D = {places where the current vector differs from F0}: a step toggles one point, or a tree node lying wholly inside or wholly
outside D. So

  **gates = 2N - 2 + (number of full-support wires that are not outputs),**

and the minimum is a shortest-tree problem in a graph on subsets. 4 points: 7 for every design (268 tried).
8 points, **exact** [ran, `exact8.py`, a dynamic programme for the smallest tree, all sign vectors and all chains of subgroups
by symmetry]: **the minimum of this model is exactly 19.** Of the 16 classes of centres (pairs along bit 0, blocks of four
along bits 0 and 1), two reach 19: the quadratic forms x0 x2 and x0 x2 + x1 x2, which tie the pair direction to the bit outside
the block of four. Twelve give exactly 21, among them all eight with an odd number of minus signs and the 4-point brick's
kind of centre x0 x1. Two give 22 (a character; the form x1 x2). So 18 at 8 points, if it exists, is not one tree and a walk.
16 points [ran, upper bounds from a shortest-path tree heuristic only; `fw4_quad.out`, `fw4_any.out`, `fwq16.out`]: 805 random
designs and all 64 quadratic centres on one chain: best 63, typical 66 to 75. No better than the textbook's 64 and far from
the tensor product's 54. As far as this search sees, one tree and a walk is an 8-point object.

**Growth, count (G):**

wires, two of them of half size. The known lower bound 2N - 1 is 15 at 8 points; 16, 17, 18 are open.

## 5. Two walls I can name, and the way round each

**Wall A: every pool in the brief has bounded coefficients** [derived]. If all wires, scaled, have entries in a fixed finite set
(0, 1, -1; or 0 and the fourth roots of unity), then every gate between scaled wires has |a| + |b| <= C (at most 3 for 0, 1, -1:
the flip u - 2v). Morgenstern's determinant argument then gives at least (N log2 N) / (2 log2 C) gates: 0.315 N log2 N for sign
pools. So quadratic sign vectors, stabiliser states and flat forms with entries 1, i, -1, -i can only move the constant, which
now lies between 0.315 and 0.792. Over-spread is necessary (a-2) and far from sufficient.

**Wall B: product wires tie exactly** [derived]. Suppose every wire is a product vector: a tensor product of k two-point
vectors, with any entries at all. Three such wires on one line agree in all but one factor (a sum of two rank-one tensors that
differ in two factors has rank two). Let the level be the number of factors that are not a basis vector: inputs 0, outputs k,
and a gate changes it by at most one. a-2's count gives N log2 N, and the textbook is of this kind. Such wires can have
s = s' = N and coefficients of any size, so they pass both known tests for a linear program (over-spread; unbounded) and still
tie to the last gate. Third necessary condition: **the bulk of a linear program is made of wires of tensor rank two or more.**
The 4-point hub is the smallest one: (output) - 2 (input).

**Round A, tried:** leave sign vectors but keep the walk. The walk's steps "full wire + c * input -> full wire" exist for any
entries; the obstacle is only that two characters are N/2 apart, so a walk that visits all N outputs by single inputs has
length of order N^2. The 19 gates work because the tree's own pieces shorten the walk. What is needed is a family of big
corrections that are themselves produced by a walk, one level down. I did not build it.
**Round B, tried:** the two ledgers together. A gate with large rise and large drop is "small-in-the-inputs + full -> small-in-the-outputs".
The hub theorem says only a bounded number of such gates per full wire can end on a tight wire. So in a linear program
almost all rise must be collected by full-to-full gates and delivered to the N outputs at the very end with rise at most 2,
or through hubs that each serve one output. The 19-gate circuit has exactly that shape: 6 full-to-full or private steps of
rise 3, then deliveries of rise 2 and 1.

## 6. Reading

- Which wires carry the rise: in the bricks, the hub (s s' = 4N), read by four gates. In the 19-gate circuit, five full-support
  wires (s s' = 4N and 8N), each read by only two gates; the rise is carried by the steps between them.
- Can one over-spread wire serve more than a bounded number of gates: toward tight wires, no (hub theorem). It does not need to.
  The cheap object is a chain of over-spread wires, each one flip from the next.
- Growth law of the best count at 4, 8, 16: 7, 19, 54; ratios to N log2 N 0.875, 0.792, 0.844. The 8-point gain does not
  compound beyond tensoring. Nothing here grows slower than N log N.

## 8. Criticism, last

- The 19-gate circuit is exact and replayed three ways; that part is solid. "Best known" means best in tonight's notes
  (22, then 21). I did not search the literature for the least number of additions for the 8-point transform with free
  scalings; it may be known.
- "No 18" is exact only inside the one-tree walk model (trees made of cosets of a chain of subgroups). In the pool of 304
  sign-vector wires it is a local statement: one exchange radius, a few million annealing moves. Not a lower bound.
- The 16-point flip-walk numbers come from a shortest-path tree heuristic over 869 designs; they are upper bounds of that
  model only. At 8 points the same heuristic reached the exact minimum on 6% of random designs, so 63 may be far from that
  model's true minimum.
- Walls A and B and the hub theorem are hand derivations of tonight. The hub theorem was machine-checked at 4 and 8 points on
  two alphabets; wall B was not machine-checked.
- In count (O) the gain appears only from 512 points (4352 against 4608) and only as a constant; at 64 points it ties (384).
- None of this changes the order. A saving of 1e-1 or linear size needs wires that are over-spread, not from a finite
  alphabet, and of tensor rank at least two, in bulk.
