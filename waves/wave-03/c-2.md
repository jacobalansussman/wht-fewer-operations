## 1. The claim, stated precisely

N = 2^k points, H[x][y] = (-1)^(x.y). For sets S (outputs) and U (inputs), H[S,U] is the block of H on those rows and columns.

**Theorem 1.** rank H[S,U] >= |S||U|/N always. Equality holds exactly when U is a union of cosets of the perpendicular of
Stab(S), where Stab(S) is the subgroup of shifts d with S + d = S. (A coset of a subgroup W is a shifted copy x + W.)

**Tree step.** A matrix A with a binary tree on the positions and a matching of rows to columns, such that for every node V of the
tree the blocks A[V, outside V'] and A[outside V, V'] have rank at most 1 (V' = the columns matched to V). g-6 costs one at about
4N gates (G) [read, not checked by me].

**Lemma A.** If H = A.B with two tree steps, then for every node S of the first tree and node U of the second,
rank H[S,U] <= |S' meet U'| + 2, hence nu(S,U) <= 4, where nu = rank H[S,U] + rank H[S, complement of U] - |S|.

**Floor.** Assume (P): for a subgroup W with w elements, the W-cosets are nodes of the first tree and the cosets of W-perp
(c = N/w elements each) are nodes of the second; and (G): each first-tree block shares exactly one middle wire with each
second-tree block. Then for N >= 64 no such A, B exist. At 32 points g-6 left 8 x 4 "tight" and 16 x 2 open.
The engine is a count: each block needs a minimum number of "exceptional" columns, and at most c + w exist in all.

## 3. Pushed one size further

- Theorem 1 at 32 points: 2,511 structured and random pairs, 449 equality cases, 0 disagreements. At 64 points: 1,029 pairs, 162

- The exception count for larger blocks, with a proof instead of cases. [derived] Take the smallest node with at least 3 leaves;
  its children have at most 2, so it has 3 or 4. The rule "good columns outside the node lie in one coset of size w/2^dimension"
  then forces: 3 leaves: 3w/4 - 3; 4 leaves in a plane: 3w/4 - 4; 4 leaves not in a plane: 7w/8 - 4. So **e(w) >= 3w/4 - 4**.
  [ran, `c2_e16.py`: 2, 8, 20, 44 at w = 8, 16, 32, 64]. At w = 8 the true value is 3 (table above). At w = 16 the true value is
  **exactly 8**: an explicit tree with 8 exceptions passes the rule at every node [ran]. g-6 had "between 6 and 9".
- The budget table for every shape with 2 <= w, c and N = 16 to 4096 [ran, `e16.out`]: N = 16 all open; N = 32: 8 x 4 and 4 x 8

  (N/2) x 2: 3N/4 - 8 needed against N/2 + 2 available, so the smallest margin is N/4 - 10 (6 at 64 points, 22 at 128).
  This is g-6's table, now without hand cases.

## 4. New: the tight case at 32 points is impossible

g-6's rule treats all exceptional columns alike. They are of two kinds: (first kind) the block's outgoing direction u is itself
proportional to a character, chi_eta1; at most one per block of the first step, c in all. (second kind) the incoming direction of
a block of the second step is proportional to a character; at most one per such block, **w in all**.

Refinement [derived]: in a block that has a first-kind exception, the common direction at every node R is u restricted to R, which
is chi_eta1 restricted to R and has no zero entry. So the good columns outside R lie in the coset *through eta1*, and eta1 itself
is not good: one fewer than g-6 allowed, at every node.
[ran, exhaustive over all trees, matchings and exceptional sets, `c2_refined8.py`] With this rule a block of 8 that has a first-kind
exception needs **4** exceptional columns in all (none with 1, 2, 3), so at least 3 of the second kind. A block without one needs 3,
all of the second kind. Either way: **each block of 8 needs 3 exceptions of the second kind.**

- Shape 8 x 4 at 32 points: 4 blocks x 3 = 12 needed, 8 exist. **Excluded.** Shape 4 x 8: the same with the two steps swapped.
- Shape 16 x 2: each block needs at least 8 of the second kind (8 without a first-kind exception; 9 in all, so 8, with one
  [derived by hand from the one-node bound; only the 8-point count was machine-checked]).
  2 x 8 = 16 needed, 16 exist. **Exactly tight.** Not decided.
- Shape 8 x 2 at 16 points: 6 needed, 8 exist. Open, as before.

So, under (P) and (G) with 2 <= w <= N/2: two periodic tree steps exist at 16 points (shape 4 x 4, an eight-parameter family at
least), are impossible at 64 points and beyond, and at 32 points can only have the shape 16 x 2 with every one of the 16
second-step blocks spent. This answers experiment 3 of k-cross ("hatch32") for the shape 8 x 4 without a solver.
Evidence level: the refinement is twenty minutes old and checked by nobody else; the machine checked the count, not the rule.

## 5. Is it known?

- The bound rank >= |S||U|/N for a block of a Hadamard matrix is classical (it follows from "sum of squared entries / largest
  squared singular value"); it is the standard tool in the rigidity literature. [recalled: Lokam's survey "Complexity lower bounds
  using linear algebra", 2009; Kashin and Razborov 1998; not opened tonight]
- The equality case. The classical neighbour is the Donoho-Stark inequality |support of f| x |support of its transform| >= N
  (Donoho and Stark 1989), with equality exactly for a character times the indicator of a coset of a subgroup. [read: search
  summary of the survey arXiv:2006.11206 and of Matusiak, Ozaydin, Przebinda, "The Donoho-Stark uncertainty principle for a finite
  abelian group", 2004; the summary attributes the inequality to Matolcsi and Szucs and the classification to K. T. Smith]
  Theorem 1 is the same phenomenon for pairs of sets instead of functions (the two cut-off projections commute). I found no paper
  that states it in two searches. I would call it folklore until someone finds it; it is not a publishable novelty on its own. [guess]
- The floor. Nearest known theory [read: abstract of Zheng, Riccietti, Gribonval, "Efficient identification of butterfly sparse
  matrix factorizations", arXiv:2110.01230, and its companion arXiv:2110.01235]: a matrix with the butterfly structure has an
  essentially unique factorisation into log2 N butterfly factors, unique up to scalings, and the Hadamard matrix is the named
  example. That is g-6's "every block is forced to be the smaller transform" for factors that are zero outside fixed positions.
  g-6 allows dense factors (rank one outside the blocks) and finds the one extra freedom, a rank-one term, and that nesting kills it.
  I found no statement of that. Tree steps are "hierarchically semi-separable matrices of rank 1" in numerical linear algebra
  [recalled: Chandrasekaran, Gu, Pals 2006]; I know of no theorem on products of two or three of them. [recalled]
- **What the known theory predicts for "linear or not": nothing.** Inside the fixed-position butterfly class the answer is "log N
  factors, no choice". For general circuits with free scalings no bound above linear is known for any explicit matrix
  [recalled; see `wht33/research-2026-10-09/lenses/readers/lit-lower-bounds.md`]. g-6 sits strictly between and settles one case.

## 6. What it gives in the user's terms

**Nothing yet: no circuit, no formula, no exponent.** Precisely:

- The family: H as a product of L tree steps costs about 4 L N gates (G) [read: g-6's count]. Bounded L would be linear size.
- Known upper side: L = k/2 (one step per pair of bits), about 2 N log2 N gates, twice the textbook count. So the family beats the
  textbook only if L < (log2 N)/4.
- What g-6 plus this report prove: L >= 3 from 64 points on (and at 32 points outside the shape 16 x 2), **only** for the periodic
  grid shape. Growth law observed: none for L; the margin by which two steps fail grows like N/4.
- Why it cannot become a growth law as it stands [derived]:
  1. Three steps: the argument gives a dictionary and no bound (g-6 section 5; I agree with the reasoning).
  2. Hypothesis (P) is not forced. Lemma A is the only unconditional statement, and at 32 points non-periodic pairs pass it
     (g-6's quadric pair; its nu = N/8 at larger sizes is one example with extra free bits, trivially doubling, as g-6 says).
  3. The rank tool has a ceiling. For k-cross section 1 B: "in a sum of tree rectangles, rank H[A,B] <= 2 + (rectangles strictly
     inside A x B)" is correct [derived: rectangles containing the block's rows add up to one rank-one term, those containing its
     columns to another; I did not re-run k-cross's 49 pairs]. But against trees made from a chain of subgroups and the chain of
     their perpendiculars every block has rank |A||B|/N or 1, so over any set of disjoint blocks the lemma adds up to at most
     (number of rectangles) >= N - 2: a linear bound, never more. It is a filter for searches, not a floor. Lemma A alone is the
     same: nu = 0 on such trees, so it excludes none of them; the floor needed the block law. That is the honest reading of the "+2".

  two-step products, which the floor does not cover (g-6 section 6 says so correctly).
