Words. K3 = [[1,1,1],[2,0,-2],[1,-1,1]]. Its r-fold Kronecker power acts on 3^r numbers; I call it "the K3 power". The *textbook*
for it is 4 gates per copy of K3: 4r 3^(r-1) gates (4, 24, 108, 432). *Inherited* means: obtained from circuits for the Walsh-Hadamard
transform by the reduction of section 2. A *brick* is a small circuit that beats the textbook and is used as a building block.
A *bit* is one unit of log2 |determinant| (K3 with its middle row scaled to (1,0,-1) has 2 bits, a butterfly has 1).

## 1. Six descriptions of K3, each checked exactly [ran, `play.py`, `play.out`]

- K3 is the 4-point transform on data that does not change when the two address bits are swapped: in the coordinates
  (x00, x01 + x10, x11, x01 - x10) the 4-point transform is K3 on the first three and the number -2 on the last. [d-7; re-checked]
- K3^2 = 4 I, and **K3 = 2 I + u v^T** with u = (-1, 2, 1), v = (1, -1, -1): K3/2 is a reflection, with rational eigenvectors.
  (The 2-point transform has eigenvalues +-sqrt 2 and no rational eigenvectors.) v K3 = -2 v and (1, 1, -1) K3 = 2 (1, 1, -1): two flat
  wires that are the same number before and after.
- **The 7-gate brick is this reflection, with the split and the merge absorbed.** In the raw coordinates the same fact reads
  "4-point transform = 2 x (swap of the two bits) + a matrix of rank one", and the brick's helper x00 - x01 - x10 - x11 is the form v.
  On K3 alone the reflection form costs 5 gates against 4; it pays only because it swallows the two butterflies of the split and merge.
- **The Fourier transform of the cyclic group of order 4, in the coordinates (f0, f1 + f3, f2, f1 - f3), is K3 on the first three and
  the number 2i on the last.** So the 4-point Walsh-Hadamard transform and the 4-point Fourier transform are the same matrix except
  for one scalar (-2 against 2i), and the K3 power is the common core of both: the r-dimensional Fourier transform over (Z/4)^r
  restricted to functions that are even in every coordinate.
- K3 transposed is the 3-point cosine transform of type I (entries cos(pi j k / 2), end weights 1, 2, 1). [ran] It is also the matrix
  that coding theory uses for the dual of a code over Z/4 counted by Lee weights [recalled, not looked up tonight].
- The antisymmetric square of K3 is -2 K3^T. The symmetric square is section 6.

## 2. The reduction K3 <- transform, and why the recursion is circular

[derived] K3 (a, b, c) = (s + b, 2 d, s - b) with s = a + c, d = a - c: one butterfly on the letters (0, 2), then one butterfly on the
slots (s, b). Take r copies. The first butterflies of all positions commute; together they are: for every set M of positions that hold
letter 1, the Walsh-Hadamard transform on the 2^(r-|M|) points with letters 0, 2 elsewhere. Call this layer A. The second butterflies
give a layer B of the same kind (blocks: fixed set of positions holding the difference slot). So

    K3 power = B . A,     cost(3^r) <= 2 * sum over j of C(r, j) T(2^(r-j)),      T = cost of the transform.

A block of A and a block of B share at most one point. Typical block: 2^(2r/3) points.

[ran, `k3.py`, `k3.out`] At textbook prices the right side is 4r 3^(r-1) exactly (r = 1..8). Circuits built and replayed in
fractions, every output proportional to its row:

| numbers | 3 | 9 | 27 | 81 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| plain butterflies | 4 | 24 | 108 | 432 |
| 7-gate bricks inside the blocks | 4 | **22** | **98** | **388** |
| formula with e2-0's prices 7, 20, 52 [read; `prices.py`] | 4 | 22 | 94 | 364 |
| bits (Morgenstern-type bound for coefficients of size 1) | 2 | 12 | 54 | 216 |

The 22-gate circuit in words: the 4 corners of the 3 x 3 array through the 7-gate brick; sums and differences of the two edge pairs
(4 gates); four outputs by one gate each; the four "full" outputs are the 7-gate brick again on (corner sum, edge sum, edge sum, centre).

Another way to say the same thing [ran, `play.py` item 10, r = 1..4]: store the 3^r numbers as a 2^r x 2^r array X supported on pairs
of **disjoint** sets. Transform every column, keep the disjoint pairs, transform every row, keep the disjoint pairs: that is the K3
power. The K3 power is the Walsh-Hadamard transform of the staircase.

**Both reductions together.** d-7: per entry t(k) <= 2 t(about k/4) + tau(about 3k/8), tau the per-entry cost of the K3 power.
Mine: tau(r) <= 2 t(about 2r/3). Insert: t(k) <= 4 t(k/4). Free solution: exponent 1. [derived; exact identity k 2^k at k = 2..32, ran]
So:
- the K3 power is never harder than the transform (same exponent or better; linear size for the transform gives linear size for K3);
- the transform is never harder than the K3 power, down to exponent 1/2 [d-7's lemma];
- hence above 1/2 the two exponents are equal. "Does K3 have a saving exponent?" is the original question in other coordinates.
  The only room K3 has of its own is below 1/2: K3 power linear while the transform sits at N sqrt(log N).
- a-1 said the pair route "is the textbook circuit". For K3 that is now literal: K3 is two of the textbook's four quarter-layers.

## 4. The 12-gate brick for K3 (x) H2 [ran, `brick12.py`, `brick12.out`]

Six inputs a0, a1, b0, b1, c0, c1 (letter of K3, then one bit). Outputs: (row i of K3) (x) (1, 1) and (x) (1, -1).

    g  = c0 + c1 ;  g' = g + a1 ;  h = a0 - g'                     (h = a0 - a1 - c0 - c1 : the 7-gate brick's helper on rows a, c)
    row (1,0,-1)(x)(1, 1) = h + 2 a1          row (1,0,-1)(x)(1,-1) = h + 2 c1
    p  = b0 + b1 ;  h1 = h + p                                      (h1 = + - + + - - : all six inputs)
    row (1, 1,1)(x)(1, 1) = h1 + 2 g'         row (1,-1,1)(x)(1, 1) = -(h1 - 2 a0)
    h2 = h1 + 2 c0                                                  (h2 = + - + + + - : all six inputs)
    row (1, 1,1)(x)(1,-1) = h2 - 2 b1         row (1,-1,1)(x)(1,-1) = h2 - 2 b0

Twelve gates. The inherited circuit is: 7-gate brick on rows a, c (7), butterfly on b0, b1 (2), four gates for sum and difference
with b (4) = 13. The saving: the sum and the difference of row b are never formed; one flat helper h1 carries "+(b0 + b1)", and
both signs of b0 - b1 are reached from it by subtracting twice a single input. A walk through flat sign patterns, one gate per step,
two outputs per helper.

[ran] Written in the outputs, h, h1, h2 have 6, 6 and 4 non-zero coefficients: spread on both sides, like every wire that has ever
won tonight (i-cross W3). The GF(3) search first returned a 12-gate circuit that does NOT lift (it uses 2 = -1); the GF(5) one lifts.

What it gives elsewhere, replayed exactly:
- **8-point transform, 22 gates**: split one pair (4) + this brick (12) + one butterfly on the antisymmetric line (2) + merge (4).
  A third shape of 22. Since 12 is the exact minimum (in the three fields), **no circuit that goes through the pair coordinates stage
  by stage (split, middle, merge, each finished before the next) has fewer than 22**, while e2-0 has 20: at 8 points the
  symmetric/antisymmetric split costs at least 2 gates. [ran for the 12; read for the 20]
- **K3 cubed on 27 numbers, 96 gates** (two layers of the brick), against 98 with 7-gate bricks. With e2-0's 20-gate circuit the
  inherited formula gives 94 [not replayed by me], so 96 is not the best known either.

## 5. Pushed once: the growth law [ran, `pairroute.py`, `pairroute.out`; formula in `brick12.out` part 4 and `prices.out`]

The transform on 8^t points, built gate by gate: split t pairs (a transform on every swap orbit, 7-gate bricks), the 12-gate brick
along every factor whose pair is symmetric, transforms on the antisymmetric lines, merge. All outputs checked against the rows.

| points | 8 | 64 | 512 |
|---|---|---|---|
| this route, replayed | 22 | 343 | 4,076 |
| product of 7-gate bricks | 22 | 336 | 4,096 |
| e2-0's family (3/4) N log2 N + N/4 [read] | 20 | 304 | 3,584 |
| textbook | 24 | 384 | 4,608 |

Largest coefficient in any gate: 2. By formula the route is below the product of 7-gate bricks at 2^9 points and, among the sizes
I computed (8^t, t = 6, 8, 12, ..., 256), from 2^18 points on, and it tends to 83/96 = 0.8646 of the textbook; using the route for its own split, merge and antisymmetric transforms gives **6/7** [derived, not
replayed]. e2-0's 3/4 is better at every size. **Order unchanged; saving exponent 0; nothing for linear size.**

The rule behind these constants [derived]: in any route of this kind the limit constant is (gates per bit of the best brick) / 2.
Butterfly 2.000; 7-gate brick 1.750; this brick 12/7 = 1.714; e2-0 1.667, 1.625, 1.583 at 8, 16, 64 points, limit 1.5.
With coefficients of size at most 2, Morgenstern's bound allows at best 0.5 [recalled: gates >= bits / log2(2c) for coefficients of size c]. A change of order is "gates per bit tends to 0", and that
needs coefficients that grow with N [read: Morgenstern; i-cross W4].

## 6. The symmetric square: a reorganisation that loses [ran, `s6_min.out`, `s6_more.out`, `s6_lift.py`]

K3 (x) K3 commutes with swapping its two factors. Antisymmetric part: -2 K3^T, 4 gates. Symmetric part S6 (6 x 6): exactly 11 gates
(no 10 in three fields; an 11-gate rational circuit replayed). Route: split (6) + 11 + 4 + merge (6) = **27** against 24. A tie
would need S6 = 8.
[derived] Why it loses where the pair swap ties: the swaps of the pairs commute with one another, so splitting by them is a
partial transform (a-1's "textbook in other coordinates"). Swapping the two pairs as well gives a group that does not commute (8
elements), and then the pieces are no longer transforms: S6 is the 5 x 5 Krawtchouk block plus a number. The same thing d-7 saw for
the full symmetric group (twice the textbook) starts here, at 9 numbers, with a loss of 3 gates. Triples in place of pairs lose too:
(20 + c)/24 of the textbook, c the cost of the 4 x 4 Krawtchouk block, which is between 6 and 8, so between 13/12 and 7/6
[derived by hand: split 8, merge 8, two butterflies 4, per 8 points; c not minimised; not run].

## 7. The brief's questions

- **What is conserved in the ties?** Every route that is a product of plain butterflies pays 2 gates per bit, and K3 is two butterflies.
  The pair route's tie needs no more explanation than that plus section 2. What is NOT conserved: gates per bit once a wire is flat
  on many inputs (1.75, 1.714, ..., 1.5).
- **Is "the rest" cheaper than the 2^r-point transform inside the K3 power?** No such split: that block is one block of layer A and
  carries a share (3/2)(2/3)^r of the cost. The bulk is blocks of 2^(2r/3) points. [derived]
- **Does K3 need growing coefficients to gain?** For a constant, no: the 12-gate brick uses 1 and 2. For the order, yes (section 5).
- **Odd radix.** K3 is not flat (it has a zero), so the "tight wire" inequality does not hold for it: the wire a - c has 2 non-zeros in
  the inputs and 1 in the outputs, product 2 < 3. The K3 power has 2^r wires of that kind for free. I found no use for them. [derived]
- **Two copies** of K3 on disjoint data cost exactly 8: the direct-sum wall (i-cross W2) holds at the smallest size.

## 8. The question it turns into

Layer A and layer B are each a direct sum of transforms on disjoint blocks, and every block of A meets every block of B in at most
one point. Section 4 says: at 6 numbers the pair (B after A) costs 12 while the best circuits for A and for B cost 9 + 4 = 13
(9 is the exact minimum for A in three fields [ran, `layerA_min.out`]; B is four outputs of one gate each). So **two crossing layers of block transforms are cheaper together than apart, at the first size where it could
show.** The transform itself is four such layers (section 2). The growth law of that gap is the question:

    gap(r) = [best cost of layer A alone + best cost of layer B alone] - [best cost of B after A],   on 3^r numbers.

gap = 0 at 3 numbers (4 = 2 + 2), 1 at 6 numbers (K3 (x) H2), between 0 and 7 at 9 numbers (11 + 11 against 15..22). If the gap stays a fixed share of the cost, constants; if the share
tends to 1, an order. Experiment: close the 15..22 at 9 numbers (one layer alone is exactly 11 [ran, `layerA9_min.out`]; mincirc at 15 gates over GF(3)
needs more than 590 s, perhaps an hour on the rented machine), then K3 (x) H4 on 12 numbers by a better heuristic (36 known).

Three smaller leads.
- **(Z/4)^r.** [derived from section 1, not checked against OpenAI's statement files] Fourier transform over (Z/4)^r = (4^r-point
  Walsh-Hadamard transform) after an operator that is the identity on the symmetric part of every pair and -i on the antisymmetric
  part. That operator is "split, multiply by powers of -i, merge", and split and merge are transforms on blocks of at most 2^r points.
  So the published exponent passes to the Fourier transform over (Z/4)^r, a group that is not cyclic. Worth one look at whether the
  published statement already covers products of cyclic groups; if not it is a corollary for the release notes, not a new order.
- **Stage-crossing wires.** e2-0's 20-gate circuit at 8 points must contain a wire that is neither symmetric nor antisymmetric under
  the swap of a pair yet is used on both sides of it (section 4). Reading that circuit in pair coordinates would show which.
- **Commuting families only.** Section 6 says a symmetry split ties when the symmetries commute and loses when they do not. The
  free exponent of d-7's recursion is 1/2 because pairs give two copies at a quarter of the bits. Whether some other commuting
  family of address maps gives two copies at less than a quarter (free exponent below 1/2) is a finite classification nobody ran.

## 9. Criticism, last

- Nothing here changes the order. Item 2 of the short version says the idea cannot, above exponent 1/2, unless the transform does.
- The 12-gate brick is a constant, and a weaker one than e2-0's family, which I read only tonight at 23:09 and did not re-verify.
  My brief's "22 at 8 points" was already out of date (21 in e-2 and e2-3, 20 in e2-0); the 512-point count 4,076 beats the product
  of 7-gate bricks and nothing else.
- Lower bounds are modulo 3, 5, 7 only. The 9-number bound (15) is modulo 3 only, and 3 is a suspicious prime for a 3-point kernel
  (K3 is congruent to -I + rank one there; one GF(3) circuit did not lift).
- The limit constants 83/96 and 6/7 are formulas; replays stop at 512 points.
- Literature: one search tonight. Alman, Guan and Padaki, "Smaller low-depth circuits for Kronecker powers" (arXiv 2211.05217) and
  Alman and Li, "Kronecker powers, orthogonal vectors, and the asymptotic spectrum" (arXiv 2509.14489, September 2025) treat Kronecker
  powers of any fixed matrix and the disjointness matrix at depth 2; I read search summaries only, and found no statement about
  the 3 x 3 Krawtchouk kernel. The Z/4 and cosine descriptions are standard facts, checked here by computation, not by source.
- The heuristic searches (section 3) found nothing and cannot be read as evidence: the same tool, seeded two gates above a known
  circuit, did not find it.
