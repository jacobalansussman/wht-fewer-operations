# g-4 zetalead: the published exponent carries to subset sums, and what that says back about the transform

> Editor's note (11 October 2026): the transfer of the published exponent to subset sums is this agent's argument. It is not in Lean and nobody has reviewed it, so "provably" is too strong, and I claim nothing about the question of Boyar and Find or about Aaronson's suggestion, which this report knows through a summary. The page `note-for-zeta-readers.md` that it names is a draft I have not published; it is not in this repository ([LEFT-OUT.md](LEFT-OUT.md)).

2026-10-10, 60-minute box. Private research: nothing published, no git, no Lean. Marks: [ran] = exact run of mine tonight (files in
`g-4-zetalead/`), [derived] = by hand tonight, [read] = read tonight (file or page named), [guess] = marked guess.
Counts: (G) = a gate is any a*u + b*v, scalings free; (O) = OpenAI's count, every addition, subtraction and multiplication by a
constant charged. Words: N = 2^k points, addressed by the subsets S of a k-element set (or by k bits); |S| = size of S.
**Transform** H: the Walsh-Hadamard transform, y_S = sum over T of (-1)^|S and T| x_T. **Subset sums** Z (the "zeta transform"):
y_S = sum of x_T over the T inside S. **Moebius transform**: the inverse of Z. D(a,b): multiply the entry at S by
a^(k-|S|) b^|S| (a diagonal matrix). T(N): cost of an exact program for H. z = 1 - 8.7625e-4.
**Yates's method** (1937): one pass per element of the set, k 2^(k-1) additions for Z; the textbook. **Sierpinski matrix**: the
0/1 matrix of Z read over the field with two elements, where additions are XOR. **Morgenstern's bound** (1973): a linear program
whose gates a*u + b*v have |a|, |b| <= 1 needs at least log2 |determinant| gates. **Call**: one use of a program for H.

## 0. In ten lines

1. **The transfer is a theorem for every k, with one line of proof** [derived + ran k = 1..10]:
   Z = D(1,3/5) H D(1,-1/2) H D(1,1/3) H D(1,5/4). Cost: **3 T(N) + 4(N-1)** in (O), **3 T(N)** in (G). Same for Moebius and for
   superset sums. So subset sums cost O(N (log N)^z) over the rationals, z = 1 - 8.7625e-4.
2. **Three calls is the least, in a stronger sense than a-0 had** [derived + ran]: no two-call form exists even with arbitrary
   relabellings of the N addresses and arbitrary diagonals between and around the calls. Two calls reach Z only in a limit.
3. **The word needs 2, 3 and 5 invertible, and no word of any length avoids that** [ran]: over the fields with 3 and with 5
   elements Z is not in the group generated by H and diagonals at all; with 7, 11 and 13 elements that group is everything.
4. **The constant does not carry.** With every constant set to 1 the three-call program falls below Yates's k 2^(k-1) additions only
   when log2 N exceeds about 1.1e888 [ran]. At ordinary sizes it is 6 to 10 times worse. Small sizes show no gain at all [read].
5. **Literature** [read, dated, section 4]: I found nobody who states an exact subset-sum transform below N log N over the
   rationals, and nobody who writes the three-call word. The opposite direction (the transform from two subset-sum matrices and one
   diagonal) is in print: Alman and Li, September 2025.
6. **What it means for that field**: additions alone need exactly k 2^(k-1) (Kennes; Boyar and Find). So over the rationals
   subtraction and division provably help for this matrix, by the factor (log N)^(8.7625e-4). Subset convolution drops from
   N (log N)^2 to N (log N)^(2 - 8.7625e-4) operations [derived + ran small].
7. **What does not carry**: min-plus and other semirings (no subtraction), the field with two elements (H is singular there; the
   question there is the open one of Boyar and Find, whether the Sierpinski matrix needs more than linearly many XOR gates, which
   Aaronson suggested it does), bit complexity, any practical size.
8. **Turned around, for the user's question** [derived]: T(N) <= 2 cost(Z) + N-1, and with coefficients bounded by 1 the integer
   k 2^(k-1) appears three times: Yates's count, Morgenstern's lower bound for H, and the price of the ONE diagonal D(1,-2) in
   H = Z D Z-transpose. Morgenstern's N log N is the price of that diagonal. What is left is two subset-sum transforms, and their own
   bounded-coefficient floor is only N sqrt(log N) (0.277 N sqrt(k) - N, by minors) [derived + ran].
9. A 16-point subset-sum circuit with 27 gates or fewer in (G) would beat the best known 16-point transform (56). Two machine
   searches of about 16 minutes found nothing below Yates's 32; the tool did not reach the known 12 at 8 points in its test, so
   this says nothing.
10. No new count for the transform, no new growth law for it. One better question, section 6.

## 1. The identities, proved for every k (task a)

**The 2 x 2 fact** [ran, exact; by hand in the note below]. With H = [[1,1],[1,-1]], Z = [[1,0],[1,1]]:

    Z      = D(1,3/5)  H D(1,-1/2) H D(1,1/3) H D(1,5/4)         (mine: every diagonal starts with 1, no global factor)
    Z      = D(1/4,1/5) H D(3/2,-1/2) H D(2,1) H D(1,5/3)         (a-0's word)
    Z^-1   = D(1,-3/5) H D(1,-1/2) H D(1,1/3) H D(1,-5/4)         (Moebius: signs folded into the two outer diagonals)
    Z^T    = D(1,5/4)  H D(1,1/3) H D(1,-1/2) H D(1,3/5)          (superset sums: the word read backwards)
    H      = Z D(1,-2) Z^T ,    Z^T = X Z X  (X = complement every bit) ,    Z^-1 = D(1,-1) Z D(1,-1)

**All k** [derived]. The rule (AB) tensor (AB) = (A tensor A)(B tensor B), applied k - 1 times, turns each line into the same line
for the k-th tensor powers; the k-th power of diag(1,r) multiplies the entry at S by r^|S|. Nothing else is used.
The runs at k = 2..6 as full N x N matrix identities add nothing to the 2 x 2 check; I did them because the brief asks [ran].

**As a program** [ran, k = 1..10, exact rationals, against Yates on random inputs]:
multiply x_S by (5/4)^|S|; transform; multiply by (1/3)^|S|; transform; multiply by (-1/2)^|S|; transform; multiply by (3/5)^|S|.

**Exact overhead.**
- (O): each diagonal D(1,r) with r not 1 or -1 has exactly one entry equal to 1 (the empty set), so the four diagonals cost
  **4(N-1)** multiplications. Total with the textbook transform: 3kN + 4(N-1), measured exactly so at k = 1..10 [ran]. a-0's word
  costs 4N - 2 (two of its diagonals have no entry 1). Preparing the constants: 4k multiplications of numbers and a table of |S|,
  O(N) index work in OpenAI's machine [derived, not written as a program for that machine].
- (G): scalings are free, so the overhead is **zero**: cost_G(Z) <= 3 T_G(N).
- The way back: T(N) <= 2 cost(Z) + (N-1) in (O), T(N) <= 2 cost(Z) in (G). Moebius: cost(Z) + N in (O) (N/2 sign changes on each
  side), equal in (G).
- No better word of this kind: in the family below every ratio r differs from 1 and -1, also over the Gaussian rationals (the
  ratio i would need p^2 + q^2 = 0 or a square root of 2 or of i) [derived].

**The family** [derived, ran for 2,128 rational pairs]. For p, q with 2pq(p^2+q^2)(q^2-p^2) invertible:

    Z = D(1/(2pq), 1/(p^2+q^2)) H D((p+q)/2, (p-q)/2) H D(q, p) H D(1, (p^2+q^2)/(q^2-p^2)).

Where it comes from: H D(a,b) H = [[p,q],[q,p]] with p = a+b, q = a-b; then [[p,q],[q,p]] D(c,d) H has top-right entry pc - qd,
which vanishes for (c,d) = (q,p); the three remaining entries are 2pq, p^2+q^2, q^2-p^2. Normalising all four diagonals to D(1,r)
leaves the global factor ((p+q)/(4p))^k, which is 1 exactly when q = 3p. That is the word in line 1 of this section.

**Three is the least, strongly** [derived; ran for all permutations at 2, 4, 8 points, `more.py`].
Claim: Z is not M1 H M2 H M3 for any M1, M2, M3 that are relabellings of the N addresses times diagonals (over the rationals, or
any field of characteristic 0).
Proof. Write M2 = (relabelling pi) x (diagonal d). Z has a row with a single non-zero entry (the empty set). That row of
H M2 H is the transform of the vector d(y) (-1)^(x0 . pi(y)); a transform with one non-zero entry is the transform of a constant
times a character, so d(y) = c (+-1). Then H M2 H is c times the table W[x][w] = sum over y of (-1)^(x.pi(y) + w.y), shifted. Each
row of W has whole-number entries whose squares add to N^2 = 4^k (Parseval). Z has rows with exactly two non-zero entries (the
one-element sets), so W would need a^2 + b^2 = 4^k with a, b both non-zero: impossible (both must be even; divide by 4; repeat).
[ran: at 8 points the rows of all 40,320 tables have 1 or 4 non-zeros, never 2.]
- This is more than a-0's "H D H has no zero or two": the diagonals need not be tensor powers and addresses may be permuted.
- **Two calls in the limit** [ran]: D(1/(2m),1/2) H D(m+1,m-1) H D(1,1/m) = [[1,1/m^2],[1,1]] exactly, which tends to Z. So the
  "border" count is 2 and the exact count is 3. (The general way of making a limit exact costs k+1 times as much; here it costs
  one call.)

**Which rings** [ran, `fields.py`]. The family needs 2, a, b, a^2 - b^2, a^2 + b^2 invertible. Over the integers
2pq(p^2+q^2)(q^2-p^2) is never a power of 2, so an odd prime is always inverted; the smallest choice inverts 2, 3 and 5.
- Field with 3 elements: no usable ratio. Field with 5: none either (there 2^2 = -1). Every field of odd characteristic
  with more than 5 elements has one (at most five ratios are excluded: 0, 1, -1 and the two square roots of -1) [derived].
- Stronger: the group generated by H and ALL invertible diagonals inside the invertible 2 x 2 matrices has order 16 of 48 over
  the field with 3 elements and 96 of 480 with 5, and Z is not in it; with 7, 11, 13 elements it is everything.
  So over the dyadic rationals (only 2 inverted), or with 3 or 5 not inverted, **no word of any length** in H and tensor
  diagonals gives Z. The set {2, 3, 5} is forced for subrings of the rationals, not an accident of the word.

**The class constants** [read: a-0 3.2, not rerun; one addition of mine]. For every invertible rational 2 x 2 kernel M that is
not a relabelling with scalings: T <= 4 cost(M) + O(N), cost(M) <= 6 T + O(N). Two calls reach exactly the kernels whose invariant
tau = m11 m22/(m12 m21) is a rational square. Addition [ran, `calls3.py`; derived]: three calls reach tau exactly when the curve
y^2 = (1+tau)^2 (1-s^2)^2 + 4 (1-tau)^2 s^2 has a rational point with s not 0, 1, -1. tau = 6 has one
(H D(-1/2,-3/2) H D(5/2,1) H = [[-4,-6],[1/2,9/2]]); tau = 2, 3, 5, 7, 10, -2, -3, -5, 1/2, 2/3, -1/2 have none with s of height
up to 150. So the number of calls a kernel needs is an arithmetic matter (rational points on a curve of genus one). It has no
bearing on the order. Kept as an anomaly.

## 3. The exponent carries, the constant does not

Yates: k 2^(k-1) additions = (k/2) per point. Three-call program: 3 T(N) + 4(N-1).
- With the textbook transform (T = kN) the program is worse by the factor 6 + 8/k: 10 at k = 2, 8.3 at k = 3, 6.8 at k = 10 [ran].
- With T(N) = N (k+1)^z and **every constant set to 1**, it falls below Yates when (k+1)^(1-z) exceeds 6 and a little:
  **k = log2 N above about 1.1e888** (6^(1/(1-z)), 1/(1-z) = 1141.23; solved in logarithms: log10 k = 888.05) [ran]. N then has
  about 3.4e887 decimal digits. At k = 10^9 the ratio to Yates is still 5.89.
- With the factor 2^a that the release's README reports for the program as written (a = 6049 [read: README, its mark "(c)"]):
  log2 k above (a + log2 6)/(1-z) = 6.91 million, that is **log2 log2 N above 6.9 million** [ran: arithmetic].
  Aside for the release track: README line 742 says "about 8e6" for a/(1-z); with a = 6049 and 1 - z = 8.7625e-4 I get 6.90e6
  (8.09e6 is what 7.4745e-4 gives). I may be misreading which figures pair up; worth one look.
- Small sizes: no gain at all. 12 gates at 8 points is the exact minimum in four finite fields, 4 gates at 4 points [read: d-0];
  circuits made only of "tight" wires need exactly Yates's count at every size [read: a-2 section 2].
- So there is a smallest size k* at which subtraction beats Yates for subset sums: k* is at least 4 and at most about 1e888.

## 5. What transfers and what does not (task c)

**Transfers** [derived].
- Subset sums, superset sums, Moebius inversion, on values in any vector space over the rationals (rationals, reals, complex
  numbers, polynomials or matrices over them): 3 T(N) + 4(N-1) operations, where an operation is an addition, a subtraction or a
  multiplication by a rational constant. Whole-number inputs give whole-number outputs; the values in between are fractions.
- The published program wants the number i as an input. For rational data, carry real and imaginary parts separately: a constant
  factor [derived, routine, not written out].
- Every Kronecker power of a fixed invertible rational 2 x 2 matrix (the matrix applied to each of the k bits separately):
  at most six calls [read: a-0].
- **Multilinear polynomials**: a polynomial of degree at most one in each of k variables, given by its 2^k coefficients, has as
  its values on the grid {0,1}^k the subset sums of its coefficients. So evaluation on that grid and interpolation from it cost
  3 T(N) + 4(N-1); on any grid {a,b}^k with a, b rational and different, at most six calls [read: a-0 for the six]. H is the grid
  {1,-1}^k, and H = Z D Z-transpose reads: substitute x = 1 - 2y in every variable (a triangular change of coefficients), then
  evaluate on {0,1}^k. Three two-point algebras sit behind the three problems: x^2 = 1 (the transform), x^2 = x (subset sums,
  union product), x^2 = 0 (subset convolution); the first two are the same algebra after that substitution, the third is not
  semisimple, which is why it needs the split by set size. A parallel worth keeping for both communities.
- The cube's neighbour sum A (sum over the k sets that differ in one element): two calls, N A = H diag(k - 2|S|) H. The "add one
  element" operator U: U = (A + WA - AW)/2 with W = diag(|S|), so four calls [ran k = 1..6]. Both are below N log N for the same
  reason. (Z is exp(U); W Z - Z W = U Z.)
- **Subset convolution**, (f*g)(S) = sum over T inside S of f(T) g(S minus T). The method of 2007 splits f and g by set size,
  takes k+1 subset-sum transforms of each, multiplies two polynomials of degree k at every set, and inverts k+1 slices. The
  3(k+1) transforms are the bottleneck once the polynomial products are done by any fast method (Karatsuba is enough). So over
  the rationals: **O(N (log N)^(2 - 8.7625e-4)) operations against O(N (log N)^2)** [derived; pipeline ran exactly, k = 1..7,
  with schoolbook products]. I did not find this stated. Same crossover as in section 3.

**Does not transfer.**
- **Semirings without subtraction** (min-plus, max-plus, Boolean OR): the transform H needs -1. Kennes's bound says additions
  alone need exactly Yates's count, so there the textbook is optimal and nothing can carry. The 2007 route for min-sum embeds small
  whole-number weights into polynomials; the transfer then holds in operations on polynomials, which says nothing useful about time.
- **Arbitrary rings**, as in the 2007 theorem: the word divides by 2, 3 and 5, and section 1 shows that no word in H and tensor
  diagonals avoids that. Rings where 30 is not invertible (the integers modulo m with m not coprime to 30) are out for words of
  this kind; whether another kind of program serves them I do not know.
- **Fields of odd characteristic p**: the word exists once the field has more than 5 elements, but I did not check which primes
  the published transform program divides by, so I claim nothing there.
- **The field with two elements.** H is singular (H^2 = 2 = 0). All four invertible 2 x 2 kernels that are not relabellings ARE
  the Sierpinski matrix up to relabelling, so there the whole class is one matrix, and "is it linear size" is the open question
  of Boyar and Find, with Aaronson's suggestion that it is not. Link [derived]: a subset-sum circuit over the rationals whose
  constants all have odd denominators reduces modulo 2 to a circuit of the same size for the Sierpinski matrix. So if that matrix
  needs order N log N gates over two elements, **every** rational program for subset sums below N log N must divide by 2
  somewhere. The three-call program does: its diagonals contain 5/4 and -1/2.
- **Bit complexity, word-RAM time, numerical stability, practical sizes**: nothing.

## 6. Turned around: what subset sums say about the transform

This is the part that bears on the user's question. All [derived] unless marked.

**6.1 Z is the half-price half.** T(N) <= 2 cost(Z) in (G), 2 cost(Z) + N - 1 in (O); the textbook counts satisfy this with
equality in (G) terms (kN = 2 * kN/2). Known (G) values: Z needs 4 and 12 at 4 and 8 points, H needs 7 and at most 22: the ratio
Z/H is 0.571, 0.545, above 1/2 only because H has small gains and Z has none.
- Any saving exponent found for Z passes to H with no loss. The published one came the other way and lost a factor 6.
- Lower bounds pass too: cost(Z) >= T(N)/2 in (G) and (T(N) - N + 1)/2 in (O), always; and T(N) >= (cost(Z) - 4N)/3.
  So a proof of cost(Z) <= c T(N) + O(N) with any c < 1/2 would force T(N) = O(N). No black-box word can do that (a word is at
  least one call); it would have to open the transform program.
- **Target at 16 points**: cost_G(Z) <= 27 would give T_G(16) <= 54, below the known 56. Yates is 32. So "subset sums at 16
  points in 27 gates" is a record for the transform in disguise, and 28 to 31 would be the first size where subtraction helps
  subset sums. d-0 left 16 points unsearched; see section 7.

**6.2 Morgenstern's N log N is the price of one diagonal.** Take gates a*u + b*v with |a|, |b| <= 1 (bounded coefficients).
Morgenstern (1973): no minor of the matrix can exceed 2^(number of gates). For H the determinant gives (N/2) log2 N = k 2^(k-1).
In H = Z D(1,-2) Z-transpose the diagonal has entries (-2)^|S|; with bounded coefficients each costs |S| doublings, and the sum
of |S| over all S is k 2^(k-1) [ran k = 1..10]. One integer, three roles:

    k 2^(k-1)  =  Yates's additions for Z  =  log2 |det H_N| (Morgenstern's bound)  =  doublings in D(1,-2) to the k-th power.

So with bounded coefficients: k 2^(k-1) <= T_bc(N) <= 2 Z_bc(N) + k 2^(k-1). **The whole of the proved lower bound is paid by the
middle diagonal.** The determinant says nothing about the two shears (det Z = 1).
The same must hold for every bound that is a function of the determinant alone, because D(1,-2) to the k-th power has the same
determinant as H_N. Pudlak's bound for layered circuits with bounded coefficients (2000; about 1.88 N log2 N wires for H, met by
the textbook) is of that kind [read: a search summary of its form, through Alman 2021; the paper not opened]. So neither bound
can be turned into a lower bound for Z through H = Z D Z-transpose: I tried that reduction on paper and it returns exactly zero.

**6.3 The shears' own floor is N sqrt(log N), not N log N** [derived + ran, `fields.py` part D]. Z's singular values are
phi^(k-2j), C(k,j) times each (phi = golden ratio; Z_1-transpose Z_1 = [[2,1],[1,1]]). The sum of squared r x r minors is at least
the product of the r largest squared singular values (Cauchy-Binet), there are at most 2^N choices of rows and as many of columns,
so some minor is at least 2^(0.694 N E|k-2J|/2 - N), and by Morgenstern's count

    Z_bc(N) >= N (0.277 sqrt(k) - 1)        (k = 64: 1.21 N;  k = 1024: 7.86 N;  Yates: k/2 = 32 N and 512 N).

Superlinear, but only at the square-root scale. It is the same square root as the Pascal blocks in i-cross Hybrid 2 and d-0
section 4, and for the same reason: all of Z's volume sits in those blocks, whose plain cost is of the order of sqrt(k) per
point [read: d-0 section 4; a-0 section 5 for the transform's blocks].
[The minor-doubling lemma is from memory and re-proved in two lines. The general singular-value form of Morgenstern's bound is
Buergisser and Lotz, arXiv:cs/0301016 (2003) [read: search summary of the abstract]; one search did not find it applied to the
subset-sum, disjointness or Sierpinski matrix. So: a known method, an application I did not find written.]

**6.4 The better question.** *With coefficients bounded by 1, do subset sums cost o(N log N)?*
- Caution first: with Yates's circuit for Z the route "Z, diagonal, Z-transpose" costs 1.5 kN bounded gates, WORSE than the
  textbook's kN. The butterfly (u+v, u-v) does a shear, a doubling and a shear in two gates instead of three. So the route beats
  the bounded textbook only if Z_bc falls below kN/4, half of Yates's count. Nothing at small size suggests that.
- If yes, then T_bc(N) = (1/2 + o(1)) N log2 N: Morgenstern's bound would be asymptotically exact for the Walsh-Hadamard
  transform, against the textbook's N log2 N in this count. (The 7-gate brick does not help here: it uses the constant 2, so with
  bounded coefficients it computes the transform divided by 2, and the doublings cost more than it saves [read: `j-machine/slp.py`,
  circ_J4].) I did not check whether the factor 2 between Morgenstern's bound and the textbook is closed in the literature.
  It is a question about a matrix of determinant 1, where no determinant argument can interfere.
- If the answer is N (log N)^a with a < 1, the user's model inherits exponent a at once (bounded is a special case of unbounded),
  and the only proved obstruction, 6.3, allows a down to 1/2: a saving of 0.5, five times the standing bar of 1e-1.
- The matching upper route is the one i-cross and a-0 already named: Z = B^-1 (Pascal blocks) B with B the change to the
  symmetric-chain basis. The blocks cost O(N sqrt(log N)) with coefficients 1 (a Pascal matrix is a product of 0/1 bidiagonals).
  Over the reals B can be taken orthogonal (the Schur basis; the blocks are then Pascal matrices rescaled by square roots), so
  no volume count sees it [derived, not run]. **In the bounded world "Z in N sqrt(log N)" would follow from "a change to the
  Schur basis and back in N sqrt(log N)", and upper and lower bound would then meet.** That is a sharper target than "linear",
  and a proof that it fails would have to be a new kind of lower bound, for a matrix that is orthogonal up to scalings.
- Honest limit: this does not make the basis change cheaper. It says where every proved bound lives (diagonal: N log N; blocks:
  N sqrt(log N); basis change: nothing), so where a proof of "not linear" would have to put new content.

**6.5 Why H and Z are the two special members** [derived]. Kernels up to relabellings and scalings on both sides are
classified by tau up to tau <-> 1/tau. H is the fixed point tau = -1 (extra symmetry: it is its own inverse up to a factor). Z is
the boundary point (tau = 0 or infinity). Cost falls at the boundary: 4 against 7 at 4 points in (G), for every generic kernel
[read: i-cousins]. Z is a limit of kernels reachable by two calls but is not one of them. If one wants the member of the class
with the least structure in the way, it is Z: determinant 1, 0/1 entries, a one-parameter group (Z^t = D(1,t) Z D(1,1/t), so
every power, root and inverse of Z costs cost(Z) + 2N), and no known small gain to explain away.

4. **Arithmetic of the class**: for which rational tau does the three-call curve have a point (tau = 6 does)? Pure curiosity,

5. **Terwilliger words**: which operators that commute with permutations of the bits are bounded words in H and diagonals that

   k 2^(k-1) known at ANY finite size? If k* were small, it would be news there and a new brick here.
