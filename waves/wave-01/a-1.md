# a-1: criticism of "Circuit" (i-cross Hybrid 3: the squares of the 4-point transform, 62 gates at 16 points)

> Editor's note (11 October 2026): this report was written before the paper of Coppersmith, Feig and Linzer (1994) had been read. The 4-point circuit in 7 operations and the rate 7/8 are due to that paper, not to the patent filed in 2002 or to Alman and Rao (2023). See [Earlier papers](../README.md#earlier-papers).

Private research note, 2026-10-10. Marks: [read] [ran] [derived] [guess]. Gate count used throughout is the one of the object
under review (count G of PICKS.md): a gate is a*u + b*v with any rational a, b; scalings are free; this is NOT OpenAI's cost model.

## 0. Verdict in five lines

- Every number in the claim is correct. I replayed it with my own code, and pushed the gate-by-gate replay from 16 points to 64, 256
  and 1,024 points. [ran]
- It is known mathematics, and the tie with the textbook is a theorem, not an anomaly: the route IS the textbook circuit, written in
  the address coordinates (x, x+y), with the butterfly order chosen slice by slice. Its half-way wires are the textbook's half-way
  wires, entry for entry. [ran + derived]
- It can never beat the plain product T(q^2) <= 2q T(q), whatever the prices: route minus product = 2(q-1)(2T(q/2) + q - T(q)) >= 0.
  At q = 4 that is 62 - 56 = 6. [derived, checked on 20,000 random price lists]
- For "linear or not": nothing. Growth law n log2 n, constant between 7/8 and 1, approaching 7/8 from above. Saving exponent 0.
- What survives as a fact: the 6 x 6 antisymmetric square needs exactly 12 gates over GF(3) and over GF(5) (two independent programs
  agree), and has no 10-gate circuit over GF(7). Over the rationals the lower bound is still not a proof.

## 1. The claim, stated precisely

Write the 16 inputs as a 4 x 4 array F; the transform is G = H F H with H the 4-point Sylvester matrix. Swapping the two base-4 digits
is the transpose, and it commutes with the transform, so the transform splits into its action on symmetric arrays (dimension 10) and
on antisymmetric arrays (dimension 6).

- (a) The antisymmetric part, in the basis of pairs x < y, is the 6 x 6 matrix A2 of 2 x 2 minors of H (the second compound matrix).
  Entries are 0, +2, -2, four non-zeros per row, A2^2 = 16 I, and the zero pattern is the octahedron's (three row slices, three column
  slices, each row slice misses one column slice).
- (b) A2 has a circuit with 12 gates and none with 10 or 11 (the lower bound claimed only over GF(3) and GF(5)).
- (c) The symmetric part S2 (10 x 10, entries are the matching "permanents" of H) has a circuit with 26 gates; lower bound 18 asserted.
- (d) Hence 16 points in 12 (split) + 26 + 12 + 12 (merge) = 62 gates.
- (e) For every q = 2^j: T(q^2) <= 2(q^2 - q) + 2 T(q) + 4(q - 1) T(q/2), and the right side equals q^2 log2(q^2) when T is the textbook count.

Two things in the claim that I could not confirm:
- "lower bound 18" for the symmetric square. The argument I can write down gives 12 (the first output with all ten entries non-zero
  needs nine gates below it, and three more such outputs come later). I found no proof of 18. It does not change anything below. [derived]

## 3. Is it known? Yes, in three layers

1. **The route is the textbook circuit in other coordinates.** [derived, then ran] Put m = x + y. Then u.x + v.y = (u+v).x + v.m, so in
   the coordinates (x, m) -> (w = u+v, v) the transform is again the plain transform: first along x inside every slice m, then along m.
   The swap of the two digits is x -> x + m inside slice m. So "split into symmetric and antisymmetric" is the first butterfly layer of
   the slice's own transform, taken along the direction m; "transform each half" is the rest of it; "one number per block" is the
   textbook's half-way array c[w][m] = sum over x of (-1)^(w.x) F[x][x+m]; and the output side is the mirror image. My replay asserts
   this equality for every (w, m) at every size up to 1,024 points: it holds exactly. This is the classical fact that the
   Walsh-Hadamard transform commutes with invertible linear changes of the address (H P_A = P_(A^-T) H), plus the freedom to pick a
   different basis of butterfly directions on each slice. It is the hatch "address relabelling that mixes columns" from the floor note,
   in its tamest form: the relabelling is linear, and a linear relabelling changes no count.
2. **The squares are Krawtchouk matrices.** [derived, then ran] H4 = H2 x H2, and for two 2-dimensional spaces
   antisym(V x W) = sym(V) x antisym(W) + antisym(V) x sym(W). The symmetric square of [[1,1],[1,-1]] is the 3 x 3 Krawtchouk matrix
   K = [[1,2,1],[1,0,-1],[1,-2,1]], its antisymmetric square is the number -2. So A2 = -2 (K + K) in the basis
   {01, 03-12, 23 | 02, 03+12, 13}, one butterfly away from the pair basis (extra.out prints the block-diagonal matrix). Likewise
   S2 = (K x K) + (4). In general the 4^j-point transform is, after the basis change (identity on 00, 11; butterfly on 01, 10) on every
   base-4 digit, the direct sum over subsets S of the digits of (-2)^|S| K^(x (j - |S|)). Symmetric powers of the 2 x 2 Hadamard matrix as
   Krawtchouk matrices: Feinsilver and Kocik, "Krawtchouk polynomials and Krawtchouk matrices" (2005; arXiv:quant-ph/0702073) and
   "Krawtchouk matrices from classical and quantum random walks" (2001; arXiv:quant-ph/0702173). [read: abstracts and search summary only]
3. **The only saving in the 62 is the known one.** The 2 gates below 64 are two uses of the 7-gate block on a full 4-point slice. That
   block is the low-rank-plus-sparse form of the transform: Alman and Rao, "Faster Walsh-Hadamard and Discrete Fourier Transforms From
   Matrix Non-Rigidity", STOC 2023, arXiv:2211.06459, (23/24) N log N + O(N) in their operation count. [read: abstract; their count is
   not count G, so 7/8 and 23/24 are not comparable figures]

I found no paper that states the 12-gate minimum for the second compound matrix of H4. It is a small search result, not a theorem, and
it says the same thing as every other exact minimum tonight.

What the known theory predicts for "linear or not": a symmetry that commutes with the transform splits it into blocks, the blocks are
again transforms (here: of the slices, or Krawtchouk tensor powers), and the split itself costs what one butterfly layer costs. Counted
honestly the pieces add up to the same butterflies. No lower bound follows either: this is one family of circuits, not all circuits.

## 4. Does it scale? Growth law

Write s(n) = n log2 n - T(n) for the gates saved at n points. Because (e) is an identity at textbook prices,

    saved by the route at q^2 points   = 2 s(q) + 4 (q-1) s(q/2)        [derived]
    saved by the plain product         = 2 q s(q)
    route - product                    = 2 (q-1) (2 T(q/2) + q - T(q))  >= 0, with equality only when T(q) is no better than one layer on top of T(q/2).

So the route inherits savings made at sizes q and q/2 and never creates one. With the best prices known tonight (7-gate blocks):

| points | textbook | route | 7-gate blocks | route / textbook |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2^4 | 64 | 62 | 56 | 0.969 |
| 2^6 | 384 | 352 | 336 | 0.917 |
| 2^8 | 2,048 | 1,912 | 1,792 | 0.934 |
| 2^10 | 10,240 | 9,216 | 8,960 | 0.900 |
| 2^16 | 1,048,576 | 950,144 | 917,504 | 0.906 |
| 2^32 | | | | 0.891 |
| 2^64 | | | | 0.883 |
| 2^128 | | | | 0.879 |

(first four rows replayed gate by gate [ran]; the rest by formula.) The ratio tends to 7/8 from above and never reaches it.

## 5. In the user's terms

- Circuit size for n points: c n log2 n with 7/8 < c < 1. Order unchanged. Saving exponent: 0 (nothing to add to 8.7625e-4, and this
  count is not OpenAI's in any case). Linear size: no contribution.
- It does not fail; it is a tie by construction. The i-cross note reads the many exact ties as "what a conservation law looks like".
  For this one the explanation is plainer: it is the same circuit. I would not count Hybrid 3 as independent evidence for W3.

## 6. The nearest statement that is not a tie by construction

Only one place is left where this object could do something: a square taken as a whole might be cheaper than its slice circuit.
- At the smallest case the answer is no: the antisymmetric square at q = 4 costs 12 = its slice circuit (section 2).
- The symmetric square at q = 4: 26 known. To beat the 56 one needs 19 or fewer. Open; a search on 10 wires at that depth is beyond
  tonight's exhaustive programs.
- The Krawtchouk form turns this into a cleaner question. [derived] With kappa(3^t) the cost of the t-fold tensor power of K
  (4 gates for 3 points; textbook price (4/3) t 3^t):

      T(4^j) <= 2 * sum over s of C(j,s) 2^(j-s) T(2^s)  +  sum over t of C(j,t) kappa(3^t).

  At textbook prices both sums equal j 4^j, half the textbook each. The first sum is transforms of about the fourth root of n points.
  So per point, with L = log2 n: cost(L) <= 2 cost(about L/4) + (Krawtchouk cost per point at t about 3L/8). If Krawtchouk tensor
  powers had a saving exponent, the transform would inherit it, down to a floor of sqrt(log n) per point. [derived; concentration of
  the binomial sums not written out] There is no evidence that K-powers are easier than the transform itself (K contains a 2 x 2
  Hadamard block, so its powers contain the transform on 2^t of the 3^t points), and at 4 points this form costs 8, worse than the
  7-gate block. A conditional of the same kind as i-cross's Hybrid 2, not a mechanism. [guess: not worth a run before the mirror-basis
  experiment of i-cross section 5]
