Words. N = 2^k points. H: the ±1 transform; H' its half-size copy. A *letter* for a flag (a chain of subgroups, equivalently a balanced tree of blocks) is any operator diagonal in that tree's many-scale (Haar) basis: N free numbers, O(N) gates. Diagonal matrices D_i between letters are free. Target: (-1)^(x^T β y) for any invertible bit matrix β, which is the transform after a linear relabelling of one side.

## The answer in four lines

1. **Dropping commutativity does buy something at 8 points, exactly.** H_8 = D0 · L1 · D1 · L2 · D2 with both letters on the *same* tree and a middle diagonal that is not a scalar and need not be unitary, for example (1, -1, 2, -2, 3, -3, 5, -5) [ran, exact, `exact8.out`]. The commuting class has no word on one flag at 8 points.
2. **I can say exactly what that word is, and it is the textbook recursion written serially.** For *any* half-size matrix G: G ⊗ H_2 = diag · (2iG ⊗ P+ + I ⊗ P−) · (I ⊗ Z) · (2G ⊗ P+ + I ⊗ P−) · diag [ran, exact, 16 and 32 points, G the transform and G a random integer matrix]. Each factor carries one half-size call; the middle sign diagonal is what makes a product behave like a sum.
3. **The growth law is negative.** Two letters: plentiful at 8 points; at 16 points only the known "pair the bits" word, whose freedom I can count exactly; at 32 points none in 270 cases, while the same solver does find the three-letter word there [ran, floats].
4. **The wall has a name: two calls.** If two factors share their finest pairing, both must be diagonal scalings of the half-size transform, even when each factor is allowed an *arbitrary* half-size block [derived]. Section 5 states it as a lemma and says where it is still open.

## 2. The exact word at 8 points [ran, exact]

Points (x1, x2), x1 in 4 points, x2 one bit. K[x][y] = (-i)^|x+y| on 4 points is one letter (eigenvalues -2i, 2i, 2, 2 on the tree {0,3} | {1,2}) and H_4 = D K D with D = diag(i^|x|). P± are the projectors on sum and difference along x2. For any invertible diagonal A1 on 4 points:

- L1 = K ⊗ P+ + (-i·A1⁻¹) ⊗ P−, L2 = K ⊗ P+ + A1⁻¹ ⊗ P−, D1 = A1 ⊗ Z,
- D0 = D ⊗ diag(2i, 2), D2 = D ⊗ diag(1/(1+i), 1/(1−i)),
- H_8 = D0 · L1 · D1 · L2 · D2.

Checked for A1 = (1,1,1,1), (1,2,3,5) and a Gaussian-rational choice; both letters checked to be diagonal in the many-scale basis of one tree. The letters commute with each other; it is the diagonal between them that does not commute with either.

## 3. What the middle diagonal does [derived]

Pair the points along the finest direction of a letter. A letter is T ⊗ P+ + C ⊗ P−: real work (T, itself a letter of half size) on the sum sector, a mere diagonal C on the difference sector. A diagonal is S ⊗ 1 + A ⊗ X in the sum/difference basis: its odd part A moves data between the sectors.

So a word expands into a **sum over sector paths**. With D1 = Z the paths (+,+) and (−,−) vanish (P+ Z P+ = 0 = P− Z P−) and the word is T ⊗ |+><−| + T' ⊗ |−><+|. That is the answer to "what does a non-commuting letter do that a butterfly cannot": *the diagonal turns a product of letters into a sum of one-call terms, each on half the space.* It raises no quantity by more than a constant per gate; it re-routes.

## 4. Why 16 points gives only the pair word [derived count, matches the run]

For the pair word, every block of the first letter is its own 4-point word. Counting: 4 blocks × 1 parameter + 1 common scale, twice, plus a function of x2 and a function of y1 that can be moved between neighbouring diagonals: 18. The solver's Jacobian nullity at every 16-point solution is 18 [ran, floats]. So the non-scalar middle diagonal at 16 points is fully explained by blockwise freedom of the known word; nothing new lives there.

Incidence table of the one solved random case: V_2 = (V'_2)^⊥ with respect to β, and V_2 ∩ V'_2 = 0: two complementary 4-point block systems, each the annihilator of the other.

## 5. The wall, as lemmas

**Proved here [derived], "two calls, shared pairing".** Let n ≥ 3 and suppose
H_{2n} = D0 · (T ⊗ P+ + C ⊗ P−) · D1 · (T' ⊗ P+ + C' ⊗ P−) · D2
with T, T' *arbitrary* invertible n × n matrices and C, C', D_i invertible diagonals (and the even part of D2⁻¹ invertible). Then D1 is odd under the pairing, the ratio of D0 across each pair is the same number ε = ±i, and T and T' are both diagonal scalings of H_n.
Proof sketch: write D0⁻¹ H D2⁻¹ in the sum/difference basis; the (−,−) block of the word is the diagonal C·S1·C'; comparing gives e·G − G·f = diagonal with G a scaled H_n and e, f diagonal; all entries of G are non-zero, so e = f = one scalar ε, the block is 0, S1 = 0, then the (+,+) block forces ε² = −1 and the two mixed blocks are T·A1·C' = εG and C·A1·T' = G.
Consequence: two *letters* sharing a finest pairing exist only at 8 points (half size 4 is the only size that is one letter).

**Also derived, weaker.**
- One letter is impossible from 8 points on: it needs a half-by-half block of H of rank one, and a rank-one ±1 block a × b has ab ≤ N.
- m letters on one tree give top off-diagonal rank at most m; H needs N/4. So trees must change.
- Two letters, any depths j, j': if U = V_j + V'_j' is a proper subspace then 2^k / |U ∩ U^⊥| ≤ 2^j + 2^j'. Satisfiable; it prunes, it does not kill.
- Different finest pairings v, v' (only the finest level of each factor is used, so it holds for arbitrary half-size blocks too): for every pair {x, x+v} and every disjoint pair {y, y+v'}, a double difference gives: if β(v,v') = 0 then d0 is ±-periodic along v with sign pattern a character, a(x+v) = ε·χ_v'(x)·a(x), and likewise d2 along v'; if β(v,v') = 1 then a(x+v)/a(x) = r^(±1) and b(y+v')/b(y) = s^(±1) with s = (1+r)/(1−r), one free number. Not checked against the numerical solutions.

**The wall, as a possible lemma [guess].** *In any identity H_{2n} = D0 · F1 · D1 · F2 · D2 with F_i = (anything on the pair sums along v_i) ⊕ (a diagonal on the pair differences), n ≥ 16, each of F1, F2 contains a half-size transform up to diagonal scalings and an added diagonal.* The case v1 = v2 is proved above. The general statement would say: this family of words obeys cost(2n) ≥ 2·cost(n), the textbook recursion, and cannot give linear size with any bounded number of two-factor levels. For m ≥ 3 factors sharing a pairing, the (−,−) block is a word in the inner factors alone plus a diagonal, which gives a recursion I did not finish.

## 6. Regularities met on the way

1. [ran, exact] The serial identity holds for an arbitrary G: it is a fact about H_2 alone, H_2 = diag(1, −i) · (2i|+><−| + 2|−><+|) · diag(1+i, 1−i)⁻¹.
2. [derived by hand, four checks] At 4 points the signed Haar basis diag(1,1,1,−1)·(Haar of the tree {0,3}|{1,2}) is an *eigenbasis* of H_4/2. "4 points is one letter" is the same fact as "a many-scale basis is adapted to the mirror of the reflection".
3. [derived] The −1 eigenspace of H/√N is the graph {(u, −u − √2·Q'u)} over the half cube, Q' the half-size normalised transform: reaching the mirror from the half cube costs one call.
4. [ran, floats] The misses are quantised: the best failed two-letter fit leaves squared residual 8.000 at 8 points and 32.00, 32.06, 48.00, 51.16 at 16 points (multiples of N in the clean cases). Unexplained.
5. [ran, floats] In the solved cases the factors are badly conditioned within their families (spreads of several hundred at 32 points) while the product stays the transform: ill-conditioning is available but does not by itself create new words.

## 7. Limits

- Everything about existence at 16 and 32 points is floating point with a few random starts per case; a missed solution is possible. The 8-point word and the serial identity are exact.
- At 32 points only β = identity and 270 flag pairs were tried, out of about 10^8 inequivalent triples. General trees (not from subgroups) and arbitrary permutations between letters were not tried at any size.
- Three letters at 128 points (where the commuting class needs four) was not run.
- The lemmas are mine alone; the last two bullets of "also derived" were not tested numerically.
