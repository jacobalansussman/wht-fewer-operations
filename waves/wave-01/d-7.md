## The short version

1. The basis exists in code now, with entries 0 and ±1 only, and the transform really is tiny blocks in it. [ran, k = 2 to 8 and 10]
2. The first experiment's target was not met: at 16 points the in + middle + out route costs about 70 to 74 against 64. Built one bit at a time, the basis change costs k − 1.6·√k per entry each way, so twice the textbook overall. [ran by formula, derived by hand at 16 points; no search over the multiplicity freedom was run]
3. The idea turned into a sharper one. Adapt only to swapping the two bits of each pair. Then the basis change **is a Walsh-Hadamard transform again**, on blocks of about N^(1/4) points, and the middle is the Kronecker power of one fixed 3-point kernel, `K3 = [[1,1,1],[2,0,-2],[1,-1,1]]`. At textbook prices this ties exactly with k·N at every size. [ran]
4. That gives a recursion, t(k) = 2·t(about k/4) + (cost of the 3-point kernel power). Its solution: **any saving in the exponent for the 3-point kernel passes undiluted to the 2-point transform, down to exponent 1/2**. A linear-size 3-point kernel power would give N·2.5·√(log N). [derived; solved numerically]
5. The √(log N) that would not go away is the free solution of that recursion: 2·(1/4)^(1/2) = 1. Two for in and out, a quarter because pairs halve the bits and half the pairs are unequal. [derived]
6. So the better question is: **can the Kronecker power of K3 on 3^r numbers be computed in fewer than (4/3)·r·3^r additions?** Smallest case: K3 ⊗ K3 on 9 numbers in fewer than 24.

## 1. What was built

Read the k address bits left to right; a 1 opens a bracket, a 0 closes the most recent open one. Each address gets a set of matched pairs (j of them) and k − 2j unmatched positions, of which t hold a 1. The basis vector for that address is: on each matched pair the antisymmetric combination e10 − e01, on the unmatched positions the sum of all placements of t ones. This is the valence-bond basis; the relabelling is free (it only renames addresses).

[ran, `spin.out`] For k = 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 10 the transform maps each basis vector to (−2)^j times a Krawtchouk combination of the vectors with the same pairs and other t. Exact integer check, all passed.

| non-zeros of the basis, per entry | 1.50 | 2.00 | 3.00 | 4.25 | 6.38 | 9.25 | 13.88 | 30.56 |
| non-zeros of the middle, per entry | 2.25 | 3.00 | 2.94 | 3.75 | 3.53 | 4.38 | 4.06 | 4.54 |

Block multiplicities at k = 4: one block of size 5, three of size 3, two of size 1, as the brief said.

A guess of mine that failed: I expected the basis to be a relabelling times a triangular matrix with unit diagonal. It is not. That determinant cancels between in and out, so it does not matter for what follows.

## 2. Where the n log n sits in this form

Morgenstern's bound counts volume: log2 of the determinant, k·N/2 for the transform. In the adapted basis that volume splits into two parts. [ran, `counts.out` part A; the two parts add up to k·N/2 exactly at every k tried]

| k | 4 | 16 | 64 | 256 | 1024 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| carried by the scalings (−2)^j, per entry | 0.81 | 5.26 | 26.09 | 115.72 | 486.96 |
| carried by the small blocks, per entry | 1.19 | 2.74 | 5.91 | 12.28 | 25.04 |

The blocks carry about 0.78·√k per entry (limit √(2k/π) [derived]). Everything else is one multiplication per entry by a power of two. So in this form the quantity that forces n log n for bounded coefficients is paid by N scalings and a middle of size N·√(log N). No volume argument says anything about the basis change. That is why the idea is not dead on arrival. It also says nothing in its favour: the basis change has to be cheap for its own reasons.

## 3. The first experiment, honestly

Built one bit at a time (each step splits blocks of size n, tensored with one bit, into sizes n + 1 and n − 1; 2 operations per pair of entries, the two end entries free):

| k | 4 | 8 | 16 | 64 | 256 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| basis change, per entry, one way | 1.625 | 4.35 | 10.52 | 52.18 | 231.44 |
| dense middle, per entry | 2.375 | 3.65 | 5.48 | 11.82 | 24.56 |

[ran] The two rows add up to exactly k at every k from 2 to 256: in + dense middle = k·N. So in + middle + out = 2·k·N − (dense middle), about twice the textbook. At 16 points: 26 in.

[derived, by hand] At 16 points I looked for a cheaper basis change layer by layer. The middle layer (6 entries) splits with 3 butterflies on complementary pairs (12|34, 13|24, 14|23): the three differences are the size-3 blocks' middle coordinates, the three sums split 1 + 2 in 4 more operations. That gives 10 for the layer and 22 in all if the three size-3 blocks need not use the same basis in every layer; 25 if they must. A count of outputs suggests about 22 in, 22 out and 20 in the middle are needed, so this route does not get under 64 at 16 points. This is a sketch, not a proof, and **I did not run the search over GL(3) × GL(2)**. The 64-point case with two groups of 3 bits costs 128 each way by tensoring the 8-point basis change (8 additions per 8 entries); the target of 96 was not reached and not searched.

## 4. The pair scheme: the basis change is the transform again

Adapt only to the k/2 swaps of bit 2i−1 with bit 2i. For one pair the coordinates are x00, x01 + x10, x11 and x01 − x10. [ran, `pairs.out`] In them the 4-point transform is

    [[1, 1, 1, 0], [2, 0, -2, 0], [1, -1, 1, 0], [0, 0, 0, -2]]

that is K3 on the first three and the number −2 on the last. The factorisation was checked exactly at 4, 16 and 64 points.

Two observations make this a recursion. [derived]
- **In and out.** The swaps move an address around an orbit of 2^s addresses, s being the number of pairs with unequal bits. The basis change on one orbit is the 2^s-point Walsh-Hadamard transform. There are C(k/2, s)·2^(k/2 − s) orbits of that size.
- **Middle.** Where r pairs are in the symmetric part and the rest antisymmetric, the transform is the r-fold power of K3 on 3^r numbers, times a power of −2.

So, with T the cost of the transform and c3(r) the cost of the K3 power,

    T(k)  ≤  2 · Σ_s C(k/2, s) · 2^(k/2 − s) · T(s)   +   Σ_r C(k/2, r) · c3(r)   +   O(N).

[ran, `counts.out` part B] With textbook prices on both sides (T(s) = s·2^s, c3(r) = 4r·3^(r−1)) the right side equals k·N exactly, at k = 2, 4, 8, 16, 32: half in the basis change, half in the middle. An exact tie, at every size.

Per entry: t(k) = 2·E[t(s)] + E[τ(r)], with s about k/4 and r about 3k/8, τ the per-entry cost of the K3 power. [ran, part C, numerical solution up to k = 1024]

| price of the K3 power per entry | t(16) | t(64) | t(256) | t(1024) | growth exponent, 256 → 1024 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| textbook, 4r/3 | 16.00 | 64.00 | 256.00 | 1024.00 | 1.000 |
| (4/3)·r^0.9 | 14.50 | 52.39 | 186.09 | 654.85 | 0.908 |
| (4/3)·r^0.5 | 10.51 | 27.97 | 69.64 | 166.20 | 0.627 (log factor at exponent 1/2) |
| flat 4/3 (linear size) | 8.10 | 18.14 | 38.42 | 79.07 | 0.521 |

**Statement, as a possible lemma.** If the r-fold Kronecker power of K3 costs O(3^r · r^(1−δ)), then the 2^k-point Walsh-Hadamard transform costs O(N · (log N)^(1−δ)) for δ < 1/2, and O(N·√(log N)) up to a log log factor beyond. A saving of 1e-1 for K3 is a saving of 1e-1 for the transform.

The reverse does not follow: I see no way to get the K3 power on 3^r numbers from a Walsh-Hadamard transform of comparable size (the obvious embedding blows up by (4/3)^r). [derived] So K3 is at least as hard. It is also a different object: three letters per column, a zero entry, and nobody has run a search on it.

## 5. Answers to the three standing questions

**Is the cost of the basis change really additive over tensor factors?** [derived]
- Not for the symmetric end. The fully symmetric coordinates (one sum per weight layer) cost N additions in all. The next types cost O(N) each, because they have few coordinates.
- For the bulk, in the pair scheme, the question answers itself: the basis change is a Walsh-Hadamard transform of smaller size. It is exactly as additive as the transform. The idea does not move n log n into a new kind of object; it moves half of it into smaller copies of the same object and half into K3.
- Adapting to one single swap already costs at least N/2 operations: N/4 antisymmetric coordinates and N/4 symmetric ones each depend on two inputs.
- For the full symmetric group, any coordinate of the type with j matched pairs depends on at least 2^j inputs [recalled: minimum supports of eigenfunctions of Johnson graphs], and the bulk has j near k/2. No lower bound on circuit size follows.

**Cheapest circuit for splitting one weight layer?** [derived] At 16 points: 10 for the middle layer, 6 for layers 1 and 3. A count of outputs gives about 2 per entry as the least one can hope for; one bit at a time gives k − 1.6·√k. Between 2 and k nothing is known to me.

**Why does √(log N) appear four times?** [derived for the first three, guess for the link to the others]
- It is the number of bits left over when zeros are matched against ones: k − 2j is of order √k for almost every entry (weights proportional to n²·exp(−n²/2k), mean 1.6·√k).
- It is the saving of the bit-by-bit build (the free end entries of each block), which equals the dense middle exactly (section 3).
- It is the free solution of the pair recursion, 2·(1/4)^(1/2) = 1.
- [guess] The ceiling 1/2 of OpenAI's generic amplification and the leftover volume of the subset-sum form are the same count seen from their sides.

## 6. Second half of the idea: blocks only on the antisymmetric half

[derived, not run] In the pair scheme, split an array into its symmetric and antisymmetric parts under the swap of two columns. On data in the floor's diagonal the antisymmetric part is zero, so it carries no weight in the floor's count, and the transform is the number −2 there for that pair. The whole weight falls on the symmetric part, where the K3 block acts. So this is a true carrier for the hatch, and it does not escape the floor by itself: the symmetric part is one live array and still needs its block. What it changes is the price of that block: K3 on 3/4 of the entries, 4 additions per 3 entries. No engine unit was built.

## 7. Criticism, last

- Every count above ties with or loses to the textbook. Nothing here saves an addition at any size.
- The reduction goes one way. K3 may be harder than the transform, in which case section 4 is a restatement.
- All schemes above use coefficients of size at most 2, apart from the final scalings. Morgenstern's bound then keeps them within a factor 2 of the textbook. A real saving for K3 would need the same kind of growing coefficients that OpenAI's construction uses.
- The search of the first experiment was not run; the 16-point bound of about 64 is a hand count.
- The recursion ignores the cost of gathering orbits (a relabelling pass per level); in OpenAI's count that adds O(N) per level, which the "flat" row already absorbs. I did not check this against their cost files.
