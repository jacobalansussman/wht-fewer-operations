## 0. Verdict in six lines

- **The conjecture is true, for every N = 2^k, and the proof is ten lines** (section 2). It needs no search and no classification:
  only that a tight wire has s = 2^d, s' = N / 2^d, and that in a gate each of the three forms lies in the span of the other two.
- The count is exact: tight wires only => at least N log2 N gates, and the textbook reaches it. Every level d = 1..k must hold at
  least N wires. The same argument gives k 2^(k-1) for the subset-sum (zeta) transform, which is Yates's count: d-0's tie.
- The inequality s s' >= N and its equality case are **known** (Donoho-Stark 1989; Smith 1990; Matusiak-Ozaydin-Przebinda 2004).
  The circuit statement I did not find anywhere; its nearest relatives are Morgenstern 1973, Papadimitriou 1979, Ailon 2013/2014.
- One part of the reading is **wrong beyond 4 points**: "the winners contain a wire flat on both sides (s s' = N^2)". In the 56-gate
  16-point circuit the helpers have s s' = 4N, not N^2. The true statement is "not tight".
- A stronger statement fell out, with slack (section 6): **the sum over gates of log2(1 + 2P/N) is at least N log2 N**, P being the
  largest s s' among the gate's three wires. So a program with cN gates must contain a wire with s s' >= (N^(1+1/c) - N)/2.
- In the user's terms: no circuit and no saving. It is a necessary condition on any program below N log2 N, and it does not by
  itself forbid linear size (P may be as large as N^2).

## 1. The claim, stated exactly

N = 2^k, H the Walsh-Hadamard matrix. A **circuit** is a list of linear forms ("wires") in the inputs x: first the N inputs, then
gates w = a u + b v with u, v earlier wires and a, b non-zero numbers; the N outputs (rows of H) must each appear up to a non-zero
scalar. Scalings are free, so a wire is a form up to a scalar and a gate whose two inputs are parallel is not counted. For a wire with
coefficient vector a: s = number of non-zero entries of a; the same form written in the outputs y = Hx is (Ha/N).y, so
s' = number of non-zero entries of Ha. **Tight** means s s' = N.

Claim (W3). (i) s s' >= N for every wire, with equality exactly for a scalar times a character on a coset of a subgroup.
(ii) A circuit all of whose wires are tight has at least N log2 N gates. (iii) Every circuit with fewer gates contains a wire with
s s' > N. Given (i), (iii) is (ii) read backwards; they are one statement.

## 2. Proof of (ii), all N [derived]

*Level.* A tight wire has s s' = 2^k with s, s' whole numbers, so s = 2^d and s' = 2^(k-d); call d its level. Inputs have level 0,
outputs level k.

*Gate lemma.* In a gate w = a u + b v (u, v not parallel, a, b non-zero) each of u, v, w is a combination of the other two, so the
non-zero set of each lies in the union of the other two. H is linear, so the same holds for the three transformed vectors.
Sort the three levels a <= b <= c. Inputs side: 2^c <= 2^a + 2^b, so c <= b + 1, and c = b + 1 forces a = b.
Outputs side: 2^(k-a) <= 2^(k-b) + 2^(k-c), so a >= b - 1, and a = b - 1 forces c = b. Hence **c - a <= 1: the three levels of a
gate differ by at most one.**

*Count.* Fix d in 0..k-1. Call a gate d-crossing if its output has level above d and one of its inputs has level at most d; by the
lemma its output then has level exactly d + 1. Every wire of level above d lies in the span of the outputs of d-crossing gates
(induction along the circuit: such a wire is not an input; its gate is d-crossing, or both its inputs have level above d).
The N outputs have level k > d and are independent, so there are at least N d-crossing gates. Crossing gates for different d are
different gates (output level d + 1). Total at least kN = N log2 N. QED.

By-products of the same proof [derived]:
- every level 1..k holds at least N wires of the circuit;
- for any target made of tight forms: gates >= sum over d < k of the rank of the outputs of level above d. For the subset-sum
  transform this is the sum of |x| over all x = k 2^(k-1), Yates's count (12 at 8 points, 32 at 16: d-0's exact ties);
- nothing used the group beyond "s s' = N and N is a power of two": the same bound holds for the 2^k-point Fourier transform over
  the cyclic group with tight wires, tied by the radix-2 algorithm;
- for a group of order p^k with p odd the gate lemma allows no change of level at all: tight wires alone cannot compute the transform.

## 4. Is it known

- **(i) is a theorem.** Donoho and Stark 1989 (cyclic groups, with the equality case); K. T. Smith 1990 and Matolcsi-Szucs for
  general groups; Matusiak, Ozaydin, Przebinda, "The Donoho-Stark uncertainty principle for a finite abelian group", Acta Math.
  Univ. Comenianae 73 (2004) 155-160: minimisers are indicator functions of subgroups up to translation, modulation and scalar
  [read, abstract]. Survey with the version for general Hadamard-like matrices: Wigderson and Wigderson, arXiv:2006.11206 (2020).
- **(ii) I did not find.** Nearest known results [read, search summaries and abstracts only]:
  - Morgenstern 1973: coefficients bounded by 1 force (N/2) log2 N (determinant). Tight-only is a narrower class and gets twice that,
    exactly the textbook.
  - Papadimitriou, "Optimality of the fast Fourier transform", J. ACM 26 (1979) 95-102: the FFT is optimal inside a restricted class
    of algorithms. I could not open it (the publisher's page refused); it is the first paper to compare with before calling (ii) new.
  - Ailon, arXiv:1305.4745 (2013): n log n for 2 x 2 unitary gates, with **entropy** as the quantity that can rise only by a bounded
    amount per gate; arXiv:1403.1307 (2014): (n log n)/R for condition number R. My level is the entropy of a tight wire; the proof
    above is the support-size version of his argument, with no condition on the coefficients and a condition on the wires instead.
  - Jansen and Regan 2006 used the uncertainty principle (Tao's form) for bounded-depth bilinear circuits: same tool, other problem.
- What known theory then says about "linear or not": every one of these bounds is a statement that N log N is forced **inside a
  class** (bounded coefficients, unitary, well conditioned, now tight). None touches the unrestricted question; no superlinear bound
  is known there. (ii) adds one more class to leave, and section 6 says by how much.

## 5. Scaling

The evidence for W3 was the 4-point brick plus readings. The proof covers every N; its one lemma was machine-checked at 4, 8, 16
(every pair of wires) and at 32 and 64 (one wire per level against all 11968 and 180800). Growth law, exact: **least tight-only size = N log2 N** for all
N = 2^k (lower bound section 2, upper bound the textbook).

## 6. The same argument with slack: what a linear program must be made of [derived, checked by runs]

For any wire put sigma = log2 s. For a gate with inputs u, v (s_u <= s_v), output w, and P = the largest s s' among the three:
the outputs-side inclusion gives N/s_u <= s'_u <= s'_v + s'_w <= P/s_v + P/s_w, and the inputs-side gives s_w <= s_u + s_v; together
**s_w <= (1 + 2P/N) s_u**. For a threshold t in [0, k) the gates whose output has sigma > t and one input sigma <= t must number at
least N (same span argument). Integrating over t:

  **sum over gates of (rise of log2 s from the smaller input to the output) >= N log2 N**, and each rise <= log2(1 + 2 P_g / N).

Consequences:
- G gates => some wire has s s' >= N (N^(N/G) - 1) / 2. For G = cN: s s' >= (N^(1+1/c) - N)/2.
- For G = cN, about a fraction 1/(2c) of all gates must touch a wire with s s' >= N^(1+1/(2c)) (averaging; P <= N^2) [derived, rough].
- A fixed brick on q points, tensored up, has P <= qN, so it saves at most a factor log2(1 + 2q): bounded slack = constant factor.
  [ran] the 7-gate bricks: helpers have s s' = 4N exactly at 16, 64, 256 points (8, 48, 256 of them); ledger 88 >= 64 at 16 points.
- For N (log N)^z gates the average of log2(1 + 2P/N) over gates must be at least (log2 N)^(1-z): at the published saving this is
  about 1 and says nothing; at a saving of 1e-1 it grows like (log N)^0.1; at linear size it grows like log N.

Honest weight: this is necessary, not sufficient, and weak. A generic wire has s = s' = N, so the inequality never forbids linear
size. What it does say is that the over-spread wire is not an occasional helper in a linear program: it is the bulk of the program.

## 7. What fails, and the nearest true statements

- "Every circuit that beat the textbook contains a wire flat on both sides (s s' = N^2)" is true at 4 points [ran, all 228
  seven-gate sets over entries 0, 1, -1] and false as a general reading: at 16 points the 56-gate circuit's non-tight wires all have
  s s' = 4N = 64, not 256 [ran]. Nearest true statement: section 2 (a non-tight wire) and section 6 (how far from tight, summed).
- "A conservation law": the conserved thing is the rise of log2 s, N log2 N in total (section 6); tight circuits can raise it by
  one per gate at most, and the textbook raises it by exactly one at every gate [ran, equality at 2..256 points].
- The 7-gate search is over wires with entries 0, 1, -1 only; that 7 is the least over all coefficients I did not check.
